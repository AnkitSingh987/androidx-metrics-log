While real growth is at what are pretty much aspirational levels, nominal growth is ending up to be much lower than the 10.1% number which is what this year's budget had assumed. Nominal GDP matters because it is the base for revenue generation, as tax payers pay taxes on nominal, not real, incomes and prices. It matters for borrowings and national debt too.

A significantly lower nominal GDP growth, therefore, can upset fiscal and revenue calculations of not just the government but also private companies. Thus the below-trend nominal GDP growth has significant implications for the fiscal aggregates. The gross tax revenues are up just 4% in the first seven months of 2025-26.

For the first half (April-September), the gross tax revenue buoyancy is only 0.32 as against a budgeted buoyancy assumption of 1.1. This implies that to meet the 12.5% growth in gross tax revenues over the previous year, a growth of more than 20% would be needed in the past five months of 2025-26. If nominal growth averages 9%, then all nominal variables will have to be recalibrated downwards.

It will be hard to expect earnings, credit or tax growth to be in strong double digits if the new normal for nominal GDP is 9-9.5%. The lower growth of nominal GDP along with increased private consumption raises some questions on the low levels of inflation. Low inflation combined with low nominal GDP growth can create economic challenges.

When price growth is persistently subdued, firms may struggle to raise revenues, leading to weaker profits, reduced hiring, and lower wage growth. In addition, very low inflation can constrain monetary policy as the central bank will have less room to cut interest rates during downturns, increasing the risk of an economy becoming stuck in a low-growth, low-inflation equilibrium. The quarterly estimate does not fully account for emerging global economic headwinds.

Weak external demand, tightening financial conditions in major economies, geopolitical instability, and commodity price volatility pose significant risks to India's export performance and capital flows. If global conditions deteriorate further, India's external-facing industries may face pressure, potentially tempering future growth. Thus, it can be viewed that India's second-quarter GDP estimate of 8.2% signals a strong short-term performance alongside moderate inflation.

However, the apparent strength masks multiple structural challenges and sectoral disparities that complicate the narrative of broad-based expansion. Hence, while the quarterly data provide grounds for cautious optimism, they also highlight the need for sustained policy vigilance. M. Suresh Babu is Director, Madras Institute of Development Studies.
