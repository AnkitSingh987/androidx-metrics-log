Title: Millets are still not on our menu

Category: Editorial

The primary impediment to the widespread adoption of millets lies in the deeply entrenched, structurally biased architecture of India's agricultural policy, specifically the legacy of the Green Revolution. For decades, the entire apparatus of the state—from the declaration of Minimum Support Prices (MSPs) and assured procurement by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) to the massive subsidies allocated for chemical fertilizers, irrigation, and power—has been overwhelmingly skewed in favor of rice and wheat. This systemic bias created a highly risk-averse environment where farmers naturally gravitated towards cultivating these two staples, ensuring a predictable, albeit ecologically disastrous, income stream. Consequently, the cultivation of millets, traditionally the staple of the dryland, marginalized farmer, was relegated to the periphery. The acreage dedicated to millets plummeted as they were systematically displaced by water-intensive cash crops. Despite recent government initiatives to announce MSPs for certain millets, the lack of a robust, decentralized procurement mechanism and guaranteed market access means that cultivating millets remains an economically precarious proposition for the average farmer.

Furthermore, the integration of millets into the Public Distribution System (PDS), the primary mechanism for ensuring food security for millions of vulnerable Indians, has been glacially slow and largely superficial. While state governments have occasionally introduced millets into their local PDS networks, the sheer volume and consistency required to effect a national dietary shift are conspicuously absent. The PDS continues to be overwhelmingly dominated by heavily subsidized rice and wheat, effectively cementing these grains as the default, inexpensive staples of the masses. If the state does not aggressively procure millets and distribute them at highly subsidized rates through the PDS, integrating them into mid-day meal schemes and Anganwadi programs, they will remain an inaccessible luxury for the vast majority of the population.

Beyond the supply-side constraints, profound challenges exist on the demand and processing side. The contemporary urban and semi-urban Indian palate has been fundamentally conditioned over generations to prefer the soft texture, bland taste, and ease of preparation associated with refined wheat flour and polished rice. Millets, possessing coarser textures and stronger, often unfamiliar flavor profiles, require a significant cultural and culinary recalibration. More critically, the processing infrastructure for millets is woefully inadequate compared to the highly industrialized, efficient milling networks that exist for wheat and rice. De-husking and processing millets, particularly minor millets, is labor-intensive and technically challenging, severely restricting their shelf life and increasing processing costs. The lack of investment in decentralized, modern processing technologies ensures that millets reach the retail market either in a raw, difficult-to-cook form or as highly expensive, heavily packaged "health foods" targeted exclusively at an affluent, health-conscious urban demographic.

Moreover, the current marketing narrative surrounding millets often inadvertently undermines their mass adoption. By positioning them primarily as premium "superfoods" or trendy dietary supplements for managing diabetes or weight loss, the discourse alienates the broader demographic. This premiumization creates a perception of exclusivity, masking the historical reality that millets were the traditional, robust sustenance of the working classes. The challenge is to rebrand millets not as an elite dietary choice, but as an affordable, accessible, and culturally resonant component of the everyday Indian thali.

In conclusion, the rhetoric surrounding the millet revolution, while laudable in its intent, is currently far outpacing the reality on the ground. Transforming millets from a celebrated diplomatic talking point into a mass dietary staple requires interventions that go far beyond superficial marketing campaigns. It necessitates a radical restructuring of the agricultural political economy, demanding a decisive shift in subsidies, procurement strategies, and PDS allocations away from the rice-wheat duopoly. It requires massive investments in decentralized processing infrastructure to make millets convenient and affordable, coupled with a nuanced cultural campaign that re-integrates them into the everyday culinary fabric of the nation. Until the government addresses these formidable structural impediments and aligns its economic incentives with its nutritional rhetoric, millets will remain a niche commodity, and the profound ecological and health benefits they offer will remain tragically unrealized for the vast majority of Indians.
