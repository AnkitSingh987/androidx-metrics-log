Title: Permanence of life insurance Part One

Permanence of life insurance

Companies may come and go, or get merged, but policyholders' rights are unaffected A fixed deposit matures in five years; an equity share can be sold tomorrow. A whole life policy taken out by a father naming his newborn son may not discharge its obligation for 70 years. An immediate annuity bought at 30 pays as long as the annuitant lives  -  beyond a 100, if he lives that long.

These are not rare cases. They are the product. And they change what regulation must do. Most people assume insurance regulation exists to protect policyholders from bad companies  -  fraud, mismanagement, insolvency. That is incomplete. It exists to protect the contract from the mortality of companies.

Companies fail, merge, restructure and exit markets. The contract can do none of these things; it must survive whatever happens to the institution that wrote it. That is the organising principle behind every provision governing mergers, amalgamations, portfolio transfers, run-offs and winding-up in life insurance law, in India and abroad.

What the Indian law encodes The Insurance Act, 1938 addresses this in a cluster of provisions often cited but rarely explained. Under Section 35, no life insurance business may be transferred or amalgamated with that of another insurer except under a scheme approved by IRDAI. Section 36 goes further: before approving one, IRDAI must send notice to every life policyholder concerned and hear any who applies to be heard.

The policyholder is not a passive party to a corporate transaction but a stakeholder whose voice must be heard. Section 37A empowers IRDAI to prepare a scheme of amalgamation itself, without waiting for the companies to propose one, if it is satisfied that the public interest, policyholders' interests, or an insurer's sound management requires it; the insurer taking over must consent in writing. This is an active intervention power, not a passive supervisory one.

Whatever such a scheme does to the company, Section 37A(4A) fixes what it cannot do to the policyholder: he is to have the same interest in, and rights against, the insurer that emerges as he had in the one he started with. Shareholders whose rights come out lower are compensated. Policyholders are not, because theirs do not change.

Run-off is the quietest version of the same duty. An insurer closed to new business must go on servicing the policies it has already written, for as long as they last, with no fresh premium arriving to make it convenient. Beneath all this is a structural requirement: the life fund. Under Section 10, a life insurer must keep a separate life insurance fund, which stands as the security of the life policyholders, and cannot be applied for any other purpose.

Section 56 carries that logic into failure. In a winding-up or an insolvency, the life assets and liabilities must be valued separately from everything else, and those assets cannot be applied to any other liability except to the extent they exceed the life liabilities. Other creditors reach them only after the policyholders have been answered.

IRDAI's July 30, 2026, amendment to its registration and amalgamation regulations goes further still: where a non-insurance holding company is amalgamated into an insurer, solvency ratio after the amalgamation must stay above the 150 per cent control level. This is not an India-specific narrative. In the UK, a transfer of insurance business under Part VII of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 needs the sanction of the High Court, in place of each policyholder's consent.
