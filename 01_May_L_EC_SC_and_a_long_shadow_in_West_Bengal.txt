Title: EC SC and a long shadow in West Bengal

Category: Editorial

The Election Commission, vested with plenary powers under Article 324 of the Constitution, is tasked with the Herculean endeavor of orchestrating the logistical and administrative machinery required for conducting elections. In West Bengal, this mandate is routinely complicated by allegations of deep-seated partisan bias within the state administrative and police apparatus. The ECI’s standard operational protocol—deploying massive contingents of Central Armed Police Forces (CAPF), ordering the transfer of controversial state officials, and implementing multi-phase polling schedules—is designed to mitigate the influence of the ruling dispensation and create a level playing field. However, these interventions often provoke fierce resistance from the state government, which decries them as an unconstitutional usurpation of federal prerogatives and a deliberate attempt to demoralize the state bureaucracy. This institutional tug-of-war frequently culminates in a paralyzing administrative gridlock, severely compromising the ECI’s ability to execute its mandate effectively.

Furthermore, the ECI’s enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC) in West Bengal is frequently subjected to intense critical scrutiny. The Commission's responses to inflammatory political rhetoric, the weaponization of state welfare schemes for electoral dividends, and instances of localized violence are often perceived as either excessively draconian or inexplicably lenient, depending on the partisan affiliation of the aggrieved party. This perception of inconsistency, whether real or manufactured, fundamentally erodes the moral authority of the ECI, reducing its interventions from objective acts of umpiring to contested moves on a political chessboard. When the neutrality of the referee is continuously litigated in the court of public opinion, the legitimacy of the entire electoral exercise is inevitably compromised.

It is within this context of institutional friction and pervasive mistrust that the Supreme Court is repeatedly compelled to intervene. The apex court is frequently approached by political stakeholders seeking judicial remedies for alleged electoral malpractices, ranging from the denial of permission for political rallies to the systemic intimidation of voters and candidates. The SC’s interventions, while essential for safeguarding fundamental rights and ensuring adherence to due process, inadvertently complicate the ECI’s operational autonomy. When the highest court in the land is routinely required to adjudicate on the minutiae of electoral management—such as the deployment patterns of security forces or the scheduling of polls—it implies a profound failure of the primary electoral machinery.

Moreover, the SC’s jurisprudence on electoral matters in West Bengal often involves walking a tightrope between upholding the rule of law and avoiding judicial overreach into the domain of the ECI. While the court has consistently affirmed the plenary powers of the ECI, it has also demonstrated a willingness to intercede when it perceives a manifest failure of the Commission to ensure a free and fair electoral environment. This dynamic creates a complex, tripolar tension between the state government, the ECI, and the SC, often resulting in last-minute judicial directives that introduce profound logistical and administrative uncertainties into the electoral process.

The persistent reliance on judicial intervention to manage elections in West Bengal is symptomatic of a deeper, more systemic malaise. It highlights the failure of the political class to adhere to the normative boundaries of democratic competition and the inability of the state administrative apparatus to function independently of partisan dictates. The long shadow cast by this institutional friction obscures the fundamental agency of the voter, reducing the electoral process to a high-stakes legal and administrative battlefield.

In conclusion, the electoral dynamic in West Bengal serves as a stark reminder of the fragility of democratic institutions when confronted with entrenched political polarization. The recurring conflicts between the Election Commission, the Supreme Court, and the state government are not mere procedural anomalies; they are profound constitutional crises that threaten to hollow out the democratic edifice. Resolving this crisis requires more than just judicial pronouncements or administrative reshuffles. It demands a fundamental political consensus on the sanctity of the electoral process and a genuine commitment to institutional autonomy. Until the political stakeholders in West Bengal recognize that the integrity of the process is more important than the immediate electoral outcome, the long shadow of violence and institutional conflict will continue to eclipse the promise of a truly vibrant democracy.
