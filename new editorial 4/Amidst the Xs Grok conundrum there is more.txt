Title: Amidst the Xs Grok conundrum there is more

Amidst the X's Grok conundrum, there is more to fix

There is an urgent need to have a proactive policy to curb explicit content generated through AI The recent surge of disturbing AI-generated images generated through Grok on X (formerly Twitter) has sparked widespread concern. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology's response to the controversy is welcome, but the pervasiveness of the issue demands greater scrutiny. Furthermore, reactive measures rather than proactive willingness to prevent the creation of such material with AI, will not solve the issue either.

Woefully, the proliferation of Gen AI created explicit material poses a threat to women and minors, as any image posted online can be distorted. The question, then, is not whether enforcement exists on paper, but whether women and children can participate publicly without fear. Two factors Two drivers explain why action in the aftermath of circulation will not fully resolve the problem.

Firstly, weak guardrails are a design problem as safety controls are often too narrow, inconsistently applied, or easily bypassed. As a result, filters that catch obvious prompts may fail to detect coded phrasing, regional slang, mixed-language requests, or workflows where a user starts with a normal image and gradually pushes the system toward a sexualized result through successive edits. Consequently, jailbreaking strategies that exploit these loopholes, employing euphemisms, step-by-step prompting, and indirect instructions to circumvent restrictions and elicit outputs the system are designed to refuse, rendering the "policy" meaningless.

Secondly, dataset governance remains a pertinent concern since many open-sourced AI training datasets contain sexually explicit material, AI models can internalise those patterns and increase the likelihood of sexualised outputs. This creates a paradox, as when an AI tool is trained with content and rules are created to prevent the production of explicit images, the training itself makes the tool susceptible to such content. This makes the surface-level filters brittle, as they might block obvious keywords but may fail when users target their weaknesses.

Reactive response India's regulatory response has been more focused on responding to damage. The draft amendments on synthetically generated information move toward disclosure, requiring platforms to seek a declaration from the uploader on whether the content is AI-generated. If it is declared synthetic, the platform must ensure it is prominently labelled or carries a persistent identifier or metadata.

MEITY's advisories have emphasised the importance of intermediary due diligence and takedown compliance under the IT Rules, which is an important step, though it does not holistically serve the purpose. The AI-enabled sexual synthetic content can spread through platforms like WhatsApp and Telegram without any checks, as there is no system to monitor the synthetically generated content on such platforms. This policy and legislative stance also shape how the law is presently deployed to tackle this, it reacts only after circulation.

The plausible remedy available under the IT Act, Section 67 which targets publishing or transmitting obscene material in electronic form, can apply the moment a morphed sexual image is circulated. They do not solve the replication problem because even a quick takedown on one platform does not stop screenshots and forwards through closed groups, where the image continues to travel, and reporting of such circulation is seldom. Safety by design The default mechanism must be Safety by Design across the lifecycle of these systems, including audited training data practices, structured red teaming that tests whether models can be exploited to cause harm, and provenance measures that make outputs traceable rather than easily launderable.

Regulation must also shift from post hoc punishment to capability control, meaning independent pre-deployment audits for high-reach systems, mandatory input and output abuse detection with accessible user reporting, and a high-risk classification for nudging functions that should be delisted, demonetized, and blocked rather than merely managed after the creation. By Harshita Gupta & Utkarsh Yadav Gupta is a final year law student at National Law University, Jodhpur; Yadav is a final year law student at RMLNLU
