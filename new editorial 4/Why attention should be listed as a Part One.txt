Title: Why attention should be listed as a Part One

Why attention should be listed as a competency in higher education

As a competency in higher education The fix for our shortening attention is not more willpower, but less noise There is a Jataka tale about monkeys that were asked to water a king's garden. Wanting to be efficient, they decide each sapling needs exactly as much water as the length of its root. So, they pull up every plant to measure it.

The garden dies of good intentions. I thought of those monkeys while watching a bright postgraduate student explain her study routine. She had four productivity apps, a Pomodoro timer, a colour-coded dashboard, two accountability WhatsApp groups, and a phone that buzzed nine times during our half-hour talk.

She was uprooting her attention every few minutes to check whether it was growing. The numbers are no longer anecdotal. EY's annual media report found Indians spent 1.1 trillion hours on smartphones in 2024, about five hours a day per user, nearly 70% of it on social media, gaming and videos. Surveys place Indian teenagers above seven hours of daily screen exposure.

A January 2026 study in the International Journal of Indian Psychology, surveying university students across disciplines, found that rising screen time had a significant effect on declining attention span, with students reporting greater difficulty concentrating and completing tasks without frequent breaks. A correlational study at a university, using the Digit Span Test, found the same association. We have built an education system that assumes sustained attention while operating in an environment that systematically dismantles it.

Which means the knowledge gap is not between what students know and what they should know. It is between what we ask of their attention and what we have built to support it. That gap has a policy name nobody uses. NEP 2020 asks for critical thinking, holistic development, and learning how to learn, but not one syllabus in Indian higher education lists attention as a competency.

We examine what we never taught. The wrong fix We are a nation that loves exhortation. Our hierarchies, moral codes, and collective identity stories nudge us toward a familiar reflex: prescribe willpower. Concentrate harder. Wake at 4.00 am. Delete Instagram. Take a vow. A senior school principal in Bengaluru put it plainly: "We kept adding a new app for attendance, another for assignments, a parents' group, a staff group.

Nobody ever asked what we should stop. The teachers were exhausted, and none of it was because of teaching." Her instinct  -  that the problem is accumulation, not indiscipline  -  echoes with that of the cognitive psychologist Yousri Marzouki's. Writing in Psyche in February 2026, he proposes 'psychological minimalism': the trouble is not a deficit of technique but an excess of cognitive noise.

Rather than relying on constant self-regulation, clarity emerges when fewer demands compete for limited cognitive resources. Attention becomes less about moment-to-moment control and more about structural support. The distinction matters for institutions. Willpower is a private virtue; structure is a public good.

If focus depends on individual grit, a university can only nag. If focus depends on design, a university can build it. Audit before the vow For students, the first step is reconnaissance, not renunciation. Relevant literature asks you to spend one day marking every attention leak: each phone glance, each tab-hop, each non-urgent ping and then identify the two or three highest-frequency, lowest-value inputs.
