Title: Demographic Deadline

Demographic Deadline

For decades, India worried about having too many people. Population control, family planning and the spectre of unchecked demographic growth shaped public policy and political debate. For decades, India worried about having too many people. Population control, family planning and the spectre of unchecked demographic growth shaped public policy and political debate.

Now the country confronts a reversal that should provoke not panic, but serious thought: India's fertility rate has fallen below the replacement level of 2.1 children per woman. The significance lies not in an imminent population collapse. India will continue growing for some time because of demographic momentum.

Nor should falling fertility automatically be treated as a national failure. On the contrary, it reflects profound social change: women are marrying later, receiving more education and exercising greater control over reproduction; families are responding rationally to the rising costs of raising children. The real question is whether India's institutions have kept pace with this transformation.

India's demographic dividend ~ the economic advantage supposedly conferred by a large working-age population ~ was always presented as a historic opportunity. But a demographic dividend is not a gift. Young people become an economic asset only when they are healthy, educated and productively employed.

Otherwise, a large youth population can become a source of frustration rather than prosperity. That makes declining fertility a deadline. India has a limited period in which an exceptionally large working-age population will support relatively fewer elderly dependants. If the country fails to generate productive employment, improve education and bring more women into the workforce during this window, it cannot assume that another generation will provide a second opportunity.

The challenge is complicated by India's enormous internal demographic differences. Bihar and Uttar Pradesh remain substantially younger than southern and eastern states where fertility has fallen sharply. Kerala and Tamil Nadu are already confronting questions associated with ageing societies, while other parts of the country continue to grapple with a rapidly expanding young population.

This divergence also has political consequences. The proposed delimitation of parliamentary constituencies has exposed an uncomfortable contradiction in Indian federalism: states that successfully controlled population growth fear being penalised with diminished political representation, while faster-growing states could gain more seats. A demographic achievement in one generation may thus become a political disadvantage in the next.

Yet the worst response would be to imitate countries that have reacted to low fertility with nationalist anxiety and exhortations for women to have more children. India should resist the temptation to make reproduction a new instrument of public policy. The more urgent task is to prepare for the society that demographic change is already creating: an economy requiring higher productivity rather than merely more workers; cities capable of supporting smaller families and an ageing population; workplaces that enable women to combine careers and motherhood; and social-security systems that do not depend indefinitely on ever-larger younger generations.

India's demographic revolution should therefore be understood neither as catastrophe nor celebration. It is a transition that imposes a timetable on the country's economic ambitions. The question is no longer how many Indians the future will produce. It is whether India can make the most of those it already has.
