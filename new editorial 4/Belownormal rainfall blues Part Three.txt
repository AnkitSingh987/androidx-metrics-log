Title: Belownormal rainfall blues Part Three

Below-normal rainfall blues

Contract longevity is a built-in feature. Now this traditional model is under pressure from AI, which is beginning to erode labour arbitrage. These firms face a profound strategic dilemma. As clients rethink their cost structures and seek to renegotiate or exit legacy arrangements, the temptation for service providers to engage in rapid liquidation of these commitments will become immense.

Leadership within these firms could easily justify redirecting resources towards higher-margin work while allowing legacy contracts to wither under the argument that the market has fundamentally changed and capital should follow growth. Many will take that route. However, doing so ignores the structural debt embedded in those relationships, which were built on years of mutual dependence that cannot be easily replicated.

Fiduciary resilience in this context requires the unfashionable commitment to staying the course. Not blindly or without adaptation, but with the intent to optimise and evolve the legacy model using the very tools that currently threaten it. Firms that choose to absorb the inconvenience of honouring these commitments are essentially buying a form of insurance that reinforces their position as reliable partners at a time when reliability is an increasingly scarce commodity.

In the world of enterprise technology, the client who is supported through a difficult transition today is often the same client who awards the next major transformation mandate tomorrow. Conversely, a firm that optimises solely for the quarterly margin by stepping away from difficult obligations may find itself excluded from future opportunities. I can understand the pressure to do so, having myself lived under the gun of quarterly numbers.

But trust, once discounted, is prohibitively expensive to rebuild. One example from my career springs to mind. Several years ago, a British energy major was looking to lift and shift work from one Indian major in Hyderabad to another in Bangalore. A large amount of work: circa 1,000 people. My team and I were asked to assist in this transition.

Understandably, there was great friction at the ground level. A few calls with the "losing" CEO later, the process became significantly easier. The "losing" firm continued on the supplier panel of the client, and won a large, long-term, and profitable contract less than a year later. To a purely financial model, cooperating with one's own displacement looks inefficient.

Time is spent where returns are uncertain. Or in this case, returns were disappearing. But future transaction friction is reduced. The trust premium is, simply put, the gap between what a deal is worth in theory and what it costs to execute it in practice. When reliability becomes a form of infrastructure, legal safeguards, while still present, become less central to the execution of the deal.

There is a profound cost to ignoring this, as every abandoned obligation creates a form of reputational debt that follows a firm across transactions and cycles, regardless of how it attempts to narrate the departure. In a world where information is persistent and professional networks are dense, the ability to constantly reinvent oneself has hit a ceiling of diminishing returns. The market eventually prices the pattern rather than the story.

While strategy must adapt to evolving markets, there remains a line between legitimate renegotiation and opportunistic abandonment. The line matters. It becomes even sharper as execution is increasingly handled by automated code. Technology does not remove the human choice to honour or exit a commitment.
