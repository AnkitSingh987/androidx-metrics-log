Title: A flood isnt just a natural disaster Part One

A flood isn't just a natural disaster

A river does not know the language of development. A mountain does not understand a building permit. A river does not know the language of development. A mountain does not understand a building permit. A glacier recognises no political boundary, yet human beings increasingly behave as though nature must adjust itself to our plans.

The devastation in Nepal should make us ask a more uncomfortable question than simply, "Why did it rain so heavily?" or "Why did the glacier collapse?" An extreme natural event may trigger a disaster, but human decisions often determine its scale. When settlements, roads, bridges and economic assets are placed in vulnerable locations while natural protection is destroyed, a hazard becomes a catastrophe. We have become good at predicting hazards but poor at respecting warnings.

We are engineering the landscape to make civilisation possible while simultaneously engineering away its capacity to protect civilisation. This is not merely an engineering failure. It is a failure of responsibility  -  a willingness to ignore nature in the name of progress. Modern development asks, "What can we build here?" It should first ask, "What can this landscape safely carry?" Climate change makes this question more urgent.

Burning coal, oil and gas, deforestation and destruction of natural ecosystems increase greenhouse gases and warm the planet. In the Himalayas, warming is affecting glaciers, snow and rainfall, increasing the risks of floods and landslides. But climate change should not become an excuse for poor decisions.

A forest is not merely a collection of trees. It is a living community of trees, shrubs, grasses, insects, birds, fungi and microorganisms that protects the land and regulates water. Roots hold soil, vegetation slows rainfall, organic matter stores water, and biodiversity helps forests recover from disturbances.

Destroying forests or their biodiversity means losing a natural system that slows floods, prevents erosion, stabilises slopes and releases water gradually. A forest may still look green after losing much of its biodiversity, but it may no longer provide the protection of a healthy ecosystem. Then comes the assault on the river.

We encroach upon floodplains and later describ e the river as "encroaching" upon us. We narrow channels, occupy banks, fill wetlands and cover soil with concrete, then wonder why water refuses to obey the drainage plan. Unregulated mining of sand, stone and gravel can destabilise riverbeds and accelerate erosion.

Hill cutting without proper geological assessment can turn stable slopes into landslide hazards. Development is necessary, but excess can be dangerous. A road providing essential connectivity is development; a road cut through an unstable mountain for speculative construction is something else. Infrastructure driven by speed, prestige or profit, without regard to ecological limits, becomes a gamble with public safety.

Development that destroys the natural systems on which society depends is not genuine progress. It is borrowed prosperity, with nature eventually presenting the bill. Corruption is not merely money changing hands. There is also corruption of judgment: scientific warnings ignored because they are inconvenient; environmental assessments reduced to paperwork; safety margins compromised; political visibility valued above ecological necessity; and responsibilities divided among agencies until nobody is accountable.

Scientists may produce excellent data, engineers sound designs and meteorologists timely warnings. But if scientific knowledge does not change construction, land-use or evacuation decisions, science becomes decoration. We often ask scientists to explain disasters after they occur instead of asking why known risks were allowed to accumulate.
