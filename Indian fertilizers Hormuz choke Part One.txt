Title: Indian fertilizers Hormuz choke Part One

Indian fertilizers' Hormuz choke

Given India's import dependence, a prolonged war can raise fertilizer prices straining the fisc and spur inflation The Iran-Israel war and resultant blockage of Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint handling major energy and fertilizer feedstock flows, can destabilise global fuel, fertilizers and food markets. For India, whose agricultural productivity depends heavily on imported fertilizers and energy-linked inputs, the conflict introduces serious vulnerabilities. Even if Iran facilitates uninterrupted access to India on this route, ongoing war operations have already led to rerouting of vessels, reduced ship availability, rising freight rates, refusal of insurance coverage or unaffordable high war-risk premiums and shutdown of gas terminals.

These conditions complicate the movement of bulk commodities from Middle Eastern export hubs. India imported fertilizers worth nearly $7.2 billion, accounting for 2.9 per cent of global fertilizer imports and ranking seventh worldwide, underscoring the country's structural integration with global fertilizer markets. If the crisis prolongs, it could trigger fertilizer supply uncertainty, rising prices, larger subsidy burdens, and inflationary pressures across agriculture; therefore, it is important to decode these risks and explore the way forward.

Supply-chain dynamics India's fertilizer ecosystem is closely tied to global markets because domestic production is uneven across nutrient categories. While India has developed strong capacity in nitrogenous fertilizers, the country remains highly dependent on imports for phosphatic and potassic nutrients. India has highest vulnerability in Diammonium Phosphate (DAP) (see table).

India is the largest importer globally, accounting for 28.7 per cent of global DAP imports. Major suppliers include Saudi Arabia (24 per cent), Morocco (22 per cent), both in critical conflict zones affecting maritime access via Hormuz and Red Sea. Any logistical disruption in these supply routes directly and adversely complicates fertilizer accessibility, availability and affordability as shipping delays and elevated freight costs can rapidly raise landed prices for Indian importers.

Similarly, India imported about $2.2 billion worth of urea, representing 7.2 per cent of global imports and 20-25 per cent of domestic requirements. Major suppliers such as Oman (15 per cent), and Saudi Arabia (9.5 per cent) are from conflict zone raising dependence on Russia (18 per cent) and associated price pressures. Moreover, urea production depends heavily on natural gas because ammonia synthesis is energy intensive.

If geopolitical tensions disrupt gas supply chains or maritime logistics in the Gulf region, the global cost of ammonia and urea production could increase sharply. Indeed, urea prices have already risen by around $80 per tonne, tightening availability and increasing import costs. The risk extends beyond imports to domestic production as India operates more than 30 urea manufacturing plants, most of which rely on natural gas or naphtha feedstock.

A considerable share of India's liquefied natural gas imports originates in the Gulf region and prolonged disruptions in LNG shipments can significantly raise production costs for Indian fertilizer manufacturers or reduce operational capacity. Thus, even domestically produced fertilizers remain indirectly exposed to geopolitical shocks through energy markets. Potassic fertilizers represent a major vulnerability in India's fertilizer supply chain.

Potassium chloride (MOP) shows near-total import dependence of about 95-100 per cent, with major suppliers including Russia and Canada. Similarly, NPK complex fertilizers, where imports meet nearly 35-45 per cent of domestic demand, depend heavily on imported raw materials such as phosphoric acid and potash. These patterns reveal the structural fragility of India's fertilizer system, which remains closely tied to global trade and energy-linked supply chains.
