Title: A Phone Problem

Category: Intermediate

Tom adjusted his tie in the mirror of his hotel room, taking a deep breath to steady his nerves. Today was arguably the most important day of his professional life. His company had sent him to Berlin to negotiate a major logistics contract, and if he succeeded, a promotion was guaranteed. He had spent weeks preparing his presentation, memorizing facts, and anticipating questions. All he had to do now was get to the client’s headquarters on the other side of the city by 10:00 AM. 

He grabbed his briefcase and checked his smartphone. It was 8:30 AM, giving him plenty of time to navigate the unfamiliar city. He opened his map application, typed in the address of the headquarters, and reviewed the route. It seemed simple enough: take the U-Bahn subway for six stops, transfer to a bus, and walk two blocks. 

Feeling confident, Tom left the hotel and walked briskly toward the nearest subway station. The morning air was crisp, and the streets were filled with commuters rushing to work. As he walked down the stairs into the station, he pulled out his phone to check which platform he needed. 

Suddenly, a businessman rushing past him bumped hard into his shoulder. The impact caused the phone to slip from Tom’s grasp. He watched in horror as the device tumbled down the concrete steps, bouncing violently before landing face-down at the bottom. 

"Sorry! Excuse me!" the businessman shouted over his shoulder, barely slowing down as he disappeared into the crowd.

Tom hurried down the steps and carefully picked up his phone. The screen was completely shattered, a web of deep cracks distorting the glass. He pressed the power button, hoping it was only cosmetic damage, but the screen remained terrifyingly black. He pressed it again, held it down, and tapped the glass, but the phone was completely dead.

Panic washed over him like cold water. All his directions, his contact numbers, his presentation notes, and his digital train tickets were on that device. He didn't speak German, he had never been to Berlin before, and he didn't even remember the exact street name of the headquarters, only the general district. 

He stood frozen in the middle of the busy subway station, feeling utterly helpless. His entire modern existence was contained within that broken rectangle of glass and metal. Without it, he felt blind.

He checked his wristwatch. It was 8:45 AM. He couldn't afford to go back to the hotel to try and access a computer; he would definitely miss the meeting. He had to figure this out right now.

Tom took a deep breath, trying to suppress the rising panic. He remembered his grandfather telling stories about traveling across Europe in the 1970s with nothing but a paper map and a few phrases in a local language. "If he could do it," Tom muttered to himself, "I can navigate one city."

He looked around the station and noticed a small newspaper kiosk. He approached the elderly woman behind the counter. "Excuse me," he said, speaking slowly and clearly in English. "Do you sell maps? A map of the city?"

The woman nodded, pulling a folded paper map from a rack and handing it to him. Tom paid with a crumpled banknote, thanking her profusely. He unfolded the large map, which immediately proved difficult to manage as other commuters brushed past him. 

He remembered the name of the district where the meeting was located: Mitte. He scanned the dense grid of streets until he found the neighborhood. Now, he needed the exact address. He closed his eyes, trying to visualize the digital map he had looked at in his hotel room. It was near a large park, and the building was called the 'Friedrich Tower'. 

"Okay, find the park," he whispered, dragging his finger across the paper. He spotted the green area on the map and found a street nearby named Friedrichstraße. It was a guess, but it was the best he had.

He studied the subway lines printed on the map, tracing the route from his current station to the one nearest the park. It was much harder than following a blue dot on a screen, but he eventually figured out he needed the yellow line heading north. 

Tom navigated the station, reading the physical signs hanging from the ceiling instead of staring at a screen. He boarded the train and counted the stops, watching the station names out the window carefully so he wouldn't miss his transfer. Without his phone to distract him with emails or news, he found himself actually observing the city and the people around him. He noticed the architecture of the underground stations and the varied styles of the Berlin commuters. 

When he finally emerged from the subway in the Mitte district, the sun was shining brightly. However, he still didn't know exactly where the Friedrich Tower was. He checked his watch; it was 9:20 AM. 

He decided he needed local knowledge. He walked into a small, busy bakery and waited for a break in the line. He approached the young man working behind the counter. 

"Excuse me, I'm very lost," Tom admitted, feeling slightly embarrassed. "My phone is broken. Do you know where the Friedrich Tower is?"

The young man wiped his hands on his apron and smiled friendly. "Ah, yes. It is not far. You go straight down this street for three blocks, turn left at the pharmacy, and you will see it. It is a very tall building with dark glass."

"Thank you, thank you so much," Tom said, feeling a massive surge of relief. 

He followed the directions precisely. Walking without the constant guidance of GPS felt strange, but it also felt liberating. He had to trust his own senses and memory. He counted the blocks, spotted the glowing green sign of the pharmacy, and turned left. There, towering above the older buildings, was a sleek, modern skyscraper with dark glass. The Friedrich Tower.

Tom walked into the lobby, checking his watch one last time. It was 9:45 AM. He had made it with fifteen minutes to spare. 

The meeting went remarkably well. Despite the stress of the morning, Tom felt surprisingly energized and focused. Because he hadn't been able to review his notes on his phone during the commute, he had been forced to trust his preparation. He spoke naturally and confidently, maintaining strong eye contact with the clients instead of reading from a screen. 

When the meeting concluded, the lead executive shook his hand warmly. "Excellent presentation, Tom. We are very impressed with your proposal. We'll be in touch by the end of the week."

Leaving the building, Tom felt a wave of triumph. He walked out onto the bustling Berlin street and reached into his pocket out of habit, pulling out the broken phone. He looked at the shattered screen and laughed quietly to himself. 

He would definitely need to buy a new phone before flying home, and he knew the rest of the day would be complicated without internet access or an easy way to contact his office. He would have to find an internet cafe or use the hotel's business center. But as he stood on the sidewalk, unfolding his paper map to figure out how to get back to his hotel, he didn't feel anxious anymore. He had faced a modern nightmare and survived by relying on old-fashioned skills, human connection, and his own intelligence. He realized that while technology was incredibly useful, it wasn't strictly necessary for survival. He picked a direction on the map, folded it up, and started walking, enjoying the feeling of being genuinely present in the city.
