Title: Freedom of a nation and naval strategy Part Three

Freedom of a nation and naval strategy

It took a sustained line of naval thinkers to keep the maritime case alive against that gravitational pull. Rear Admiral Raja Menon's writing through the 1990s and 2000s pressed the argument for a codified Indian maritime strategy including sea denial, force structure, the discipline of thinking about the ocean as a theatre in its own right at a time when the institutional instinct still deferred reflexively to the Army. Admiral Arun Prakash, both as Chief of the Naval Staff and in the years since, has been a persistent public voice for indigenous shipbuilding, naval modernisation, and the case that maritime power is not an adjunct to India's strategic autonomy but a precondition for it.

Each generation, in effect, had to re-argue what Panikkar had already said in 1945. And each round of arguing eventually hardened into doctrine: the Indian Maritime Doctrine of 2004, Freedom to Use the Seas in 2007, Ensuring Secure Seas in 2015, and now the MAHASAGAR vision and the Indian Navy Maritime Security Strategy of 2026. This is not a story of sudden foresight.

It is a story of an argument that had to be won repeatedly, inside India, before it could be written down. Doctrine meets its moment The events of this year have not required the Navy to revise its thinking so much as confirm it. The Indian Ocean's nine chokepoints, through which more than 60% of the world's maritime trade and some 80% of its oil moves, have behaved exactly as the doctrine anticipated they might, as points of leverage, not mere geography.

One can easily recall the scale of the Hormuz disruption earlier this year and the shipping delays and cost spikes that followed even the threat of closure. What is worth noting on this particular anniversary is not the crisis itself, but the fact that India's naval establishment had already built its planning assumptions around exactly this kind of contingency. Freedom as a verb, not a plaque Independence Day tends to be marked as commemoration of something that happened in 1947.

The maritime record suggests a different grammar. "Freedom to use the seas... under all circumstances" is not a monument. It is a standing verb, something that has to be exercised continuously, contested afresh with every generation, as Sardar Panikkar, Raja Menon, Arun Prakash and many others understood in turn.

The Navy's current leadership speaks in the same register. Taking charge as the 27th Chief of the Naval Staff this May, Admiral Krishna Swaminathan noted that the Indian Navy "stands vigilant to protect national interests wherever they are and is very actively deployed in a regional security environment that continues to remain challenging, complex, unpredictable, and uncertain". Coming in the scenario of the Hormuz blockade and as we now see the Houthi action, it reads less like a routine assumption-of-command remark than a restatement of what the doctrine has said since 2007.

The Indian Navy chief has stated what every strategist must be unequivocal about: freedom to use the seas, under all circumstances, is not claimed just once. It is exercised continuously, or it is not truly held ever. Eighty years ago, the Indian Navy inherited a handful of former Royal Navy vessels and little else.

Today, it operates from a doctrine the rest of the world is only beginning to read, commanded by officers who speak of vigilance as a permanent condition rather than a crisis response. That is India's maritime inheritance, properly understood on its 80th Independence Day. It is not merely a border patrol and not even a coastline to be defended as an afterthought to the land.
