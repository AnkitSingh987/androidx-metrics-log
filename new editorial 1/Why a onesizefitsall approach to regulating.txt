Title: Why a onesizefitsall approach to regulating

Why a one-size-fits-all approach to regulating NBFCs?

A smarter framework would treat size as a preliminary filter, not the final verdict. Risk-weighted classification should follow As the RBI moves to expand the upper-layer NBFC list to include large, bank-backed entities, a basic regulatory dilemma comes into focus: should NBFCs with identical balance-sheet sizes but vastly different business models face the same supervisory intensity?

A ₹40,000-crore vehicle finance company backed by hard collateral and stable cash flows is fundamentally different from a ₹40,000-crore unsecured digital lender. The scale-based regulation (SBR) framework relies heavily on asset size to classify NBFCs. While this offers administrative simplicity, it underestimates a central truth of financial stability: risk is shaped by asset quality, funding structure, and interconnectedness  -  not by size alone.

Traditional vehicle finance NBFCs such as Sundaram Finance, Shriram Finance, and Mahindra Finance illustrate why asset quality should outweigh asset size in regulation. Their portfolios are anchored in tangible, income-generating collateral. Commercial vehicles generate daily cash flows, enabling natural debt servicing; loan tenures of three to five years align well with asset life and funding cycles; and deep secondary markets allow repossessed vehicles to be resold quickly.

. Unsecured lending The more acute risks lie in fast-growing unsecured retail lending. Unsecured loans now account for a rising share of NBFC assets, funded increasingly through short-term instruments. Borrower overlap is high, with many individuals carrying multiple live loans, raising the probability of correlated defaults.

These structures amplify rollover risk and contagion  -  precisely the dynamics that triggered the IL&FS crisis. That episode underscored a key lesson: systemic stress emerges from opacity, maturity mismatches, and tight interlinkages with the banking system, not from long-established, collateral-backed lenders with predictable cash flows. A smarter framework would treat size as a preliminary filter, not the final verdict.

Risk-weighted classification should follow, giving greater weight to factors such as secured versus unsecured lending ratios, collateral liquidity, funding maturity, and dependence on bank borrowings. Secondary factors  -  track record, governance quality, sectoral concentration, and compliance history  - can refine the assessment. Current rules require NBFCs classified in the upper layer to remain under heightened regulation for at least five years, even if their risk profile improves.

Annual risk reviews, flexibility to migrate between layers, differentiated capital requirements, and phased compliance timelines would instead preserve stability without imposing unnecessary costs. Blunt, size-driven regulation could offset those gains for institutions that fund the real economy. For asset-backed lenders with decades of demonstrated discipline, regulatory intensity should reflect actual risk  -  not balance-sheet bulk.

By Gopalaswamy J The writer has worked at a senior level in leading NBFCs
