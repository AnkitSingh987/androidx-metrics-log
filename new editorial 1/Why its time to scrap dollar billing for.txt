Why it's time to scrap dollar billing for local SEZ deals

Rupee billing could give information technology firms another reason to stay in India's special economic zones India's special economic zones were built on a simple export logic: transactions with a customer outside the zone, in the domestic tariff area (DTA), must earn foreign exchange for the country. That rule still applies to services, even though it was relaxed for goods long ago. A software company, or a defence contractor sitting inside an SEZ, selling to an Indian client a few kilometres away in the same city, is currently required to bill that client in dollars.

The Indian buyer must first purchase foreign currency from an authorised dealer bank, paying a commission, and the SEZ unit must then convert those dollars back into rupees, paying another commission, for a transaction that never actually leaves the country. Public sector undertakings in defence, space and aerospace, which rely on specialised maintenance and engineering services from SEZ-based firms, have complained for years that this pushes up costs without any benefit to the exchequer. rupee payments The Commerce Department has circulated a draft Cabinet note, proposing an amendment to Section 2(z) of the SEZ Act, 2005, the provision that governs how services must be paid for.

The change would allow SEZ units to receive rupee payments for services rendered to DTA customers, bringing services in line with the treatment already available for goods. Officials are also working to align the statutory definition of a service with the one used under the Goods and Services Tax law. The sectors expected to benefit the most are IT, IT-enabled services, defence, aerospace, engineering, maintenance, and repair and overhaul work, where an Indian unit can often complete a task in about a week, compared with over a year for a foreign vendor.

The note is currently under inter-ministerial consultation among Commerce, Finance, and the Reserve Bank before it goes to the Cabinet. If cleared, the amendment removes the pain point for the services sector and makes SEZ units more attractive to local vendors, particularly for government departments reluctant to handle the paperwork and costs of foreign-currency billing. It also arrives at a moment when several IT firms have been reconsidering their SEZ presence following the withdrawal of earlier Income Tax incentives, so rupee billing could be one more reason to stay put.

The grey areas are just as real. SEZs are legally treated as territory outside India for many purposes, and that fiction underpins their customs and tax privileges. Loosening the foreign exchange requirement for services, without an equally tight definition of what constitutes a domestic transaction, risks blurring the line that regulators have historically guarded closely.

How tightly the final rules are drawn will depend on the balance between a Commerce Ministry keen on ease of doing business and a Finance Ministry watchful of revenue leakage. A harmonised definition of services across the SEZ Act and the GST law is the right starting point, paired with sector-specific guidance, since the needs of a defence contractor billing a public sector client are not the same as those of an IT firm billing a private one. The RBI and authorised dealer banks will need a clear operating framework for rupee settlement so that banks are not left to interpret an ambiguous rule on their own.

This amendment need not be seen only as a narrow fix to a billing anomaly. It could be a first step towards a broader rethink of how SEZs, designed for a world of export-led growth, now fit into an economy where the domestic market is often the bigger opportunity. By P Saravanan & A Paul Williams Saravanan is Professor of Finance and Accounting at IIM-Tiruchirappalli; Williams is the Head of India at Sernova Financial
