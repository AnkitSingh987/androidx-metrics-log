Title: The informal sector needs better books Part One

The informal sector needs better books

Informal sector entrepreneurs need to be shown that better bookkeeping practices solve problems they care about, such as tracking credit to customers, identifying profitable products Could the key to India's MSME growth be hiding in plain sight? Even as the government considers a ₹2,000 crore technology upgradation scheme for MSMEs, one must acknowledge that these schemes, while a step in the right direction, can still only reach a fraction of India's vast number of MSMEs. Over 90 per cent of MSMEs operate in the informal sector, making them largely invisible to policymakers and generally unable to access government schemes.

At the same time, this sector faces a persistent credit gap that leaves millions of microenterprises struggling to access capital. The prevailing policy response is to push informal enterprises towards formality by encouraging them to register and meet compliance requirements. However, the solution to informality might not be that simple.

Studies show that merely reducing registration costs and making it easier to comply does not generally work as informality is often a deliberate choice. Many small entrepreneurs prefer to operate under the radar. Take for instance Kumar, who runs a general provisions store in Bara Bazaar in Shillong, largely self-financed and staffed by members of his family.

He has never availed of any government schemes, even when the Covid lockdown forced his store to shut for months, pushing him to the brink. Kumar's doubts about formal credit are echoed across the informal sector where distrust in the system runs deep. "If I take a loan, I will be trapped," he says. "I will have to pay every month and what if I can't pay some months?" Beyond distrust, formal systems often do not factor in the informal arrangements on which these businesses depend  -  relying on family labour, borrowing from community members, and operating flexibly to cover expenses.

Formal systems are not designed to recognise or replace these practices. Replacing deeply entrenched norms, such as community-based risk-sharing, with formal structures could leave entrepreneurs worse off. They would be stripped of their traditional support systems without necessarily gaining the benefits of formality.

The real problem A key difference between informal and formal firms is productivity, not just legal status. Formal businesses get more from their labour and capital investments. A registered factory with access to bank credit can hire well-trained workers and invest in better machines. An informal business, by contrast, uses low-skilled workers instead of better equipment because loans are either unavailable or perceived to be too risky.

Thus, these businesses remain unproductive and stagnate. Informal businesses face numerous constraints, including limited access to capital and unreliable infrastructure  -  challenges largely beyond their control. One practical solution, however, lies within reach: improving accounting and bookkeeping practices.

We often think of accounting as a compliance necessity. But quality accounting systems do more than just meet compliance requirements. They are also decision-making tools. Even within the same industry, productivity gaps across firms can be traced in part to accounting differences. In case of unexpected shocks, higher quality accounting can help firms adapt faster.

Accounting helps entrepreneurs understand which activities are profitable, where to invest scarce resources, and how to respond when problems arise. It transforms gut feelings into informed choices. And unlike improving infrastructure or expanding credit markets, better bookkeeping can be achieved at low cost.
