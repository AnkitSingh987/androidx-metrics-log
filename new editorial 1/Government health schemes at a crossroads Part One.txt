Title: Government health schemes at a crossroads Part One

Government health schemes at a crossroads

Better-designed treatment packages that reduce both administrative complexity and opportunities for over-treatment are needed For more than seven decades, the Central Government Health Scheme (CGHS) has provided healthcare security to serving and retired government employees and their families. Long before health insurance became a mainstream policy objective in India, CGHS established an important principle: illness should not expose families to financial hardship and people should have access to quality healthcare when they need it. That principle remains as relevant today as it was in 1954.

But the system built around it needs to change. The issue goes well beyond CGHS. The Railways, the Ex-Servicemen Contributory Health Scheme (ECHS), the Central Armed Police Forces and several State governments operate their own health schemes for employees and pensioners. Their institutional arrangements differ, but many face a common challenge: inadequate budget provision, increasing pool of beneficiaries, rising healthcare costs, fragmented purchasing arrangements and inadequate incentives for either providers or beneficiaries to use resources efficiently.

As healthcare costs continue to rise at double-digit rates, this model cannot remain fiscally sustainable indefinitely. The government's recent revision of CGHS reimbursement rates was timely and necessary. Hospitals cannot be expected to provide quality care at rates that bear little relationship to the cost of delivering it.

But periodically increasing reimbursement rates without reforming the underlying purchasing system risks treating the symptom rather than the disease. Payment process The more fundamental question is not simply how much government pays for healthcare, but how it pays for it. This is where reform must begin.

First, government schemes need to move decisively away from fee-for-service payment. Under fee-for-service, every additional consultation, investigation or procedure generates additional revenue for the provider. The incentives therefore favour more services rather than better outcomes or greater efficiency.

Wherever feasible, payment should instead be based on well-defined treatment packages. A hospital treating a patient for a particular condition should receive a predetermined amount covering the entire episode of care, rather than billing separately for every investigation, procedure and consumable. Over time, India should move towards more sophisticated systems such as diagnosis-related groups, which adjust payments for the complexity of patients while encouraging efficient treatment.

Better-designed packages would reduce both administrative complexity and opportunities for over-treatment. Second, medicines offer another major opportunity for efficiency. Government should use its enormous purchasing power far more effectively. Generic medicines of assured quality should be the default across government schemes, supported by strong formularies, bulk procurement, better inventory management and prescription audits.

The potential savings are substantial. Medicine expenditure is one of the fastest-growing components of CGHS expenditure, with excessive dependence on purchases through authorised local chemists rather than more efficient procurement channels. The objective should not be to restrict access to medicines but to obtain the same quality at a substantially lower cost.

Third, gatekeeping needs to become much more effective. A health system in which patients can move directly to expensive specialist and tertiary care without appropriate clinical referral will inevitably become costly. CGHS already has an important asset in its network of wellness centres. These should become genuine coordinators of care  -  providing strong primary care, managing chronic diseases, ensuring preventive screening and referring patients to specialists when clinically necessary.

Similar mechanisms should be strengthened in ECHS, Railways and state employee schemes. Effective gatekeeping is not about denying care. It is about ensuring that the patient receives the right care, at the right level, at the right time. Fourth, we need a mature discussion about beneficiary contributions.
