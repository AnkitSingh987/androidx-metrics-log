Title: The informal sector needs better books Part Two

The informal sector needs better books

It is within reach. No benefit seen Many informal sector entrepreneurs do not need to be taught record-keeping. They generally understand basic accounting concepts but do not see the value in adopting better bookkeeping practices. This is especially true among retail traders who are a significant part of the informal sector.

As Kong Mertina who runs a small textile shop in Bara Bazaar put it, "I know how much I buy for and how much I sell for. At the end of the day, I know if I've made money or not. Writing it down won't change that." In surveys we conducted, 60 per cent of retail traders did not maintain any type of record-keeping, or relied on rudimentary tools when they did.

This scepticism mirrors the broader distrust of formal financial systems. Just as many entrepreneurs ask "Why should I take a bank loan when I can borrow from my neighbour?" they question why they should maintain detailed records when they already have an intuitive sense of their finances. Formal tools are perceived as unnecessary complications that serve external interests rather than their own.

Left to their own devices, many would never adopt better bookkeeping practices. They would need to be shown, through demonstration or peer example, that these tools solve problems they care about: tracking credit to customers, identifying profitable products, or simply knowing whether the business is making money or not. Enables informed decisions Rather than dragging informal firms into formal structures that they are not ready for, they must be met where they are.

Existing practices in informal markets can be enhanced by making them more systematic and more useful through minimally disruptive, technology-enabled tools. While compliance is important, the primary goal should be capability. Better information leads to better decisions which leads to higher productivity  -  eventually making formality worth pursuing.

This is a patient approach, but one that respects the wisdom embedded in informal systems while offering a genuine pathway forward. The informal sector cannot be formalised by fiat. But it can grow its way there one better decision at a time. By Rimmy E Tomy The writer is an associate professor at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business
