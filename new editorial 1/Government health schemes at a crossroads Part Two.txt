Title: Government health schemes at a crossroads Part Two

Government health schemes at a crossroads

When healthcare is completely free at the point of use, with virtually unlimited benefits, some degree of moral hazard is inevitable. This does not justify imposing substantial financial burdens on pensioners or people with serious illnesses. But modest and carefully designed co-payments or deductibles for selected services, particularly treatment in private hospitals, deserve consideration.

Such provisions can be differentiated by income, age and type of service, with appropriate exemptions for vulnerable groups and chronic conditions. Even a small element of cost sharing can influence utilisation behaviour without undermining financial protection. Structural issue There is also a larger structural question.

Why should different arms of government separately empanel hospitals, negotiate prices, process claims, build IT systems and establish anti-fraud mechanisms for essentially similar populations? Greater convergence across CGHS, ECHS, ESI, Railways and other government schemes could create larger risk pools and vastly strengthen the government's purchasing power. This effort should be led by the National Health Authority that implements my most cherished scheme Ayushman Bharat.

Complete institutional merger may take time and may not always be feasible, but common provider networks, standard treatment packages, shared technology platforms, interoperable claims systems and coordinated price negotiations should be entirely achievable. State governments could increasingly adopt the same architecture. None of this diminishes the importance of fixing the operational problems that providers rightly highlight today.

Payments must be timely. Hospitals need visibility into the claims lifecycle. Grievances and disputes must be resolved within clearly defined timelines. Digital platforms should simplify rather than complicate transactions. Fraud controls must be strong but should not become an excuse for delaying legitimate payments.

Public health programmes ultimately depend on partnerships. Governments finance healthcare, providers deliver healthcare and patients benefit from healthcare. When providers have confidence in the system, they are more willing to invest, innovate and expand capacity. When confidence weakens, the impact is eventually felt by beneficiaries.

CGHS has been one of India's most enduring healthcare success stories. The opportunity before policymakers today is not to dismantle what has worked but to strengthen its foundations for the future  -  and to apply these lessons across government health schemes. The recent revision in CGHS rates was an important first step.

The next step must be more ambitious: moving from simply paying healthcare bills to strategically purchasing healthcare. That is the transition required to ensure that the schemes relied upon by millions remain affordable, efficient, transparent and trusted by those who sustain them every day. By Indu Bhushan The writer, a Senior Associate with Johns Hopkins University, was founding CEO of Ayushman Bharat
