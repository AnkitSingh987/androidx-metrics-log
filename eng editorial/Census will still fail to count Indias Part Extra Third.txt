Both countries should direct academic and policy expertise towards pressing maritime challenges  -  undersea infrastructure protection, maritime law, cable security, supply chains, and AI-driven maritime systems.

Third, maritime security must extend beyond conventional defence.

Therefore, coastguards, ports, shipping, offshore energy, and communications infrastructure must be integrated into a unified security approach. Undersea cables illustrate why this matters  -  they carry roughly 98% of global internet traffic and underpin digital finance, critical infrastructure, and military communications. Protecting these assets will require close collaboration between government, private industry, and academic institutions in both countries.

Regional integration is the fourth step. India and Australia are influential members of the Indian Ocean Rim Association, Quad, and the Indian Ocean Naval Symposium. Rather than limiting cooperation to bilateral channels, both nations should position themselves as facilitators of broader regional public goods, maritime domain awareness, security cooperation, and disaster response across the Indian Ocean rim.

The declaration's commitment to working with Association of South East Asian Nations members and Pacific Island nations on these fronts is a promising step that plays to both regions' strengths. The declaration's emphasis on defence innovation, science and technology cooperation, and stronger supply chains sets a solid foundation. The real test now lies with policymakers and industry leaders, as they must translate this language into results.

Concrete steps like annual defence innovation summits, dedicated regular industry round tables, joint university research grants, startup innovation challenges, and regular Track 1.5 dialogues could help convert the road map's ambitions into tangible outcomes. India and Australia have laid out an impressive vision on paper. What remains is proving they can match that vision with sustained action.

UPI  -  an eventful journey 10 years on, UPI's success has been such that today it's the world's largest real-time payment system The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) began with a simple, yet ambitious idea, that sending money should be as easy as sending a message. Ten years later, it has grown into the largest real-time payment system anywhere in the world, one that has changed the way crores of Indians pay at a shop, settle a bill or send money home.

When UPI went live in 2016, digital payments were still at the margins of a largely cash-driven economy. The wider groundwork had, however, already been laid. The Digital India programme, launched in 2015, set the direction for a digitally connected economy. Jan Dhan expanded access to bank accounts, Aadhaar provided an identity layer, and mobile connectivity brought services closer to people.

Today, India has more than 108 crore internet users, 130 crore mobile subscribers and 145 crore Aadhaar holders, while 58.97 crore Jan Dhan accounts, of which 32.86 crore are held by women, anchor financial inclusion. Over ₹52 lakh crore has been transferred to beneficiaries through Direct Benefit Transfer. UPI added to this foundation the ability to move money instantly, and the results followed.
