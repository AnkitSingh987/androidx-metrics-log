Title: A deeper probe into FCNRB swap scheme Part Two

A deeper probe into FCNR(B) swap scheme

A tentative cost and return analysis reveals that even though the RBI hedges the forex risk on the principal amount, the interest cost will be a burden on the economy, as the return on investment will be lower than the cost. As reported in the media, banks are paying 6 to 7.5 per cent on these deposits, but the RBI's return on investment in foreign short-term liquid securities will average about 4 per cent (US three-month Treasury bill yield is currently about 4.1 per cent). According to the Governor, 48.5 per cent of the deposits under the scheme are for five years, 42 per cent for three to four years, and the balance of 9 per cent for four to five years.

Tentative calculations reveal that the annual interest outgo under the scheme could be about $4.3 billion on the five-year deposits and about $4.2 billion on the rest, or around $8.5 billion a year until the deposits mature. However, the interest earned on the corresponding reserves is calculated to be around $5 billion. This could result in a net pressure of about $3.5 billion a year on the current account of the BoP, and about $15 billion over the life of the deposits, resulting in higher debt service and CAD.

There is a cost to the RBI's balance sheet. The swaps released upwards of ₹11 lakh crore into the banking system, which the RBI must absorb through the variable rate reverse repo (VRRR) and the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF), at about 5.24 per cent and 5 per cent, respectively. Tentative calculation reveals that on September 11, with about ₹4.05 lakh crore under VRRR and ₹2.77 lakh crore under SDF, the annualised interest outgo from the RBI amounted to ₹21,238.40 crore for VRRR and ₹13,862.45 crore for SDF, or about ₹35,100 crore a year.

Every rupee of this eventually reduces the surplus the RBI transfers to the government. investment position According to international investment position data released by the RBI on June 30, India is a net liability country, with debt liabilities accounting for 56.6 per cent of total external liabilities as on March 31. External debt stood at $762.8 billion, or 20.8 per cent of GDP.

With the addition of about $136 billion (inclusive of ECBs) under the swap window, total debt liability will increase substantially, to about 24-25 per cent of GDP on end-March figures, putting pressure on forex reserves when the debt falls due. The external debt to reserves ratio will not worsen immediately since reserves rise by the same amount, but that is cold comfort. About $53 billion falls due in 2029-30 and $62 billion in mid-2031, and as each tranche enters the final year, it will add to short-term debt by residual maturity, which was already 47.3 per cent of the reserves in March.

These developments have adverse macroeconomic implications. The day of reckoning is not far away when the Indian economy will face the pressure of paying back these debt liabilities. When the 2013 FCNR(B) deposits of about $26 billion were repaid in 2016, the RBI had spent two years building a forward book.

This obligation is nearly five times as large. The authorities should be well prepared for this. Temporary comfort for the near term should not create conditions for a nation to sleepwalk into a debt trap in the longer term. Dr RK Pattnaik is a former central banker and a Professor at the Gokhale Institute of Politics and Economics, Pune.
