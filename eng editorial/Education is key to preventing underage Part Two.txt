Title: Education is key to preventing underage Part Two

Education is key to preventing underage driving

The law rightly places responsibility on adults. When a juvenile commits a motor-vehicle offence, Section 199A of the Motor Vehicles Act provides for serious consequences for the guardian or vehicle owner, subject to the safeguards prescribed in law. These may include imprisonment of up to three years, a fine of ₹25,000 and cancellation of the vehicle's registration for 12 months.

The juvenile may also be prevented from obtaining a driving licence until the age of 25. Yet enforcement alone cannot correct a behaviour that families quietly permit. Need for expanding road safety awareness Education must become a central part of the solution. Road safety should not be reduced to an occasional assembly, poster-making competition or annual awareness week.

It must be taught progressively as an essential life skill and a part of citizenship education. Children should understand traffic signs, pedestrian rights, helmet use, blind spots, stopping distance and the consequences of speeding, distraction and wrong-side driving. The learning must also go beyond memorising rules.

Age-appropriate case studies, simulated road situations, conversations with accident survivors, first-aid training and interactions with traffic police can help students understand how quickly a casual decision can become irreversible. Older students should be taught about adolescent brain development, risk perception, peer pressure and the legal consequences of underage driving. The objective is not to frighten them, but to build judgement before they are legally permitted to take control of a vehicle.

Schools witness the contradiction closely. They conduct road-safety programmes and repeatedly request that parents to not permit underage driving, only to see students arrive at or leave the school vicinity on vehicles they are legally too young to use. Schools can educate, counsel and alert. They cannot patrol every home.

Parents must therefore become active partners in road-safety education. A child who is taught traffic discipline at school but watches a parent jump signals, drive on the wrong side or use a mobile phone at the wheel receives two conflicting lessons. In such situations, adult behaviour will often speak louder than the classroom.

Parents sometimes get confused into thinking that being "cool" or progressive means giving children smartphones, powerful two-wheelers, or even cars much earlier than necessary. Love is expressed through access, while refusal is feared as emotional distance. But every desire need not become an entitlement.

A parent's firm "no" can be an act of protection not only for one's child, but also for every unknown person sharing the road. Police enforcement must be consistent rather than activated only after a fatal accident. Residential communities also need traffic-calming measures, pedestrian crossings, visible signage, and strict action against speeding and wrong-side driving.

Yet neither infrastructure nor enforcement can substitute for responsible parenting. Every family needs a simple, non-negotiable rule: no motor vehicle without the legally required age, licence, training and safety equipment. There must be no "just this once" and no sending children on convenient errands using vehicles they cannot lawfully drive.

Raising a child is a private joy, but it is also a public responsibility. We are not bringing up children only for our homes; we are preparing citizens who must safely share roads, institutions and a country with others. Before handing a minor the keys, every parent must ask: Is this momentary convenience worth another family's lifetime of grief and my child's lifetime of remorse?

(Dr. K.R. Maalathi is an educationist, school advisor, leadership coach, and the founder of Auuro Educational Services)
