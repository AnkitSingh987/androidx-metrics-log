Title: Missing Link

Missing Link

India's economy grew by a remarkable 7.8 per cent in the April-June quarter, comfortably exceeding expectations. Yet the celebration has been accompanied by an uncomfortable question: why does such rapid growth fail to generate a corresponding sense of economic confidence among many Indians?

The answer is not that the growth number is false. The new GDP series, introduced with a revised base year and improved methods of measuring output and prices, has been defended by the government and received support from the IMF. Manufacturing grew 9.2 per cent and services 10 per cent in the quarter, while investment and consumer demand also contributed.

The real problem is that GDP measures production, not prosperity. An economy can expand rapidly without distributing its gains evenly or generating enough secure, well-paid employment. This distinction matters in India, where millions of young people enter the workforce every year and where the quality of employment matters as much as the number of jobs.

That helps explain the strange coexistence of impressive macroeconomic statistics and economic dissatisfaction. Concerns about employment, wages and weak consumer sentiment persist even as factories produce more and services expand. Former policymakers have questioned whether the new GDP calculations fully capture economic reality, while the government insists that the methodology is sound.

The argument has become unnecessarily binary. There is a better way to read the numbers. The 7.8 per cent figure tells us that India is producing considerably more. It does not tell us whether an average household is becoming sufficiently more secure to feel the difference. Inflation makes that distinction sharper.

Consumer inflation rose to 4.82 per cent in August, with food prices rising faster and pressures spreading beyond food and fuel. For a household whose income is uncertain, even moderate inflation can erase much of the psychological benefit of headline growth. Nor should this be reduced to a question of public perception.

Growth becomes politically durable when people can connect it to visible improvements: a better job, rising wages, affordable housing, reliable healthcare, better schools, easier access to credit or greater confidence that their children will have opportunities. India has achieved something genuinely important if it can sustain growth at anything close to its present pace. But high growth creates an obligation for economic policy.

The objective cannot be merely to produce impressive national accounts; it must be to convert output into livelihoods. This is particularly urgent because India's demographic advantage will not last indefinitely. A large working-age population is an asset only when the economy can productively employ it.

Otherwise, demographic scale can become a source of frustration rather than prosperity. The political temptation is to choose between celebrating 7.8 per cent and questioning it. The more important question is simpler: how much of that 7.8 per cent reaches the lives of ordinary Indians? That is the test that will determine whether India's remarkable growth becomes merely a statistic ~ or the foundation of widely shared prosperity.
