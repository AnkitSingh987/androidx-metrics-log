Ensure smooth sailing after declaration The recent road map for India-Australia maritime cooperation is welcome; the next task is building a bottom-up ecosystem for meaningful outcomes The 2026 India-Australia Joint Declaration on Defence and Security Cooperation puts maritime security at the heart of the bilateral relationship, notably through a new Maritime Security Collaboration Road map (MSCR).

This signals that both countries now grasp how critical the maritime domain has become to the region's strategic, economic, and technological future. However, with the political framework now in place, the real challenge lies in implementation; building a bottom-up ecosystem involving the defence industry, research institutions, non-traditional maritime stakeholders, and regional partners to turn the declaration's ambitions into concrete outcomes. The joint declaration marks a key moment in one of Indo-Pacific's most consequential partnerships.

Among its many provisions strengthening defence ties, one commitment deserves particular attention: deepening maritime cooperation through a new India-Australia MSCR. This signals that both countries now grasp just how critical the maritime domain has become to the region's strategic, economic, and technological future. The relationship has travelled a long road since the 2009 Joint Declaration on Security Cooperation.

Strategic trust has deepened considerably since then, propelled by the 2020 Comprehensive Strategic Partnership and Mutual Logistics Support Agreement, alongside expanding military exchanges, defence-industrial links, structured dialogues, and the Quad. This new declaration builds on that momentum, emphasising interoperability, defence-industrial synergy, maritime cooperation, and emerging technologies. Yet, partnerships are only as valuable as their follow-throughs.

The moment has come for both countries to move past declarations and towards concrete implementation. The MSCR offers exactly that opportunity. It aims to improve coordination, information-sharing, and capability-building across maritime domain awareness, humanitarian assistance, disaster response, logistics, and undersea domain awareness.

These priorities reflect real pressures  -  intensifying competition in the Indian Ocean, rising militarisation, threats to maritime infrastructure, and increasing grey-zone activity. Both countries also depend on secure sea lanes for their economic well-being, making the protection of maritime commons a shared strategic imperative. The next task is building an ecosystem that draws in academia, industry, and other stakeholders.

Four key steps are critical to translating outputs into meaningful outcomes. Firstly, defence-industry collaboration must move beyond symbolic gestures and occasional trade delegations. The declaration calls for deeper and regular industry engagement and stronger supply-chain and innovation linkages, but achieving this requires a standing dialogue mechanism connecting manufacturers, maritime technology firms, shipbuilders, researchers, and startups to pursue real joint research and development and innovation projects.

The inaugural India-Australia Defence Industry Roundtable, held in Sydney in October 2025, has already laid the groundwork here. Australia brings strong capabilities in autonomous systems, underwater technology, cyber tools, and advanced manufacturing expertise, as documented in a CUTS International study commissioned by the defence ministry. Meanwhile, India offers substantial manufacturing scale and a fast-growing defence-industrial base.
