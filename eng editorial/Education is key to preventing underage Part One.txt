Title: Education is key to preventing underage Part One

Education is key to preventing underage driving

Education on road safety should not be reduced to an occasional assembly, poster-making competition or annual awareness week. It must be taught progressively as an essential life skill and a part of citizenship education What began as a routine vegetable run on September 8, 2026, ended in sudden tragedy on Nellikuppam Road in Chennai, leaving one man dead and a 14-year-old boy carrying a lifelong burden. To save a few minutes, the student who took a two-wheeler to a nearby roadside vendor took the wrong side of the road.

He struck an approaching motorist, who fell and was run over by a speeding lorry. The man died on the spot. The young rider is still a child. He may never have intended to harm anyone. Yet one family has lost a loved one, and the child must now live with the consequences of one impulsive decision. Behind this tragedy lies an uncomfortable but unavoidable question: who handed him the key?

Underage driving is not a harmless shortcut, a display of independence, or a sign of permissive modern parenting. It is a preventable danger for which adults must accept responsibility. Children riding scooters and motorcycles have become a disturbingly common sight in residential lanes, outside tuition centres, near schools and even on arterial roads.

We notice them, express concern, and then move on. Some adults dismiss the risk with familiar assurances: "It is only nearby"; "My child rides carefully"; or "Everyone does it." Roads, however, do not recognise parental confidence. Only speed, distance, reaction time, and human error is what matters.

India's road-safety burden is already enormous. According to the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways, the country recorded 4,80,583 road accidents and 1,72,890 deaths in 2023, approximately 474 lives lost every day. Two-wheeler users constituted the largest share of those killed. Wrong-side driving and lane indiscipline were associated with 9,432 deaths.

These are not merely statistics. Each number represents an empty chair at a dining table, a family changed forever and a future abruptly taken away. The World Health Organization identifies road traffic injuries as the leading cause of death among children and young adults aged five to 29. On Indian roads, where pedestrians, cyclists, two-wheelers, cars, buses and heavy vehicles often compete for limited space, one impulsive decision can trigger a chain of events that nobody can reverse.

The minimum driving age is not an arbitrary inconvenience. Under the Motor Vehicles Act, a person needs to be 18 to drive a motor vehicle. A narrow exception exists from the age of 16 for motorcycles with an engine capacity not exceeding 50 cc, subject to prescribed conditions and parental consent. This cannot be interpreted as blanket permission to ride the powerful scooters and motorcycles commonly used today.

Adolescents can certainly be intelligent, capable, and responsible. However, the areas of the brain involved in planning, impulse control, anticipating consequences, and resisting peer pressure continue to develop through adolescence and into early adulthood. This does not mean that every teenager is reckless.

It explains why legal safeguards, proper training and adult supervision are essential. Eighteen is also the age at which India grants voting rights and generally recognises legal adulthood. Such thresholds exist because judgement and emotional maturity develop gradually, not because a birthday magically transforms a person.
