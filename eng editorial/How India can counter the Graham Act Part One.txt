Title: How India can counter the Graham Act Part One

How India can counter the Graham Act

As the tariffs can hit exports, India must reduce dependence on Russian oil, conclude the bilateral trade deal with the US quickly, and widen the export market On February 20, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Learning Resources vs Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not authorise Trump to impose tariffs. Congress has since supplied what the Court found missing: the Lindsey O Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 - passed 86-11 in the Senate, 262-159 in the House, and now signed into law - authorises tariffs of up to 100 per cent on the five largest importers of Russian oil and gas. For India, the second-largest buyer of Russian crude after China, that makes this graver than last August's tariff shock: the threat is now statute, not something a court can strike down.

The arithmetic of exposure India imports over 88 per cent of its crude, and in July 2026 alone, Russia supplied $7.27 billion of India's $14.21 billion crude import bill  -  51.1 per cent, comfortably ahead of the UAE's 10.8 per cent and Saudi Arabia's 9.6 per cent. That dependence survived February 2026's pact that cut India's tariff burden from 50 to 18 per cent, partly in exchange for a pledge to diversify away from Russian crude. Yet, FY26 purchases of US oil reached just $9.87 billion, against a $40.82 billion Russian import bill.

India simultaneously has significant exposure to the American market. Merchandise exports to the US rose to $87.31 billion in FY26  -  19.78 per cent of India's exports and a $33.86 billion bilateral surplus  -  led by electrical machinery ($25.68 billion), pharmaceuticals ($8.67 billion), nuclear reactors ($7.19 billion), gems and jewellery ($5.12 billion), and minerals ($3.52 billion). Goldman Sachs puts India's total goods-and-services exposure to the US at roughly 4 per cent of GDP; ICRIER estimates 7.38 per cent of exports are materially at risk.

Where it really bites hard The export story gets the attention, but India's more fragile flank is its external account, which a tariff shock would hit from two directions at once. Q1 FY27's current account deficit was $4.2 billion, just 0.5 per cent of GDP, but the merchandise trade deficit beneath it widened to $86.1 billion. A tariff-induced export slump would widen that gap mechanically.

Feeding it from the other side: Brent crossed $100 a barrel on September 9, pushing India's own crude basket to $113.91, and India's net oil and gas import bill jumped 36.5 per cent year-on-year to over $60 billion during April-August, 2026. Every $10 rise in Brent is reckoned to shave 20-30 basis points off GDP growth  -  a drag that operates whether or not Washington ever pulls the tariff trigger. The reserve cushion reassures only at a glance.

Reserves hit a lifetime high of $785.7 billion in the week to September 4  -  a record $44.9 billion jump  -  but a large share is not export earnings; it is the RBI's concessional swap window, which pulled in over $136 billion in borrowed foreign-currency deposits since June. The rupee, meanwhile, touched a record low of 96.84 on May 20, and is still hovering above 95 to the dollar. A reserve buffer built substantially on rented dollars is a thinner shock absorber than the headline suggests  - precisely when a tariff-driven export slump would test it.
