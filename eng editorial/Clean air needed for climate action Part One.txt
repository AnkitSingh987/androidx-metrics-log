Title: Clean air needed for climate action Part One

Clean air needed for climate action

The BRICS Summit in New Delhi ended with a declaration that reflects the difficult energy choices facing emerging economies. It calls for a just and equitable energy transition, presses for greater climate finance and access to technology, and acknowledges that fossil fuels will continue to have a place in the energy mix of developing countries.

It also reaffirms the Paris Agreement and argues that national circumstances must shape the route countries take towards lower emissions. There is, however, a curious absence. For all its attention to climate, energy and sustainable development, the declaration does not explicitly address one of the most immediate environmental costs borne by many BRICS economies: air pollution.

Many BRICS countries are trying to solve two problems at once. They need more energy, transport and industrial production as incomes rise, while the health and economic costs of the way that growth is powered are becoming harder to ignore. In South Asia, the numbers are particularly difficult to dismiss.

A new assessment by the UN Environment Programme and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition estimates that air pollution already imposes an economic burden equivalent to about 10 per cent of South Asia's GDP, the highest of any region assessed. The study also calculates that a package of measures tackling air pollution and climate change together could return around Rs 21 in economic and health benefits for every Rs 1 invested in South Asia. The benefit-cost ratio is the second highest among the 15 regions UNEP examined.

This does not mean that a government spends one rupee and gets Rs 21 back. The return comes from losses avoided: lower healthcare costs, fewer missed workdays, higher productivity and less physical damage, alongside harder-to-price gains from avoided illness and premature death. Even excluding those non-market benefits, UNEP finds that measurable market returns at the global level still amount to about four dollars for every dollar spent.

That strengthens the case for early action in developing economies, where governments are decarbonising while still building power systems, cities and industries. Cleaner air delivers benefits now, while climate gains accumulate over time. Cleaner buses, household fuels, industrial standards and power systems can reduce emissions while improving air quality.

UNEP finds that the air-pollution gains are felt early, while the economic benefits from avoided climate damage build over decades. For governments with limited fiscal space, that timing matters. Energy transformation across power, industry and households is therefore especially relevant to the BRICS debate.

By 2035, clean cooking and heating offers the largest return among the measures examined for South Asia, followed by the transformation of power generation and industrial process emission standards. By 2050, industrial process standards, vehicle emission standards and power-sector transformation move ahead. India has experience of what such transitions look like when taken to scale.

The expansion of LPG access under the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana changed household energy use across millions of homes. The growth of solar power changed the scale and economics of renewable-energy deployment. UNEP itself cites India's clean-cooking programme and solar mission as examples of policies that have driven technological and social change.
