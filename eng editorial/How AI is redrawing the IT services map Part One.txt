Title: How AI is redrawing the IT services map Part One

How AI is redrawing the IT services map

IT firms will undergo transformation, but this does not mean the industry will cross the bridge unscathed The pyramid model is gone. With coding agents, we no longer need basic coding - this blunt assessment comes from V Balakrishnan, the former CFO of Infosys. It is more than a prediction about entry-level jobs.

For three decades, Indian IT services built scale by selling engineering capacity, measured largely through hours, utilisation and headcount. If AI can deliver the same work with fewer people, the industry isn't simply becoming more productive. The economics of what it sells are changing. That change is already challenging the foundation of the model.

Indian IT's advantage was never just cheap labour; it also rested on scarce skills. AI now attacks both, cutting the hours many tasks require and reducing the scarcity of the skills behind them. Work that once required a specialist can increasingly be handled by a much larger pool of AI augmented generalists.

When a scarce input becomes abundant, pricing power comes under pressure. All about models Nasscom President Rajesh Nambiar is right that AI may redraw India's IT services map. But the claim that 20 to 25 per cent of firms could struggle to cross over risks making a structural disruption look more bounded than it is.

The harder question is how much of the existing model can survive while its replacement is being built. AI also reduces the opacity around effort, letting clients ask why they're paying for 10 days of work that now takes two. Under hourly billing, that efficiency gain flows to the client as a lower price, not to the vendor as margin.

Pricing towards value delivered is the obvious response, but it shifts delivery risk onto the vendor and demands capabilities the old model never built. AI creates three pressures at once: fewer hours, less scarcity in the skills behind them and a growing client ability to insource what they once bought. The third is the most disruptive since it threatens intermediation itself, not just delivery cost.

Pricing alone cannot fix this: shifting from hours to outcomes only captures value from work that still exists; it doesn't guarantee that work, its pricing power, or the relationship will survive insourcing. Building an outcome-based business demands proprietary IP, domain expertise, customer intimacy and delivery risks the old model never had to carry. "Sell outcomes, not people" is an attractive answer.

It is not a complete one. No future? The old revenue model can weaken before the new one becomes viable: legacy revenue disappears quickly, while proprietary platforms and outcome contracts take years to build. A firm can do everything right and still fail if old revenue disappears faster than its replacement emerges.

Much of what passes for transformation today is automation, not redesign. A copilot writes code, an agent triages tickets and the process gets cheaper, but the organisation and pricing model underneath stay untouched. Redesign changes who owns the outcome and the risk, cannibalising the revenue that built the company's success.

Firms can automate without redesigning, then present one as evidence of the other. Automation buys time. It does not guarantee a future. Underneath sits a structural problem: the classic pyramid (many juniors, fewer seniors, thin leadership) was built to staff hours, not organise accountability for outcomes.
