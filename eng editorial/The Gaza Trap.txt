The most dangerous word in the latest attempt to end the Gaza war may be "first". Israel wants Hamas to disarm before Israeli forces withdraw. Hamas, meanwhile, has tied its willingness to surrender its weapons to an Israeli withdrawal and an end to military action.

Both positions have an internal logic. Together, they create a deadlock that could consume the latest diplomatic initiative before it has properly begun. The problem is not merely that Israel and Hamas disagree. It is that each side regards the other's concession as a potential security catastrophe. For Israel, withdrawing while Hamas retains its military capability could recreate the conditions that led to the October 2023 attack.

For Hamas, disarming while Israeli troops remain in Gaza could amount to surrendering its only meaningful leverage while leaving the territory under military pressure. That makes the sequencing question impossible to resolve through political declarations alone. Someone has to move first, and neither side believes it can safely do so.

This is where the American role becomes crucial. Washington has considerable influence over Israel, but influence is not the same as control. The Israeli prime minister must contend not only with security calculations but also with a domestic political environment in which powerful members of his coalition oppose any withdrawal that could be portrayed as a concession to Hamas.

With elections approaching, the political cost of compromise is potentially enormous. Yet any agreement that depends on Hamas becoming completely disarmed before Israel withdraws risks becoming one with no practical path to implementation. Disarmament is not a switch that can simply be turned off. It involves weapons, command structures, tunnels, fighters and the possibility of clandestine rearmament.

Israel is unlikely to accept assurances from Hamas alone, while Hamas is unlikely to surrender its arsenal on the promise that Israel will subsequently leave. The only realistic way through the impasse is therefore a phased arrangement in which neither side is asked to take the entire risk at once. Disarmament, Israeli redeployment, humanitarian access and reconstruction would have to proceed in linked stages, with credible outside verification and consequences for violations.

That would require the United States and other mediators to do considerably more than announce an agreement: they would have to build an enforcement mechanism capable of surviving the first serious breach. There is also a larger danger. If the disagreement becomes permanent, the Gaza conflict could settle into an unstable halfway state: neither genuine peace nor a decisive war, but continuing Israeli military pressure, an armed Hamas presence and a devastated civilian population caught between them.

That would expose the central weakness of diplomacy built around grand declarations. Peace is not achieved when the parties agree on the destination. It begins when they agree on the sequence by which they can safely get there. In Gaza, that sequence remains unresolved. And until it is, the latest peace initiative is less a settlement than a test of whether diplomacy can overcome mutual distrust.
