Title: A roadblock to tax certainty Part One

A roadblock to tax certainty

A recent address, the finance minister urged the Income Tax Department to implement the new Income Tax Act, 2025, in its "spirit". This implies a drift away from strict literal interpretation, raising a fundamental concern about the rule of law in taxation. While the sentiment may appear pragmatic, it sits uneasily with settled judicial doctrine and risks reopening the door to discretion, inconsistency, and uncertainty - precisely the infirmities modern tax reform seeks to eliminate.

In a system that increasingly relies on self-assessment and voluntary compliance, such uncertainty can significantly alter taxpayer behaviour, risk perceptions, and user cost of capital. Literal interpretation isn't optional, it is foundational The jurisprudence of the Supreme Court on statutory interpretation in tax law is consistent and unambiguous. Where the language of a tax statute is clear, it must be applied as written.

Courts have emphasised that taxation is a matter of strict construction: nothing is to be read in, nothing implied. Both the taxpayer and the administration are entitled to rely on the plain meaning of law  -  and both are equally bound by it. This mutual reliance forms the basis of trust between the State and taxpayers, reinforcing compliance through certainty rather than coercion.

It is only when ambiguity arises  -  when two reasonable interpretations are possible  -  that one may turn to legislative intent or "spirit". Even then, interpretative tools are applied cautiously, often with a bias in favour of the taxpayer. This clarity is the bedrock of fiscal certainty. Any suggestion that "spirit" should override text risks weakening predictability that is essential for compliance.

Where does the 'spirit' reside? If one must search for the "spirit" of a tax law, it lies  -  if anywhere  -  in the collective deliberations of Parliament and, above all, in the statute it enacts. It cannot be located in post-facto executive statements or speeches. To treat ministerial exposition as determinative of legislative intent blurs the line between law-making and administration.

Even parliamentary debates, while occasionally used as aids, are not conclusive; Parliament speaks finally through the text it passes. From certainty to subjectivity: The discretion problem The concern with a "spirit-based" approach lies in its operational consequences. Tax administration is carried out by thousands of officers across jurisdictions.

If each officer is encouraged to interpret the "spirit" of the law, the result is divergence. Such divergence can fragment the uniform application of tax law, undermining both horizontal equity and administrative coherence. One officer's understanding of intent may differ from another's; what is "consistent with the spirit" in one case may be treated differently in another.

Further, unless taxpayers and administrators read the same "spirit" into the law -  a practical impossibility  -  disputes are inevitable. This introduces subjectivity into a domain that demands objectivity, leading to differential treatment of similarly placed taxpayers  -  an outcome that offends equity and fairness. Empathy in tax administration must be institutional, not officer-based.

More troublingly, such discretion creates fertile ground for rent-seeking behaviour. When outcomes depend on interpretative latitude, the scope for negotiation increases. This is precisely the administrative culture that India has attempted to dismantle through automation, faceless assessments, and rule-based systems.

Reintroducing such discretion risks reversing gains achieved through these systemic reforms. A shift to rule-based governance Globally, tax administration has moved towards minimising discretion at the operational level. The emphasis is on clear rules, automated processes, and standardised application.
