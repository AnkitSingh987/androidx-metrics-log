How curriculum, assessment, and policy are reshaping school learning

And policy are reshaping school learning For decades, school education in India and indeed across much of the world was defined by memorisation, compliance, and examination performance. Many of us grew up in classrooms where success meant recalling information accurately and reproducing it under timed conditions. That system served a particular era well.

By 2025, however, it became increasingly clear that it was no longer sufficient. As I visit schools across the country, across boards and geographies, I have begun to notice a quiet but meaningful shift. In some classrooms, students are no longer waiting to be told what to think. They are asking questions, reflecting on feedback, managing their own learning tasks, and engaging more confidently with complex ideas.

Independent learning, once discussed largely as an aspiration, has started to appear as a visible outcome. This change did not occur overnight. It has taken years of experimentation, debate, and courage from educators and school leaders. Yet 2025 feels like a moment of consolidation when several strands finally began to come together.

Curriculum design: Trusting the learner One of the most significant shifts has been in curriculum design. Schools have begun moving away from the narrow question of what to teach towards a more fundamental one: how students learn best. Through my work with initiatives such as School Cinema, I have seen how experiential and reflective learning can unlock deeper understanding.

In one classroom, a short film on empathy led to a discussion on identity, bias, and social responsibility conversations that no textbook chapter had previously provoked. Students drew connections to their own lives and debated solutions with minimal teacher intervention. Globally benchmarked curricula have increasingly institutionalised this approach.

Frameworks such as the IB, Cambridge, and the Western Australian Certificate of Education (WACE) place emphasis on inquiry, exploration, and student ownership. Curriculum design, in this sense, has begun to trust students as active participants in their own learning. Assessment reform: Measuring thinking, not recall Assessment has always shaped behaviour in schools.

When examinations reward memory, teaching gravitates towards memorisation. When assessment values reasoning, application, and reflection, learning begins to change. By 2025, more schools have started moving away from single high-stakes examinations towards a mix of projects, presentations, portfolios, and applied tasks.

The emphasis has shifted from simply arriving at the correct answer to explaining 'why' an answer works. One student once remarked to me, "I'm not anxious about assessments anymore because I understand what I'm being assessed for." That observation captures the essence of assessment reform. International boards laid the groundwork for this transition years ago.

Systems such as WACE have strengthened it through moderated, criterion-referenced assessments that are transparent and consistent across contexts. When assessment becomes predictable and meaningful, anxiety reduces and learning deepens. The changing role of the teacher Another important shift has been in how teachers see their role.

Teachers are no longer expected to be the sole source of knowledge. Increasingly, they are facilitators of learning, listening more, asking better questions, and allowing students to engage with productive struggle. Through TAISI, I interact with educators across the country, and many describe a similar journey.

Letting go of control was not easy, but it enabled students to grow. In WACE-aligned schools, teachers undergo sustained professional development not only in subject knowledge, but also in assessment literacy and facilitation skills. This confidence is visible in classrooms, where teachers support students in planning work, reflecting on feedback, managing time, and gradually taking ownership of learning.
