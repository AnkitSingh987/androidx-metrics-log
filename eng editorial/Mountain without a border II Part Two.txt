Title: Mountain without a border II Part Two

Mountain without a border ~ II

Heavy bridging and earth-moving plant could be pooled and pre-positioned rather than flown in piecemeal after each collapse. And when the water has done its worst, the country it strikes should not be left alone with the bill: a regional reconstructionfinance facility, paid into in the quiet years and drawn upon in the ruinous ones, would spread the cost that now falls, by the accident of where a glacier sat, on whichever economy stood beneath it. Such a fund should teach as well as console ~ its money moving only where the corridor is opened and the new standard met, so that a plant rebuilt to the old drawing draws no second cheque while a village moved off the debris path does.

To all of which the seasoned objection is ready, and it is not a foolish one. You cannot, it runs, build shared institutions across a border that is itself unsettled. Let a foreign team cross the line, even to save lives, and you have conceded something about whose line it is; sit at a table apportioning a common watershed and you have dignified the other man's claim to half of it.

In a region where the boundary is the quarrel, cooperation can look like surrender. The answer is that the compact is built to prejudge precisely nothing, and that neighbours far more hostile than these three have already done exactly this. Watching a glacier together draws no line on any map; a rescue corridor confers no right of way a court could one day cite.

India and Pakistan ran a shared river commission through two wars without settling Kashmir, and opened crossing points along the Line of Control to move earthquake relief in 2005 on the plain understanding that a gate is not a boundary. But those arrangements show why goodwill alone will not do: the flood-season river readings China shares with India were quietly withheld during the standoff of 2017, and a courtesy can always be withdrawn. A treaty obligation, precisely because it is walled off from the weather of the frontier, cannot.

If corps commanders can keep a hotline open without prejudice to sovereignty, dam engineers can surely keep a shared inventory of lakes. None of this need wait on a grand ratification no legislature will grant in a single grieving season. It can begin with memoranda formalising what the rescue is already improvising at the disaster's face, and let the binding treaty be the destination, not the first step; grief opens windows that calmer seasons close, and this one should be used.

Nor is any of it only a matter of foreign policy. The same discipline the compact would demand abroad is the discipline we have postponed at home. To adopt common standards for the high Himalaya is to be forced, at last, to look honestly at what we have already built and are still building in Himachal, in Sikkim, in Jammu and Kashmir.

A relook is not a moratorium dressed as virtue; it is the plain work of re-grading each project against slope failure and debris pulse, not merely against the rainfall year some consultant once used. Some will pass. Some will need redesign. A few should be left on paper. That is not a concession wrung from us by neighbours; it is a reckoning we owe ourselves, and have deferred for as long as the projects kept the lights on.
