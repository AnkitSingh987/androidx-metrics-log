Title: Dont ignore social element in quick commerce Part One

Don't ignore social element in quick commerce

When customers treat delivery workers as an extension of an app rather than individuals, they inadvertently support a system that normalises unsafe conditions The recent parliamentary debate on India's "10-minute delivery" model has highlighted long-standing concerns about the safety and well-being of gig workers, who enable ultrafast commerce. While consumers embrace speed, the pressures created by strict delivery-time guarantees, algorithmic controls, and volatile rating systems often push riders into unsafe working conditions. Although the government has taken necessary policy measures to address the vulnerability of gig workers and formalise the platform economy, we must wait to observe the impact of this new labour policy on the economy.

A key concern emerges: Is the convenience of receiving food or groceries a few minutes faster worth the risks faced by the people who make that speed possible? Social aspect unfolded First, behind every ultrafast delivery is a workforce that remains largely overlooked  -  riders and gig workers navigating traffic, pollution, heat, rain and long hours to keep pace with app-driven expectations. Many spend most of their day on the road with limited safety gear and inconsistent insurance coverage for personal and liability risks.

The model of promising deliveries within a few minutes' compounds the pressure: slowing down at a signal or getting stuck behind a queue can trigger penalties, lower incentives or poor ratings. In such an environment, the temptation  -  or necessity  -  to over-speed or cut corners is built into the job. Second, adding to this strain is the rating and algorithm system that governs gig work.

A single annoyed customer or a delay caused by restaurant bottlenecks can sharply reduce a rider's earnings. Although platforms label workers as "partners," they often lack the protections and stability that even factory workers receive, such as guaranteed income, hazard pay or comprehensive fatality insurance. The rating system, rather than serving as constructive feedback, often operates as a mechanism of control, without offering workers a genuine voice or security.

Third, proponents of quick commerce argue that consumers should have the freedom to choose speed and that innovation should not be constrained. However, societal values must not be neglected to deliver the value of convenience. When customers treat delivery workers as an extension of an app rather than individuals with families and vulnerabilities, they inadvertently support a system that normalises unsafe conditions.

No workplace would be allowed to operate on production targets that undermine basic safety standards, yet similar pressures play out daily on our roads. Policy suggestions As the safety and security of gig workers gain some traction, the policy focus is gradually shifting towards greater accountability. If companies wish to maintain ultrafast delivery promises, they should be expected to prove  - through transparent data and enforceable safeguards  -  that these targets can be met without incentivising risky driving.

So, policy measures are worth considering that include reasonable limits on delivery-time guarantees, base earnings that are not tied to volatile ratings, universal health and accidental insurance, and clear liability for platform owners or aggregators when algorithmic pressures lead to unsafe behaviour. Second, the scale and speed of ultrafast commerce are often discussed together, but their adverse effects on the social and environmental dimensions of sustainability are evident from industry reports that raise concerns about worker disposable income, safety, and exposure risk. Forty per cent of gig workers earn below the ₹30,000 benchmark, driving 30 per cent turnover and risk-taking for incentives.
