Title: Administrative records can reshape policy Part Two

Administrative records can reshape policy

India's large informal workforce, for example, leaves only a partial administrative footprint. Similarly, healthcare databases record those who access services, not necessarily those who need them. Without careful validation, administrative data can reinforce existing blind spots rather than eliminate them.

The challenge, therefore, is not simply technical but institutional. Linking databases across departments requires common identifiers, shared metadata standards and robust privacy safeguards. Equally important is public trust. Citizens must be confident that information shared for one public purpose will not be misused for another.

In this regard, MoSPI has also given directions on data harmonisation. MoSPI is actively handling the institutional gap, such as listing unique identifiers, international and national classifications/standards among other efforts. International experience offers useful lessons. Estonia's X-Road platform demonstrates how government agencies can securely exchange information while ensuring citizens provide the same information only once.

Rather than replicating this model, India should adapt its underlying principles to its own federal governance structure, building an interoperable, privacy-preserving data exchange architecture that enables departments to work together while maintaining clear accountability. The future of official statistics is not a choice between surveys and administrative data. Surveys will continue to provide representative estimates and measure dimensions that administrative systems cannot.

The role of surveys, however, should increasingly shift towards validating administrative records, identifying coverage gaps and correcting systemic biases rather than repeatedly collecting information that governments already possess. India does not suffer from a shortage of data. It suffers from disconnected data.

The next frontier of evidence-based policymaking lies not in collecting more information, but in connecting the information already available through secure, interoperable and trusted public sources. That is how administrative data can become not just a record of governance, but a foundation for smarter governance. By MEHAKK BHARADWAJ (The writer is an Embark India Development Fellow at the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation.

The views are personal.)
