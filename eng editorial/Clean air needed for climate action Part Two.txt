Title: Clean air needed for climate action Part Two

Clean air needed for climate action

But India's next set of choices will have to be more local. Pollution profiles differ sharply from one state to another, and within states. What makes sense in an industrial district may be quite different from what is needed in an agricultural belt, a large city or a Himalayan state. National policy can set standards and provide finance; the choice of what to tackle first will often have to be made at the subnational level.

Some states have begun moving in this direction. Punjab has examined non-CO2 pollutants alongside its longer-term climate pathway, bringing methane, black carbon and ozone into a debate that has often centred on carbon dioxide. Haryana faces a related mix of agricultural, transport and urban sources within the wider Indo-Gangetic airshed.

In Himalayan states such as Himachal Pradesh, warming and air pollution play out against fragile ecosystems and different patterns of energy use, transport and development. Madhya Pradesh offers a particularly useful example of how granular this exercise needs to be. Indore, Bhopal and Jabalpur have performed strongly in recent Swachh Vayu Sarvekshan rankings.

But the state's pollution challenge extends well beyond its better-performing cities. Its recent Non-CO2 Emission Reduction Pathways for Madhya Pradesh maps emissions across all 55 districts, identifying where livestock, industry, power generation, transport and other sources dominate. The learning is that a state should not spend the same rupee in the same way everywhere.

Industrial areas may need cleaner fuels and tighter emission standards; elsewhere, the priorities may be transport, household energy, agriculture or waste. That variation also makes coordination harder. The levers span transport, municipal administration, power, agriculture, petroleum and pollution-control agencies, while the pollution is omnipresent.

Across the measures examined by UNEP, average implementation delays are estimated at roughly 7.5 to eight years. Institutional barriers are the largest single contributor, ahead of technical, economic and so cial constraints. Weak coordination, limited enforcement capacity and fragmented decision-making can turn policies already available on paper into benefits that arrive years late.

Achieving national air-quality standards on schedule from 2026 is estimated to reduce cumulative PM2.5 exposure by about 20 per cent by 2040. Introduce an eight-year delay and the reduction falls to 10 per cent. Half the gain disappears. A pollution-control system installed years late cannot clean the air people have already breathed.

Cleaner buses purchased in the next decade cannot restore school days and working days already lost. Later action cannot undo illnesses that develop while implementation stalled. The next phase of India's clean-air policy should account for time more explicitly. Government programmes already track expenditure, measures undertaken and changes in pollutant concentrations.

They should also ask how quickly an intervention reduces exposure and who is responsible when delivery slips. In South Asia, where the economic burden of pollution is already enormous, the value of an intervention depends on where it is deployed, what it tackles and how quickly its benefits reach people. For countries still building their economies, the speed at which cleaner air arrives is itself an economic outcome.

By NEHAL SHARMA (The writer is Lead, Subnational and Arctic Program, Gateway Research Institute)
