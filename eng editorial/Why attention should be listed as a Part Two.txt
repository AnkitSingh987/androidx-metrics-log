Why attention should be listed as a competency in higher education

Not all 20. Two or three. This is where 'Student Development Programmes' should begin and usually don't. Most "time management" modules hand out a template and a motivational quote. An audit produces personal data, and personal data persuades where exhortation cannot. A student who discovers she checked her lock screen 60 times before lunch has learned something no seminar could teach her.

A three-week arc any counselling unit could run: a tally sheet in week one, no interventions, just counting; three subtractions in week two, such as non-essential notifications off, social apps off the home screen and perhaps deletion of a few messages at two fixed windows; in week three a "Daily Card" naming one protected block, one first task, one boundary. Even ignored notifications impose a control cost. Latest research on attention residue suggests roughly 23 minutes to return to deep focus after a single interruption.

For students preparing for NEET, JEE, CUET, CAT or UPSC, this is not wellness fluff. It is exam strategy. An attentional environment For faculty and those who teach, an uncomfortable question: how much of the distraction in our classrooms is manufactured by our own design choices? A slide with 42 words, three animations, and a stock photograph is not a teaching aid; it is a competing bid for attention.

Marzouki's point about clutter applies here too: each visible element competes for perceptual processing and invites unnecessary attentional decisions even when no action is taken. The lecture hall is a visual field, and most of ours are cluttered. Given the same, a few solutions are: can we monotask the segment, i.e., one idea per 15-20 minutes, one visual, one question, and then a deliberate two-minute transition?

The pause is not lost time; it clears attentional residue. Second, build friction, not resistance. A phone tray at the door works better than a lecture on discipline for the same reason that a speed breaker works better than a signboard. Third, teach with constraint. Ask for a 50‑word abstract instead of a 500‑word essay.

Constraint reduces decision load while preserving meaning making. Good artists know this: a panel has borders, and the borders are why you look. Worth saying plainly: attention is a teachable outcome. We assess it in every examination hall and teach it in none of them. Attention as infrastructure Now the part that heads of educational institutions, deans and IQAC coordinators will find least comfortable because it implicates calendars.

Cancelling one recurring low-value meeting and protecting a daily focus block is, in a university, a governance decision rather than a personal one. Most institutions have a meeting culture that fragments the working day of exactly those people whose deep work, such as research, curriculum design, and mentoring, determines ranking in NIRF, QS and THE WUR along with a NAAC grade. The solutions can be: First, meeting hygiene as policy, such as no agenda, no meeting.

Thus, no recurring meeting survives two terms without justification. Alternatively, a protected institution-wide no-meetings window of a fortnight. This costs nothing and returns hours. A university that cannot say when its scholars think has not scheduled a calendar; it has scheduled an interruption.

Second, a procurement filter. Before the next portal or app: Is it essential? Is it durable? Will it simplify upkeep? Will it reduce attention load rather than add to it? Our campuses accumulate ERP modules and WhatsApp groups the way a household accumulates plastic containers and each one a login, a notification, a decision point, long after the original choice is forgotten.
