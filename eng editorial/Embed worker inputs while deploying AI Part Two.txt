Title: Embed worker inputs while deploying AI Part Two

Embed worker inputs while deploying AI

Too often, unions wait for management announcements before mobilising, positioning themselves as defenders against change rather than shapers of it. This reactive stance reinforces perceptions that unions oppose progress rather than guide it responsibly. Progressive unions like IG Metall show the alternative: developing expertise before crises emerge, proposing alternative strategies, negotiating from knowledge rather than fear.

Union accountability matters. Members deserve leadership that equips them with skills and understanding, not just grievance procedures. They need representatives who distinguish between genuine threats requiring resistance and transformations requiring adaptation. This requires serious investment in building AI expertise  -  training union officials, establishing research capabilities, creating academic partnerships.

Empty statements about "responsible AI" carry no weight without technical understanding to back them up. Unions must make their commitment visible: publishing AI readiness programmes, showcasing successful negotiations, documenting learning journeys, sharing best practices. The question for unions is stark: Do they want a seat at the AI table or to be spectators complaining from the sidelines?

Companies will implement AI regardless. The only question is whether unions will have the knowledge, credibility, and proactive engagement to shape implementation in workers' interests. The path forward The solution requires parallel commitments. Companies must invest in comprehensive training programmes, create genuine dialogue channels, and incorporate union perspectives into AI governance.

But this investment comes with expectations: unions must demonstrate equal commitment through capacity building, proactive engagement, and visible leadership on technological change. The most successful partnerships hold both parties accountable. Companies provide transparency and training; unions respond with expertise and constructive engagement.

Companies involve unions in AI design; unions contribute operational knowledge and alternative strategies. Both acknowledge AI's risks and work together on mitigation; both recognise AI's opportunities and collaborate on maximising benefits for workers and business alike. Walking the talk AI transformation isn't optional  -  it's inevitable.

The only question is whether it happens through collaboration or confrontation, with worker knowledge included or excluded, with unions as partners or bystanders. For companies, training union leaders builds better labour relations, superior AI systems grounded in operational reality, and competitive advantage through workforce engagement. The industrial engineering lesson remains true: to understand how something really works, ask the operators.

For unions, rhetoric must match action. Members need representatives who navigate change, not just resist it. Workers need unions building AI literacy, not just AI anxiety. The labour movement needs leaders who earn their seat through visible expertise and proactive engagement. In a world where unions are resurging and AI is inevitable; this isn't just about good labour relations  -  it's about whether both sides will finally walk the talk.

The smart money is on partnership, but only if actions match words. By Prabir Kumar Bandyopadhyay The writer is a retired Management Professor and Independent Researcher
