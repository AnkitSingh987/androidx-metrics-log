Title: Agrarian distress and policy myopia

Category: Editorial

The most crippling structural flaw in Indian agriculture is the abysmal lack of scale, driven by the relentless, multi-generational fragmentation of landholdings. The overwhelming majority of Indian farmers are classified as "small and marginal," attempting to eke out a living on plots smaller than two hectares. This microscopic scale of operation renders agriculture inherently unviable as a primary source of income. It precludes the adoption of modern, mechanized farming techniques, prevents meaningful capital accumulation, and completely obliterates the bargaining power of the farmer in the marketplace. The smallholder farmer is trapped in a vicious cycle: too small to negotiate favorable prices for inputs like seeds and fertilizers, and too desperate for immediate cash to negotiate fair prices for their output, effectively abandoning them to the predatory practices of local middlemen and commissioned agents.

This fundamental vulnerability is severely exacerbated by a highly distorted, state-managed marketing architecture. The Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) system, originally designed to protect farmers from exploitation by ensuring transparent auctions, has largely degenerated into a cartelized, opaque monopoly. In many states, farmers are legally compelled to sell their produce exclusively through these designated mandis, where collusive practices by licensed traders systematically depress farm-gate prices. Furthermore, the Essential Commodities Act, a draconian relic of an era of chronic food shortages, empowers the government to arbitrarily impose stock limits and ban exports at the slightest hint of retail inflation. While these blunt interventions occasionally benefit the urban consumer, they violently disrupt the agricultural supply chain, penalizing farmers by artificially crashing prices precisely when they anticipate a profitable harvest. The state essentially forces the farmer to subsidize the urban consumer's inflation mandate, destroying any incentive for agricultural capital formation.

Moreover, the entire edifice of agricultural policy is overwhelmingly, and ruinously, focused on the front-end subsidies for the production of two specific crops: rice and wheat. The mechanism of Minimum Support Prices (MSP) backed by open-ended procurement by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) has created a deeply distorted incentive structure. Farmers in states like Punjab and Haryana are heavily incentivized to cultivate immensely water-guzzling crops, leading to the catastrophic depletion of groundwater tables, the destruction of soil health through excessive fertilizer application, and a massive, unmanageable surplus of cereals rotting in government godowns. Simultaneously, this procurement bias starves the cultivation of climate-resilient, nutritionally superior, and highly profitable crops like pulses, oilseeds, and horticulture, forcing India to spend billions of dollars importing edible oils while suffocating under a mountain of unwanted wheat.

The political response to this deepening crisis has been universally characterized by short-term expediency. Successive governments, desperate to pacify the rural electorate before elections, invariably resort to the blunt, destructive instrument of massive farm loan waivers. These waivers are a spectacular macroeconomic disaster. They systematically destroy the credit culture in rural India, penalizing the honest farmer who repays their loans while severely impairing the balance sheets of public sector banks. More tragically, loan waivers offer absolutely no relief to the most desperate, marginalized tenant farmers and landless laborers who are excluded from the formal banking sector and remain hopelessly indebted to usurious village moneylenders. They treat the symptom of indebtedness without addressing the underlying disease of unviable agricultural incomes.

In conclusion, the resolution of India’s agrarian distress demands a complete departure from the prevailing paradigm of subsidies, state-mandated monopolies, and reactive loan waivers. The sector requires massive, sustained capital investment in post-harvest infrastructure—cold chains, modern warehousing, and food processing—to reduce catastrophic wastage and capture higher value. The government must aggressively facilitate the aggregation of small landholdings through formalized land leasing frameworks and the robust promotion of Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs), enabling scale without transferring ownership. Crucially, the regulatory shackles on agricultural marketing must be permanently dismantled, allowing farmers the absolute freedom to sell their produce to anyone, anywhere, while establishing a massive, decentralized digital market. Until policymakers possess the political courage to treat the farmer as a rational economic actor rather than a perpetual ward of the state, the Indian agricultural sector will remain a vast, tragic landscape of unrealized potential and profound human suffering.
