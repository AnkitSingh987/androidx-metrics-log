Title: Why the rupee has fallen steeply

Category: Editorial

The undisputed primary catalyst for the Rupee's precipitous decline is the aggressively hawkish monetary policy stance adopted by the United States Federal Reserve. In its desperate bid to rein in domestic inflation that has reached multi-decade highs, the Fed has embarked on one of the most aggressive interest rate hiking cycles in modern history. This sustained monetary tightening has profoundly altered the global calculus of risk and reward. As interest rates in the US rise, the yield on dollar-denominated assets, particularly US Treasury bonds, becomes immensely attractive. This triggers a massive, systemic rebalancing of global capital portfolios, as foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) ruthlessly liquidate their holdings in emerging markets, including India, to repatriate funds to the perceived safety and higher yields of the US market. This sustained and massive capital flight, characterized by the unabated selling of Indian equities and debt, creates an overwhelming demand for US Dollars and a corresponding oversupply of Rupees, relentlessly driving down the exchange rate.

This immense pressure from capital outflows is severely compounded by the deterioration in India’s fundamental external sector metrics, specifically the widening of the Current Account Deficit (CAD). India is structurally dependent on imported commodities, most critically crude oil, natural gas, and edible oils, to sustain its domestic economic engine. The geopolitical turbulence triggered by the conflict in Eastern Europe has violently disrupted global commodity supply chains, precipitating a massive surge in energy and food prices. Consequently, India's import bill has ballooned astronomically, far outpacing the growth in its export earnings. When a nation must expend significantly more foreign exchange to purchase essential imports than it earns from its exports, it creates a structural deficit of foreign currency. The widening CAD acts as a persistent, fundamental drag on the Rupee, as importers scramble to purchase expensive Dollars, further exacerbating the supply-demand imbalance in the currency markets.

Furthermore, the strength of the US Dollar itself acts as a massive gravitational force, pulling down almost all emerging market currencies. The Dollar Index, which measures the greenback's value against a basket of major global currencies, has surged to historic highs, driven by its status as the ultimate global safe-haven asset during times of extreme geopolitical uncertainty and macroeconomic distress. It is crucial to recognize that the Rupee’s fall is not an isolated phenomenon; it is depreciating in tandem with the Euro, the British Pound, and the Japanese Yen. The narrative of the Rupee's decline must be contextualized within the reality of an overwhelmingly strong, almost predatory, US Dollar, which acts as a wrecking ball across global currency markets.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) operates within an inherently constrained environment when attempting to arrest this steep depreciation. The central bank has actively intervened in the foreign exchange market, selling billions of dollars from its substantial, yet finite, foreign exchange reserves to provide liquidity, absorb the excess demand, and smooth out excessive volatility. However, the RBI cannot indefinitely defy fundamental macroeconomic gravity. In an environment of persistent capital flight and a ballooning current account deficit, aggressively defending a specific exchange rate target is an exercise in futility that would result in the rapid and dangerous depletion of strategic reserves. The RBI must perform a delicate balancing act, intervening to prevent a chaotic freefall while allowing the currency to gradually depreciate to reflect its true, underlying fundamental value.

Moreover, the depreciation of the Rupee presents a complex, double-edged sword for the domestic economy. While a weaker currency theoretically enhances the competitiveness of Indian exports on the global market, the benefits are often neutralized by the sheer scale of the imported inflation. As the Rupee falls, the domestic cost of all imported goods, particularly fuel and fertilizers, surges dramatically. This imported inflation rapidly permeates the domestic supply chain, elevating manufacturing costs, eroding corporate margins, and squeezing household budgets. The resulting inflationary spiral forces the RBI to tighten domestic monetary policy, raising interest rates to suppress demand, which in turn jeopardizes the broader trajectory of economic growth.

In conclusion, the steep fall of the Indian Rupee is a symptom of a highly turbulent and unforgiving global macroeconomic environment. The currency is caught in a powerful undertow generated by aggressive US monetary tightening, massive capital flight from emerging markets, and a structurally inflated import bill driven by soaring global commodity prices. While the RBI can deploy its reserves to mitigate extreme volatility, it cannot alter these fundamental global dynamics. The trajectory of the Rupee will ultimately be determined not by localized interventions, but by the eventual stabilization of global inflation, a moderation in the US Federal Reserve's hawkish trajectory, and India's ability to structurally address its deep-seated reliance on imported energy.
