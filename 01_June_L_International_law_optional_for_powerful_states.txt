Title: International law optional for powerful states

Category: Editorial

The most glaring manifestation of this hypocrisy is the operational paralysis of the United Nations Security Council (UNSC), the apex body entrusted with the maintenance of international peace and security. The veto power granted to the five permanent members (P5)—the United States, Russia, China, the United Kingdom, and France—was originally intended as a pragmatic mechanism to ensure that the UN would not act against the core interests of the major powers, thereby preventing the collapse of the institution. However, this privilege has morphed into a shield for impunity. When a P5 member, or one of its vital strategic proxies, engages in acts of aggression, territorial annexation, or gross violations of international humanitarian law, the UNSC is inevitably paralyzed by the deployment, or the mere threat, of the veto. The architecture designed to enforce international law thus becomes the very mechanism that guarantees its subversion by the powerful.

The United States, often self-characterized as the principal architect and guarantor of the rules-based order, frequently exemplifies this instrumentalist approach to international law. The 2003 invasion of Iraq, executed without a clear UNSC mandate and predicated on deeply flawed intelligence regarding weapons of mass destruction, stands as a colossal violation of the UN Charter's prohibition on the use of force. More recently, Washington’s refusal to ratify the Rome Statute establishing the International Criminal Court (ICC), coupled with aggressive actions to actively undermine the court's jurisdiction when investigating alleged American war crimes, starkly demonstrates a refusal to submit to the very legal accountability it demands of others. Furthermore, the expansive interpretation of the doctrine of pre-emptive self-defense, utilized to justify targeted assassinations and drone strikes across sovereign borders, effectively rewrites the parameters of international law to suit American strategic imperatives.

Similarly, the Russian Federation's brazen annexation of Crimea and its subsequent full-scale invasion of Ukraine represent a catastrophic repudiation of the foundational principles of territorial integrity and political independence enshrined in international law. Moscow’s actions are accompanied by a cynical manipulation of legal concepts, such as the invocation of the "responsibility to protect" (R2P) or the fabrication of historical claims, to provide a veneer of legality to naked aggression. The inability of the international community to effectively constrain Russian aggression, beyond the imposition of economic sanctions, underscores the profound limitations of international law when confronted with a nuclear-armed power determined to alter the geopolitical status quo through force.

The People's Republic of China exhibits a similar selective engagement with the international legal regime, particularly in its maritime periphery. Beijing's expansive territorial claims in the South China Sea, codified in the ambiguous "nine-dash line," blatantly contravene the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). When the Permanent Court of Arbitration at The Hague issued a definitive ruling in 2016 invalidating these claims, China summarily dismissed the verdict as "a piece of waste paper," demonstrating a complete disregard for international arbitration when it conflicts with its core strategic objectives. China's approach suggests a vision of a hierarchical regional order where historical claims and economic leverage supersede the constraints of multilateral legal frameworks.

This systemic double standard has profound consequences for the global south and smaller states, who are the primary victims of this bifurcated legal system. When international law is perceived not as a shield for the weak, but as a sword wielded selectively by the powerful, it generates profound cynicism and erodes the normative consensus required for global cooperation. This perceived illegitimacy encourages middle powers and emerging economies to adopt highly transactional foreign policies, prioritizing narrow national interests and bilateral alliances over adherence to weakened multilateral institutions. The erosion of the rules-based order thus accelerates a return to a Hobbesian international environment, characterized by spheres of influence, coercive diplomacy, and an increased propensity for armed conflict.

In conclusion, the assertion that international law is 'optional' for powerful states is not merely a cynical observation, but an empirical reality that threatens the stability of the global system. The rules-based order cannot survive if its foundational principles are reduced to rhetorical devices, deployed selectively to chastise rivals while remaining entirely inapplicable to the architects of the system. Reforming this broken architecture requires a fundamental democratization of global governance institutions, primarily the reform of the UNSC veto system, and a renewed commitment by all states to the principle of universal accountability. Until the powerful are willing to subjugate their immediate strategic imperatives to the constraints of international law, the international system will remain characterized by structural injustice, where the strong do what they can, and the weak suffer what they must.
