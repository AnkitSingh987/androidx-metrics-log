Title: Permanence of life insurance Part Two

Permanence of life insurance

An Independent Expert, approved by the regulator, reports on its effect on policyholders, who are notified and may be heard. The court's concern is not corporate convenience but whether any class of policyholder would be worse off. In the US, state guaranty associations catch the policies of a member life insurer that fails: the surviving insurers are assessed to fund covered claims.

The cover is not unlimited and limits vary by state, but the obligation does not vanish with the company. India in practice India has lived this at its most dramatic scale. The nationalising ordinance of January 19, 1956, brought in 245 entities  -  Indian insurers, non-Indian insurers and provident societies.

They were not shut down and replaced. Their life business, with its policies and liabilities, vested in the Life Insurance Corporation when it was formed on September 1 that year. Policyholders who had bought contracts from companies whose names they knew woke up as LIC policyholders. They may not have noticed.

That was precisely the point. HDFC Life describes its October 14, 2022, merger with Exide Life as the first M&A transaction in the sector. It came in two stages: the acquisition completed on January 1, 2022, then the amalgamation, sanctioned by NCLT Mumbai and approved by IRDAI. By the company's account the whole transaction, from the September 2021 announcement, took under 14 months.

The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025, in force since February 5, 2026, extends the discipline into new territory, bringing mergers between insurers and non-insurance companies expressly within IRDAI's prior approval. It also raises the FDI limit to 100 per cent, increasing ownership complexity and making the architecture that protects the contract within it more critical, not less. One policy, 75 years The clearest proof either of us has seen came from one author's service in Kenya, where he processed a claim on a whole-life policy issued by the Nairobi branch of the Oriental Government Security Life Assurance Company in the late 1930s.

From the records available to us, the policy ran through India's Independence in 1947 and Kenya's in 1963 without interruption. When LIC was formed in 1956, Oriental's obligations passed to its East Africa Division. In 1978, when Kenya required foreign insurers to incorporate locally, the Indian insurers there merged into Kenindia Assurance Company Ltd, born in Nairobi that December, and the portfolio passed to it.

The claim was settled at Kenindia in 2013  -  some 75 years after the policy was written, across the independence of two nations, one nationalisation, a change of sovereign law and three institutional transfers. Not one of those discontinuities reduced the obligation. The contract simply continued, as contracts must be in systems built to keep them.

That is what this architecture exists for: not the protection of companies, but the protection of promises.
