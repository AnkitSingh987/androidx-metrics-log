Title: Dollar deluge options for liquidity Part Two

Dollar deluge: options for liquidity management

As the situation is exceptional, the RBI has the option to temporarily suspend daily fine-tuning, widen the corridor by reducing SDF rate to 3.5 per cent. This will not only activate defunct fixed rate reverse repo but also encourage banks to invest these funds in G-secs until demand for credit picks up. As a result, the benchmark yield may remain under 7 per cent.

The RBI may have to tolerate the call rate softening below the repo rate for a while. Banks, reluctant to participate in long-term VRRR or refraining from investment in G-secs, must bear the loss compared to interest offered on deposits. Option for a hike in CRR does not exist. While CRR is exempted on FNNR(B) deposit, a system-wide increase in CRR would be blunt and could affect banks unrelated to the FCNR(B) inflows.

A temporary incremental CRR on the additional deposits mobilised under the swap scheme could violate the very exemption given to such deposits. The OMO (open market operations) sales can absorb liquidity more durably, but large government bond sales can push yields higher and raise borrowing costs. The RBI has gone for such option and announced ₹1 trillion OMO sales during September 2026.

This is not enough, given the size of liquidity surplus. India also has a history of selling G-secs under Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) to deal with large and persistent liquidity arising from capital inflows. On a burden sharing principle, the government should agree as the RBI has already absorbed costs related to swap and VRRR.

Outright forex interventions or FX sell-buy swaps offer another route, allowing the RBI to manage foreign exchange positions and rupee liquidity better. Banks may not buy dollars as their NOP (net open position) is limited to $100 million only. However, banks can buy it on behalf of their customers like oil marketing companies, without violating the NOP guidelines.

The larger point is that absorbing a surplus of this size involves trade-offs. Every instrument has a cost, whether it is paid by the RBI, banks, government, or the bond market. The real choice is which cost is easier to absorb. The RBI should therefore not try to sterilise every rupee simply because it has entered the system.

Some liquidity will naturally move into credit and investments sooner or later. The first task should be to determine how much of the surplus is temporary and how much is likely to persist. In the near term, a combination of 14-day VRRR and SDF can manage the immediate surplus, supplemented by longer-tenor VRRR.

If a sizeable portion proves durable, OMO sales, sell-buy forex swap, and eventually G-sec sales under MSS could come into play. By Barendra Kumar Bhoi & Vani Nair Bhoi is Chief Economic Adviser, and Nair is Economic Research Analyst, AU Small Finance Bank. Views are personal
