Title: Russia calculus

Russia calculus

Delhi's diplomacy with Moscow affirms strategic autonomy The just-concluded BRICS summit was not just about the Russia-China-led pushback against unilateral tariffs and sanctions by the US and the demand for expansion of multilateral institutions. A key highlight was the India-Russia engagement as a parallel development. In the run-up to the summit, Russia's flagship industrial trade fair, INNOPROM India, was held in Delhi where trade ministers from both countries held extensive dialogue.

This was followed by President Vladimir Putin's unusually strong emphasis on trade, investment, payments, technology and connectivity in a bilateral meeting with Prime Minister Narendra Modi. This marks a shift from a decade ago, when the political and military relationship between the two countries outweighed the economic one. Trade has since risen to nearly $70 billion, with both sides targeting $100 billion by 2030.

Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine triggered Western sanctions that forced Moscow to redirect its energy exports. Indian refiners seized the opportunity to buy discounted crude. The upheaval in the Middle East has reinforced Russia's role as an oil supplier. India need not be apologetic about buying Russian oil so long as the trade stays economically viable.

Simultaneously, it should push for greater Russian purchases of pharmaceuticals and engineering goods as the trade deficit has ballooned (a $50 billion trade deficit against India). India should also pitch for Russian investment. It is significant that the $100 billion trade target comes with a $50 billion two-way investment ambition.

The proposed Su-57 fighter offer, with local production and technology transfer, signals deeper industrial ties rather than simple defence purchases. This links up with Russia's emphasis on BRICS payment systems. Putin's pitch for BRICS to become more practical on payments, investment, and technology is aimed at creating economic room outside US systems.

India need not share Moscow's idea of BRICS as a counterweight to the West to recognise the utility of such a platform. Its strategic autonomy remains relevant. Washington may like to see international relations through a binary lens, but India does not have to be either with the US or against it. India can work on deepening ties with the US, while continuing to buy Russian oil, maintaining defence cooperation with Moscow or working with Russia within BRICS and the SCO.

After all, the US maintains vast commercial ties with China even as it names Beijing a strategic rival. However, this assertion of geopolitical pragmatism has not gone down well in the US. Barely 48 hours after the BRICS summit, the Lindsey Graham Bill was again in focus in Washington, with the proposed legislation carrying the possibility of tariffs of up to 100 per cent on countries buying Russian energy.

Washington's discomfort cannot override India's interests. Yet, 'multi-aligned' India is unlikely to run with an anti-Western bloc  -  even as it cannot easily accept its choices being constrained by Washington.
