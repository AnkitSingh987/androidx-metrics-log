Title: When celebration pollutes the night Part One

When celebration pollutes the night

While these remain critical concerns, an equally important but largely ignored form of environmental degradation is silently spreading across both rural and urban landscapes - the degradation of environmental aesthetics.

Whenever I reflect on my childhood in the village, I realise that one of the greatest luxuries we possessed was not electricity, but the enchanting beauty of the night. Artificial lighting was almost absent, except for the faint glow of kerosene lamps inside a few houses. After dinner, a handful of people, particularly service holders and educated villagers, would gather near a house to exchange their day's thoughts.

Students quietly studied under kerosene lamps, conscious that fuel was precious, while most families retired early and rose with the dawn. The night belonged to nature, families and quiet reflection. The sky seemed immense, filled with stars; the sounds of crickets, frogs, birds and rustling leaves created a natural symphony.

There was little incentive to compete with neighbours in displaying wealth. Education, character, hard work and relationships commanded greater respect than extravagance. These memories make me wonder whether, in our pursuit of modernity, we have forgotten the value of darkness itself. Today, the village night is changing rapidly.

Powerful floodlights, laser beams, giant LED screens and high-powered loudspeakers can transform social and religious functions into spectacular displays. A celebration that once brought people together can become a public demonstration of prosperity. A few decorative lamps may create warmth and beauty; but when hundreds of lights, elaborate structures, extravagant feasts and powerful sound systems are installed primarily to attract attention, consumption acquires another purpose: status signaling.

This is what economists describe as conspicuous consumption -  spending not merely for usefulness or comfort, but also to demonstrate wealth and social standing. The problem becomes more complicated when positional competition enters the picture. One family's extravagant celebration becomes another family's benchmark: If they can do this, why can't we?

The next celebration must then be brighter, louder or more elaborate. Gradually, expenditure itself becomes a measure of prestige. Whether wealth is earned legitimately or otherwise, conspicuous display can create a social expectation that visible expenditure is a measure of success. The environmental consequences of this competition are rarely considered.

As celebrations become more spectacular, natural darkness disappears beneath artificial illumination and the natural soundscape is overwhelmed by amplified music. Stars become difficult to see, sleep is disturbed and the quiet ecological life of the night is disrupted. Nocturnal insects, birds, bats and other organisms depend upon darkness and natural rhythms; excessive artificial light and noise can interfere with these relationships.

What disappears, therefore, is not simply darkness but an entire environmental experience  -  the visual beauty of the night, its natural sounds, tranquility and ecological character. There is also a cultural and educational dimension . Traditional village celebrations often reflected local craftsmanship, folk music, earthen lamps and community participation.

Increasingly, commercially rented lighting, electronic entertainment and visual extravagance can make one celebration resemble another, gradually weakening local distinctiveness. More importantly, children learn from what society admires. If they repeatedly see wealth displayed through spectacular celebrations receiving greater admiration than knowledge, simplicity or service, they may absorb the unfortunate message that success is something to be displayed rather than something to be earned.
