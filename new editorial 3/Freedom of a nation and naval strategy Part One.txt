Title: Freedom of a nation and naval strategy Part One

Freedom of a nation and naval strategy

India has held the sea before, decisively and on its own terms, and knows precisely what it takes to do so again These past few turbulent months have seen a quiet change in the language of reportage of strategic conversations. Chokepoints, sea lanes, freedom of navigation, and other such terms had been confined to professional military education and naval staff colleges. They now appear in business bulletins and prime-time panels.

The Strait of Hormuz, shuttered by Israeli and U.S. strikes in February, and the Houthi blockade against Saudi Arabia declared on July 20 have done what decades of naval commentary could not. They have made the ocean's chokepoints a matter of arm-chair commentary and coffee-table chats. There is a quiet irony in this.

The vocabulary the world is only now acquiring was written into Indian naval strategic lexicon some decades ago, in the plainest possible terms. In 2007, the Indian Navy published an unclassified document, titled "Freedom to use the seas: India's maritime military strategy". Its founding premise is "the freedom to use the seas for our national purposes, under all circumstances".

That was not a slogan reaching for effect. It was a doctrine, written nearly two decades before Hormuz made the phrase popular. As India enters its 80th year of Independence, that gap between what the Navy has long understood and what the wider public is only now discovering is worth a reflective pause.

It points to a truth that rarely gets told in full: the freedom of this nation has always been, first and last, the freedom of its ocean. Not a metaphor stretched to fit the occasion, but the literal, load-bearing fact of how India has fed itself, traded, defended its shores, and projected its confidence into the world. A nation's sovereignty and its command of the sea lanes that sustain it are not two stories running in parallel.

They are the same story. A maritime nation August 15 is conventionally narrated as a land story of borders drawn, provinces divided, a line struck across Punjab and Bengal by a man who had never visited India before. It is a fair telling, but a partial one, and it has quietly crowded out a richer inheritance.

It drowns out the fact that India's oldest and most enduring claim to greatness has been maritime, not territorial. K.M. Panikkar made the sharper version of this argument in India and the Indian Ocean (1945): India's subjugation was not fundamentally a story of armies overrun on land, but of a civilisation that had, over centuries, ceded command of the sea to whoever chose to contest it.

The colonial period did not begin with a land invasion. It began with ships arriving at ports India could no longer control. The corollary is the one worth dwelling on as the nation marked the Independence Day of 2026 when India has been most itself, most prosperous, most confident, and most outward-facing than ever.

It is because the nation held the sea, not merely the land beneath its feet. A power worth naming properly Before it was lost, Indian maritime prowess was formidable, and this is worth stating plainly rather than in passing. The Satavahanas built sustained prosperity on Indo-Roman trade running through Malabar's ports.
