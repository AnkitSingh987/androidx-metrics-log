Title: Freedom of a nation and naval strategy Part Two

Freedom of a nation and naval strategy

The Cholas, at their height, fielded naval forces that reached into Southeast Asia, not for territorial conquest, in Rajendra Chola's 11th-century expedition against Srivijaya, but to keep the sea lanes free of piracy and to project the authority of an Indian power across two oceans. Chinese chronicles of the Song dynasty record embassies from the Chola kings, a reminder that Indian sea power once commanded recognition as far as the courts of East Asia. By the 12th century, a quarter of the world's economic output is estimated to have run through Indian shores.

This was a maritime economy, built and held by Indian hands, centuries before any European fleet entered these waters. That command did not decay for want of skill or ambition. State patronage for shipbuilding receded, and religious injunctions against crossing the ocean took hold in parts of the subcontinent, discouraging exactly the seafaring that had built Chola and Satavahana wealth.

Into the vacuum stepped Arab and Chinese traders, and later European fleets carrying no such inhibitions. Instead of a pseudo eulogy on "Indian Knowledge Systems", the lesson worth carrying into this anniversary is not one of loss dwelt upon, but of proof already furnished. India has held the sea before, decisively and on its own terms, and knows precisely what it takes to do so again.

Resistance without a 'doctrine' And when the sea had to be reclaimed, it was Indians who did the reclaiming, well before any modern navy existed to do it for them. The Kunjali Marakkars held the Malabar coast against Portuguese carracks for the better part of a century, using shallow-draught vessels to exploit geography the heavier European ships could not follow into. Kanhoji Angre, from his forts along the Konkan coast, turned Maratha sea power into a standing irritant that European trading fleets could not dislodge for decades.

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, who held that mastery of the ocean was the mark of a truly formidable power, built the forts and the fleet that made Angre's resistance possible in the first place. Neither the Marakkars nor Sarkhel Angre had access to the word "doctrine". But what they practised as asymmetric resistance, calibrated to deny an adversary free use of the sea, is recognisably the same logic the Indian Navy would later commit to paper.

It is a lineage worth being proud of on its own terms a few centuries before India had a navy in the modern sense. It had Indians who refused to cede a vision and an operational hold over the oceans. Repeated argument What is less often told is how much of a self-actuation it took to carry Panikkar's 1945 warning to a Navy that writes its own doctrine.

Independent India's strategic culture, for much of its first four decades, remained stubbornly land-centric. One can understand this, given wars fought in 1962 and 1971 were along the northern and western borders. The sea, even as it carried the bulk of India's trade, struggled to hold the attention of a strategic establishment consumed by the "two-front" framing of Pakistan and China.
