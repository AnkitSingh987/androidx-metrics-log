Title: Schooling can put an end to child marriage Part One

Schooling can put an end to child marriage

While that view is valid today, it does not fully explain why generations of Indian families accepted and sometimes encouraged the practice.

It did not arise from ignorance alone; it developed through the combined effects of history, insecurity, poverty, social customs, and economic pressures. Understanding these forces is essential if India is to end child marriage permanently. How could a civilization that celebrated women scholars such as Gargi and Maitreyi later witness the widespread practice of child marriage?

The answer lies not in any single religious tradition or cultural belief, but in centuries of political upheaval, economic insecurity, changing social norms, and evolving notions of family honour. Child marriage in India was neither universal nor static. It developed gradually over time, influenced by historical circumstances that varied across regions and communities.

Understanding this evolution is essential, not to justify the practice, but to appreciate how societies change and why education has emerged as the most powerful force in transforming the lives of girls. The encouraging news is that India has made remarkable progress. Yet the challenge is far from over.

The country's experience demonstrates that legislation alone cannot end child marriage; sustained investment in girls' education, economic opportunity, and community awareness is what changes social behaviour. Several eminent historians have examined the evolution of marriage practices in ancient and medieval India. Historian Romila Thapar observes that early Indian society exhibited considerable diversity in marriage customs, with evidence suggesting that girls in many communities married only after attaining maturity.

A. L. Basham, in The Wonder That Was India, notes that child marriage became increasingly common during the later medieval period as social structures grew more rigid and concerns over security and family honour intensified. Similarly, historian Upinder Singh, in A History of Ancient and Early Medieval India, points out that early Indian society was far more varied than often assumed, and that marriage customs evolved in response to changing political, economic, and social conditions.

These scholarly perspectives reinforce the view that child marriage was not an original or immutable feature of Indian civilisation, but a historical development shaped by multiple factors. Over time, what began as a response to insecurity became institutionalised as social custom. Traditions that may once have addressed extraordinary circumstances eventually hardened into accepted norms, surviving long after the original conditions had disappeared.

Economic hardship has historically been one of the most powerful drivers of child marriage. For families living on the margins, marrying daughters early reduced household expenditure and was often perceived as lowering future dowry obligations. In many rural communities, parents believed that delaying marriage increased both financial costs and social risks.

However, economics alone does not explain the persistence of child marriage. UNFPA's analysis of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) concludes that child marriage is sustained by a combination of poverty, gender inequality, limited educational opportunities, and deeply embedded social norms rather than by any single factor. Education levels strongly influence marriage patterns.
