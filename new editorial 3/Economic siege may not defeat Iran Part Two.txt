Title: Economic siege may not defeat Iran Part Two

Economic siege may not defeat Iran

Their objectives are economic and strategic: securing payments for Yemeni civil servants, lifting restrictions on flights and shipping, and obtaining a greater share of revenues from Yemen's oil exports. Since 2014, they have controlled north-western Yemen, including Sana'a and much of the western coast. Geography and military adaptation favour the Houthis.

They operate from defensible highlands and use mines, caves, bunkers and tunnels. They have access to fibre-optic drones that are difficult to jam. In 2025, they shot down seven U.S. Reaper drones and nearly hit American fighter aircraft. Last month, they reportedly brought down a Turkish-made Bayraktar drone operated by Saudi forces and have begun developing their own drones.

Pressure on Iran can now reverberate across both the Gulf and Red Sea. Iran's leadership adds another complication. The emerging establishment differs from the model associated with Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who balanced competing interests by alternating between reformists and hardliners. The new generation of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps commanders appears more action-oriented, security-focused and results-driven.

Mohsen Rezaei is the new national security adviser, while Ahmed Vahidi and Hossein Taeb represent the harder security establishment. Seven senior leaders are consolidating power, while Mojtaba Khamenei has remained absent from public life. The new leadership therefore appears more uncompromising than its predecessor, although its longer-term direction remains uncertain.

For Washington, the blockade may be less a route to victory than an attempt to avoid another war. Iran may still negotiate, but its new leadership is unlikely to accept coercion without demanding substantial financial and strategic compensation. The MOU offers a possible exit because it contained major economic incentives.

Washington must use that opening without appearing to reward resistance. For India, the implications are immediate. A prolonged confrontation threatens oil prices, shipping through Hormuz and maritime commerce through the Red Sea. India's energy security and trade are therefore vulnerable to developments beyond its control.

China's continuing purchase of Iranian oil could make the crisis another arena of U.S.-China competition, complicating India's calculations. Economic coercion can weaken Iran without resolving the nuclear question. Decades of sanctions have forced Tehran to develop alternative financial and trading channels, while China provides an important economic outlet.

Military pressure has not eliminated Iran's strategic capabilities, and diplomacy has yet to bridge the fundamental dispute. For India, therefore, the significance of the U.S. economic siege lies not simply in whether Washington can impose greater pain on Tehran, but in whether it can convert that pressure into a political settlement without turning the Gulf, Hormuz and the Red Sea into a wider strategic confrontation.

By ADVITYA MADAN The writer, a retired Brigadier of the Indian Army, is an analyston geopolitics, international and defence affairs.
