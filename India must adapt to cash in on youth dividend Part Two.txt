Title: India must adapt to cash in on youth dividend Part Two

India must adapt to cash in on youth dividend

Indian engineers help build many of the world's most sophisticated AI systems, but much of the intellectual property, frontier research and commercial value continue to be created elsewhere. India exports talent while importing advanced AI platforms. Bridging this gap requires rethinking education before rethinking technology.

The National Education Policy 2020 rightly emphasises multidisciplinary learning, critical thinking and digital literacy. However, implementation must accelerate. AI is reducing the premium on memorisation while increasing the value of reasoning, creativity and complex problem-solving. Universities should integrate AI across disciplines instead of treating it as a specialised engineering subject.

Medical colleges should teach AI-assisted diagnostics, law schools should explore algorithmic governance, business schools should incorporate data-driven decision-making, and agricultural institutions should prepare students for precision farming. Equally important is lifelong learning. The traditional assumption that education ends in one's early twenties is increasingly obsolete.

Workers will need continuous reskilling as AI reshapes industries at an unprecedented pace. Countries such as Singapore have already institutionalised lifelong learning through programmes like SkillsFuture, enabling citizens to update their competencies throughout their careers. India will require a similar national commitment if it hopes to preserve labour-market resilience.

Skill development alone, however, is insufficient. India must also strengthen its AI ecosystem. The IndiaAI Mission is an encouraging beginning, particularly its emphasis on computing infrastructure, datasets and startup support. But becoming an AI leader demands sustained investment in research universities, semiconductor capabilities, high-performance computing and indigenous foundation models capable of understanding India's linguistic and cultural diversity.

Public-private partnerships will be essential, but so will long-term public investment in frontier science. There is another reason why an AI dividend matters. Artificial intelligence is no longer simply an economic technology. It is becoming a strategic capability. AI increasingly influences defence planning, cybersecurity, healthcare, governance and industrial competitiveness.

Countries that merely consume foreign AI systems risk technological dependence in areas central to national security and economic sovereignty. India cannot afford such dependence. Strategic autonomy in the 21st century will depend as much on computational capability and research ecosystems as it once depended on energy security or military strength.

The demographic dividend has always been described as a once-in-a-generation opportunity because favourable age structures eventually disappear. India's working-age population is expected to peak in the coming decades before the country too begins ageing. That leaves only a limited window to transform demographic potential into sustainable productivity.

Artificial intelligence has therefore raised the stakes rather than diminished them. A youthful population without advanced skills may struggle in an automated economy. A youthful population equipped with AI capabilities, digital literacy and adaptive education could become the foundation of India's next growth story.

The real question is not whether AI will replace India's demographic dividend. It is whether India can use AI to amplify it. If policymakers invest with urgency in education, research, innovation and lifelong skills, India could become the first major economy to convert demographic strength into technological leadership.

If those investments are delayed, the country risks discovering that the world's largest workforce is no guarantee of prosperity in an economy where intelligence  -  both human and artificial, has become the defining source of national power. By SIDDHARTH ROY The writer is a civil engineer, consultant (invitee) to Rashtriya Raksha University under the Ministry of Home Affairs, and a contemporary affairs commentator.
