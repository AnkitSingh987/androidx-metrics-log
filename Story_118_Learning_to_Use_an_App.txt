Title: Learning to Use an App

Category: Intermediate

Elena wiped down the corner table with a damp cloth, pausing to look around the bustling dining room of 'La Bella Storia.' She had worked at the family-owned Italian restaurant for eight years, and she knew every regular customer by name. She knew that Mr. Henderson liked his espresso extra hot, and that the Miller children always wanted extra cheese on their pasta. For Elena, waiting tables wasn't just a job; it was an art form. She prided herself on her memory and her trusty leather-bound notepad, where she scribbled orders in a unique shorthand that only she could understand.

However, things were changing at La Bella Storia. The owner, Marco, had recently attended a restaurant management seminar and returned with a head full of modern ideas. He had decided that the restaurant was losing money due to inefficiencies and miscommunications between the front of house and the kitchen. His solution was a complete digital overhaul.

"Starting Monday, we are going paperless," Marco announced during the Saturday staff meeting, holding up a sleek, black tablet. "Everyone will use this new ordering app. You take the order at the table, press a button, and it sends the ticket directly to the kitchen screens. It will reduce mistakes, speed up service, and automatically track inventory."

The younger staff members murmured in approval, looking excited about the upgrade. Elena, however, felt a cold knot of anxiety in her stomach. She stared at the tablet in Marco’s hand as if it were a dangerous weapon. She wasn't naturally comfortable with technology; she still used an old flip phone because she found smartphones too complicated.

"Marco, my notepad works perfectly fine," Elena argued gently after the meeting. "I never make mistakes with my shorthand, and the kitchen always knows what I mean."

"I know you're a great waitress, Elena," Marco replied sympathetically. "But this is the future. If we want to stay competitive, we have to modernize. It’s just an app. It's designed to be user-friendly. You'll learn it in no time."

When Monday arrived, Elena was handed her personal tablet. It felt heavy and awkward in her hands. During her shift, she approached her first table, feeling unusually nervous. It was a nice elderly couple who always ordered the exact same thing: two bowls of minestrone soup and a shared margherita pizza.

"Good evening, folks," Elena smiled, trying to hide her discomfort. She tapped the screen to wake it up. The app opened to a confusing grid of categories: Starters, Mains, Pizzas, Drinks, Specials. 

The man gave his order, and Elena began desperately tapping the screen, trying to navigate through the menus. She found the soup, but couldn't figure out how to change the quantity to two. She accidentally clicked on a seafood pasta instead, panicked, and couldn't find the 'delete' button. Her face grew hot as the seconds ticked by. What normally took her ten seconds of scribbling was turning into a painful ordeal.

"Is everything alright, dear?" the woman asked, noticing Elena's flushed face.

"Yes, perfectly fine," Elena lied, forcing a smile. "Just getting used to our new system."

She eventually managed to submit the order, but the stress left her exhausted. The rest of the evening was a disaster. Because she was staring down at the glowing screen, trying to find the right buttons, she wasn't making eye contact with her customers. She couldn't chat with them or read their body language. At one point, she accidentally sent an order for five garlic breads instead of one, causing the head chef to shout from the kitchen window. 

By the end of the week, Elena was seriously considering resigning. She felt slow, clumsy, and entirely disconnected from the job she used to love. She missed the rhythm of the dining room and the personal connections she used to make.

On Friday evening, the restaurant was fully booked. It was chaotic, loud, and incredibly fast-paced. Elena was assigned to a large party of twelve celebrating a birthday. As she took out her tablet to record their complex drink orders, the screen suddenly froze. She tapped it repeatedly, but it remained completely unresponsive. 

Panic set in. The customers were waiting, looking at her expectantly. She looked around for Marco, but he was busy managing a dispute at the front desk. She had no choice.

Elena slid the useless tablet into her apron pocket and pulled out her old, familiar notepad and a pen. Taking a deep breath, she looked up at the table, a genuine smile returning to her face. 

"Alright, who wants to start?" she asked brightly. 

Her confidence immediately returned. She moved fluidly around the table, joking with the guests, making recommendations, and scribbling furiously in her notepad. It felt wonderful to be looking at people instead of a screen. She took the massive order in record time and rushed to the kitchen to hand the slip directly to the chef, just like the old days.

Later that night, after the restaurant had finally closed and the last tables were being wiped down, Marco found Elena sitting at the bar. She had placed her tablet on the counter, ready to hand it back to him. 

"I'm sorry, Marco," she said quietly. "I tried. I really did. But this machine just gets in my way. It makes me a worse waitress."

Marco sighed, sitting on the stool next to her. "I saw what happened with the birthday party. Your tablet crashed."

"It did. But honestly, even if it hadn't, I hate using it. I spend so much time looking at the menus that I ignore the customers. That's not how hospitality should work."

Marco picked up the tablet, turning it over in his hands. "You make a fair point, Elena. The technology is supposed to help you, not distract you. But I also saw how quickly the kitchen got overwhelmed tonight because the other waiters were sending in huge orders digitally, while yours had to be read and entered manually by the chef."

He paused, looking thoughtfully at her notepad. "What if we compromise? What if you use your notepad at the table, so you can interact with the customers the way you always have? Then, when you step away, you can use the tablet to punch the order into the system. It might take you an extra thirty seconds, but it keeps the kitchen running smoothly."

Elena considered the idea. The main problem was the pressure of trying to use the app while people were watching and waiting. If she could use the app in quiet moments, away from the table, it wouldn't be so intimidating. 

"I can try that," she agreed cautiously. "But I'll need someone to show me how to delete things properly. I almost ordered fifty coffees today."

Marco laughed. "I will personally give you a tutorial tomorrow morning before we open. We’ll practice until you can do it with your eyes closed."

Over the next few weeks, Elena adopted the hybrid system. She greeted her tables warmly, making eye contact and taking notes with her pen. Then, she would stand near the service station and calmly enter the shorthand notes into the app. Without the pressure of a waiting customer, she quickly memorized where the buttons were. She learned how to add special requests, delete mistakes, and even check if a specific wine was still in stock without having to run down to the cellar.

To her surprise, she began to realize that the app did have benefits. When a customer asked for the bill, she could print it instantly from the tablet, rather than doing the math in her head during a busy rush. 

One busy evening, Marco walked past her station and noticed her swiftly tapping an order into the screen while simultaneously laughing at a joke one of the bartenders was telling. 

"You look like a natural," Marco teased her. 

Elena smiled, slipping the tablet into her apron right next to her notepad. "It's just another tool in the kitchen, Marco. You just have to learn the right recipe to make it work."
