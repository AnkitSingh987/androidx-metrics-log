Title: Cart before horse On India and vehicle to vehicle communication technology

Category: Editorial

The primary impediment to the efficacious deployment of V2V technology in India is the staggering heterogeneity and profound indiscipline of its traffic environment. V2V systems are predicated on a highly structured, rule-bound environment where the primary variables are other similarly equipped, predictable vehicles. The Indian roadscape, however, is a chaotic, fluid ecosystem characterized by the simultaneous presence of high-speed automobiles, heavily laden commercial trucks, millions of two-wheelers, auto-rickshaws, pedestrians, and stray livestock. This hyper-heterogeneity renders the predictive algorithms that underpin V2V communication virtually useless. A sophisticated collision avoidance system, designed to communicate with other smart vehicles, is entirely blind to the sudden, unpredictable trajectory of a pedestrian darting across a highway or a non-motorized cart encroaching upon a major intersection. The technology assumes a homogeneity of connected actors that simply does not exist in the Indian context, thereby neutralizing its primary safety benefits.

Furthermore, the foundational infrastructure required to support V2X communication—where vehicles interact with intelligent traffic signals, toll booths, and road infrastructure—is virtually non-existent in India outside of a few isolated urban enclaves. V2X relies on a ubiquitous, highly reliable, and ultra-low-latency communication network, typically predicated on widespread 5G deployment and the pervasive installation of dedicated short-range communications (DSRC) hardware on road infrastructure. The sheer scale and cost of retrofitting India’s vast, often poorly maintained road network with this sophisticated hardware are astronomically prohibitive. Mandating V2V capabilities in vehicles without the corresponding investment in the underlying supportive infrastructure is an exercise in technological futility; it equips vehicles with the capacity to communicate, but provides them with nothing meaningful to communicate with.

Moreover, the economic implications of prematurely forcing this technology onto the Indian market are highly regressive. The Indian automotive market is intensely price-sensitive, dominated by entry-level compact cars and two-wheelers. The integration of the sophisticated sensors, processors, and communication modules required for V2V capability will inevitably precipitate a significant escalation in vehicle costs. This technological premium will disproportionately impact lower-income consumers, potentially pricing them out of the market for safer, modern vehicles and forcing them to rely on older, more polluting, and inherently less safe modes of transport. This creates a perverse outcome where a safety mandate inadvertently exacerbates broader societal vulnerabilities.

The fixation on cutting-edge V2V technology also distracts from the far more pressing, foundational interventions required to improve road safety in India. The astronomical fatality rate is not primarily the result of a lack of sophisticated vehicle communication, but rather the consequence of abysmal road engineering, the ubiquitous issuance of fraudulent driving licenses, the total absence of pedestrian infrastructure, and the systemic failure to enforce basic traffic regulations. Investing massive capital in V2V technology while neglecting to fix fatal black spots on highways, failing to mandate basic structural crashworthiness in all vehicle segments, and ignoring the catastrophic indiscipline of commercial drivers is a profound misallocation of national priorities.

In conclusion, while Vehicle-to-Vehicle communication undeniably represents the future of automotive safety, its immediate application in the Indian context is highly premature. The aggressive promotion of this technology by stakeholders often prioritizes the optics of modernization over the pragmatic realities of the domestic traffic ecosystem. India must urgently address the foundational deficits in its road infrastructure, driver training, and traffic enforcement before it can meaningfully leverage advanced communication technologies. The cart of sophisticated V2V systems cannot be placed before the horse of basic, structural road safety. Until the fundamental chaos of the Indian roadscape is tamed through rigorous engineering and enforcement, advanced communication algorithms will remain an expensive, ineffective novelty, incapable of significantly reducing the tragic human toll on the nation's highways.
