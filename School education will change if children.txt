Title: School education will change if children

School education will change, if children were to vote

Much of India's schooling model assumes that knowledge must be memorised before it is understood, and that uniformity ensures quality Each election season, India debates familiar concerns - growth, inflation, jobs, subsidies and infrastructure. What goes largely unmentioned is the constituency that will live with the consequences of every policy made. India has 20 crore school-going children.

They don't vote, and, consequently, seldom shape national priorities. Their absence from the electoral map explains why education reform moves slowly. Problems like teacher shortages, inconsistent learning levels, outdated instruction, and overcrowded curricula, continue without political urgency. This raises a question: What would change if children could vote?

The treatment of teachers would be the first to shift. Even the best-intentioned teachers spend too much time on administrative work. Teaching gets the remaining time left, estimated at 19 per cent today. Children would invert this logic. Governments would have to show that teachers are teaching, not functioning as field staff.

You would also see a correction in teacher availability. Vacancies have stretched for years, and multi-grade classrooms are common. If children had a vote, this cost would be measured not in audit paragraphs but in political damage. Recruitment cycles would shorten. Absenteeism wouldn't be tolerated.

Teacher deployment would no longer be a matter of administrative convenience. The most significant shift would occur in the nature of learning itself. Much of India's schooling model assumes that knowledge must be memorised before it is understood, and that uniformity ensures quality. Children have little say in what or how they learn.

If they could vote, policymakers would have to pay attention to pedagogical design and classrooms that encourage questioning, exploration and comprehension. Finishing the syllabus would cease to be the dominant success metric. Exam tyranny Assessment would also undergo a rethinking. India's exam-heavy culture has persisted because it is administratively simple and politically safe.

Exams test memory more than understanding. Memory is easy to score, but it doesn't prepare children for an economy built on problem-solving, communication and judgement. If they had electoral power, the system would be judged not by exams conducted but by whether these reflect actual learning. The question on children's minds, "Did school help me grow?," could no longer be avoided.

The culture of learning would also change. Fear has long been the quiet engine of schooling: of marks, being left behind, or disappointing one's parents. Fear-driven environments are politically easy to maintain because the affected group has no voice. But a system unsettling children would not survive if they were voters.

Leaders would have to make confidence, curiosity and motivation central features of the classroom. Incentives would shift from enforcing discipline to enabling potential. Another point should be considered: India often celebrates jugaad, which children are naturally adept at. But jugaad cannot substitute sustained capability.

Children would likely ask: What comes after the quick fix? What enduring learning are we building? The spotlight would move from improvising solutions to equipping children to create lasting ones. This thought experiment is not about lowering the voting age. It highlights the distance between who benefits from education policy and who influences it.

Children carry the burden of weak learning outcomes, but adults decide the pace and direction of reform. India's ambitions cannot be met without confronting this. We may not be handing children ballot papers soon. But nothing stops us from evaluating schooling by a measure that truly matters: whether it expands children's abilities to think, question, imagine and contribute.

If children could vote, education would not be an afterthought. It would be the starting point of national strategy. By Vineet Nayar The writer is Founder-Chairman, Sampark Foundation, and former CEO, HCL Tech
