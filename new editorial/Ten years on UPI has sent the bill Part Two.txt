Title: Ten years on UPI has sent the bill Part Two

10 years on, UPI has sent the bill

Persistent migration toward cash could weaken deposit mobilisation at the margin, prompting banks to raise deposit rates, compress margins or reprice loans  -  making credit marginally dearer, a risk policymakers must address rather than dismiss. The tax question is sharper still. UPI's digital trail has been central to GST formalisation; cash is easier to omit from invoices and returns.

An MDR nudging precisely the higher-ticket, revenue-relevant transactions back to cash would compound that risk, unlike the innocuous tea-stall payment officials cite. Consumption, too, may suffer: by restoring the friction UPI removed, the fee could lead to discretionary high-ticket spending being postponed. Two design flaws deserve scrutiny.

First, structuring: splitting a Rs 10,000 bill into five Rs 2,000 transfers avoids the levy while multiplying the settlement and fraud-monitoring load MDR is meant to fund  -  self-defeating unless anti-structuring rules follow. Second, the P2P-P2M asymmetry: a Rs 1 lakh transfer to a friend stays free, while a Rs 2,001 transfer to a shopkeeper is charged, even though P2P transfers incur comparable infrastructure costs. NPCI's implicit justification that merchants monetise the relationship elsewhere while individuals do not is not entirely defensible.

The real test for this policy is not the MDR revenue collected, but that revenue net of the consumer-price pass-through, the cost of cash displaced onto a strained ATM network, and any tax leakage from transactions nudged back into currency. India built UPI to retire cash, not subsidise its comeback; financing the platform's second decade should not mean pricing transactions back into the shadow economy it was designed to shrink. By SANKALPA BHATTACHARJEE (The writer teaches Economics at IIM Ranchi.
