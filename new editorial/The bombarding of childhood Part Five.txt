Title: The bombarding of childhood Part Five

The bombarding of childhood

When every pause is filled, every uncertainty immediately resolved and every wandering thought pulled back by a brighter cue, waiting and reflection can begin to feel intolerable. The child learns to reach for the next stimulus before the previous one has had time to settle. To give space for a child's attention to drift inward, to reflect, is therefore to leave room for the child's inner life.

Slow media changes the relation between screen and child by saying: you may arrive at your own pace. Some critics may dismiss slow media as nostalgia: a longing for the children's television of the previous generation, or a privilege available only to parents with time and large bookshelves and big houses with quiet rooms. But that is a notion we should refuse.

The attention economy has trained us to treat a child's pause as a problem, a wandering gaze as lost engagement, and an unfilled moment as wasted time. The opposite is true  -  slow time gives children the space to perceive, process, respond and make meaning  -  and it can be designed for in children's media. Like energy drinks, fast media promise more capacity than the child actually has: more attention is supposed to lead to better performance.

Fast media feel like an extension of the child's energy, but the bargain is unsustainable and unhealthy. What looks like support can become dependency, and what promises engagement and learning is actually compulsion. Children need media that do not merely occupy them, instruct them or keep them quiet.

They benefit and grow from stories and shows that preserve children's capacity for inwardness and their relation to others. In fast media, the child's attention is continually propped up by the next stimulus. Slow media insist on the opposite. In the end, the aim is not to withdraw children from the digital world, but to make that world more worthy of them.

By Natalia Kucirkova
