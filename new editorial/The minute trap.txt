Title: The minute trap

The 10-minute trap

Quick delivery apps are taking a toll on gig workers In India's megacities, a new economic race has taken hold  -  one measured not in miles or hours, but in ten minutes flat. Ultra-fast delivery platforms such as Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart and others promise groceries, meals and daily essentials at astonishing speed. While this model thrives in developing, densely populated cities due to low labour costs, high unemployment and an abundant supply of young workers, its social and labour implications remain deeply troubling.

Unlike standard logistics systems, where delivery is planned and risk is distributed across infrastructure, scheduling and inventory management, the 10-minute delivery model concentrates risk almost entirely on the last-mile worker  -  the delivery rider and the dark-store picker. Evidence from workers themselves reveals the human cost behind the stopwatch  -  the race against time often leads to accidents and injuries. There are media reports of workers being sent home without pay for missing performance targets, with no explanation or grievance mechanism.

Delivery riders have echoed similar concerns. These testimonies align with findings from multiple labour studies showing that algorithmic management  -  opaque ratings, automated penalties, and unpredictable task allocation  -  strips workers of control while intensifying pressure. Gig workers, in their recent strike in Hyderabad, protested wage cuts, unsafe delivery timelines, lack of basic amenities at dark stores, and arbitrary app deactivations.

They have demanded  -  removal of ultra-short delivery timelines; minimum guaranteed wages; accident insurance and medical coverage; and Transparent grievance redressal mechanisms. While India's new labour codes mark an important step by recognising gig and platform workers, they do not directly address speed-driven delivery models, where risk continues to be disproportionately borne by workers. The 10-minute delivery is reshaping consumer psychology and social habits.

This transformation is not abstract. It is deeply personal. Most people are slowly getting accustomed to the convenience of 10-minute delivery app. The model is not merely demand-driven, but actively manufactures habits  - replacing planning, self-reliance and routine with instant availability. Such habits erode basic consumption discipline, weaken daily planning skills, and promote instant gratification among young people.

When a generation grows accustomed to outsourcing even the smallest acts of self-reliance, the long-term implications for productivity, resilience and social responsibility are deeply concerning. Supporters of ultra-fast delivery often argue that such services are a lifeline for senior citizens living alone. There is merit in recognising that doorstep delivery of medicines and essentials can provide real relief in such situations.

However, this benefit is neither new nor unique to the 10-minute delivery model. For decades, neighbourhood grocers and local pharmacists have informally extended precisely this support  -  delivering medicines, groceries and essentials to elderly residents without imposing artificial time pressures on workers or normalising extreme urgency. The critical question, therefore, is not whether home delivery is useful, but whether compressing it into a 10-minute promise is necessary or socially responsible.

A society that cannot wait 10 minutes without transferring risk onto another human being is not merely inefficient  -  it is unsustainable. The debate around 10-minute delivery is about what kind of work culture and consumption culture India wants to build. If speed continues to be prioritised without accountability, infrastructure or labour protection, the cost will be paid not only by delivery workers, but by society at large.

By Prabir Kumar Bandyopadhyay The writer is a retired Management Professor and Independent Researcher
