Title: Why India needs a new economic model Part One

Why India needs a new economic model

The two major problems are the lack of well paying jobs and rural stagnation. Both these issues need urgent attention India needs a new economic model. The current model, launched as part of the 1991 reforms, has delivered high GDP growth, hitting 8.2 per cent over July-September 2025. That's good.

But high GDP growth does not mean that all is well. For example, in the 1980s, before the 1991 reforms, growth accelerated to over 5 per cent, well above past low rates. Yet India suffered a major macroeconomic crash in 1991, which forced it to abandon the model it adopted in the 1950s. India is not headed to a similar crash today, and the current model has reduced poverty and increased consumption.

Nevertheless, India is facing a severe multidimensional socio-economic crisis, with its problems stemming from its economic model. The jobs factor The greatest failure is the shortage of reasonably paying jobs. Thousands of over-qualified people apply for a single low-paying job. Many people work long hours but do not make a decent living.

Even motivated and capable young people from low-income backgrounds struggle to get a reasonable job. The growth has not reached them. Most unexpectedly, over 800 million Indians, a majority, get food subsidies. Economic growth should have allowed most people to buy their food. Instead, the share of the population getting food subsidies has increased significantly.

The government funds spent on these subsidies reduce the money available for other critical areas, such as health and education. Subsidies don't come free. More than 60 per cent of Indians live in villages, primarily employed in agriculture. India's economic model has left them behind. Agriculture produces only about 15-20 per cent of the GDP.

In response to the low earnings, the government runs multiple subsidy schemes. Yet, it's not enough. Overwhelming debt leads thousands of farmers and others to commit suicide every year. In Punjab, Haryana, and Gujarat, which are leading States, young people sell their assets, and pay thousands of dollars to be smuggled into well-off countries such as the US and Canada.

'Ghost' villages Others migrate to large cities. Here, they earn so little that they live in slums. When they move to distant cities, as is common, this tears families apart. The migrants' families don't have the means to travel long distances to meet them. Young migrants can no longer care for their aging parents, except by sending money home.

Some States already have "ghost villages," where only a few elderly people live. The current model is creating a significant future problem. At the same time, life in large cities is becoming harsher. There's extensive air and water pollution. In winter, the air is hazardous to people's health in New Delhi and many other cities across India.

Further, the authorities cannot keep up with the increasing demand for municipal services. Traffic congestion and delays are often horrendous, even in cities with Metros. Today, production is concentrated in large cities. The top ten urban agglomerations, which account for about 10 percent of the population, produce about 25 per cent of the output.

Continued increases in GDP will come from higher production in such cities. It will lead to more people, more pollution, more congestion, more water problems, and more strewn garbage. Life will get harsher, not easier. On a positive note, India has had considerable success in knowledge-based sectors such as software and pharmaceuticals.
