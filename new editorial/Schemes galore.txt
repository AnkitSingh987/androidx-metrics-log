Title: Schemes galore

Schemes galore

Centrally Sponsored Schemes should be rationalised The Centre, according to a recent report in this newspaper, is keen to rationalise the number of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSSs)  -  those run with Central and States' contributions. The intent coincides with the five-year term of over 50 of these schemes ending this March. CSSs have been criticised for over two decades for being clumsy arrangements that evade accountability and generate poor outcomes.

With the upcoming Budget faced with a revenue constraint as well as inflexible expenditures, moves to rationalise CSSs, which account for over 8 per cent of the total Budget size or about ₹4 lakh crore, can make a major difference in improving the efficiency of allocations. The Centre said last May that efforts in this direction had helped release funds for capital expenditure. Indeed, there have been enough CAG reports to show that many of these schemes are riddled with inefficiencies.

Various committees over time  -  such as one helmed by former Cabinet Secretary BK Chaturvedi whose report was submitted in 2011, and a panel of chief ministers about four years later  -  have suggested changes, which have in turn been echoed by the 15th Finance Commission. They have said that schemes below a certain minimum fund size should be dissolved. Many suggestions of the CMs' panel have since found their way into policy, although their target of reducing the number of CSSs to 30 seems a far cry  -  with 'flagship schemes' being announced from time to time.

Schemes have been divided into ''core' and 'optional'  -  although it can perhaps be said that the panel's proposal to reduce the numbers to one 'umbrella scheme' per sector remains a work in progress. An important move with respect to increasing State autonomy was taken in 2016, when 25 per cent of the central allocation was earmarked as a 'flexifund', to be used by the States to design and implement the concerned scheme or its variant in its own way, but in keeping with Central guidelines. Whether this effort has paid off is worth an evaluation.

Meanwhile, CAG reports on the National Health Mission and the Pradhan Mantri Kaushal Vikas Yojana point to a familiar tale of unused allocations, mismatch between Central and State contributions and underwhelming outcomes. The lack of coordination, of course, turns into a political issue. Amidst this mess, it is best that more CSSs, especially those that fall under the State and Concurrent Lists, are run either as wholly central or State schemes, with a clear accountability trail.

CSSs, to be fair, were conceived under Article 282 of the Constitution in order to empower the Centre to implement schemes outside of its strict domain to deal with development deficits of States. There was certainly a case in post-Independent India to equalise the provision of basic services across States with different governance capacities. But the times have changed, and CSSs do not inspire the same confidence.

It is time to review the design andraison d etreof CSSs.
