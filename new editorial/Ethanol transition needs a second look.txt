Ethanol transition needs a second look

The country is advancing towards its ethanol blending targets, driven by the twin objectives of reducing oil imports and strengthening energy security. Yet as E20 becomes the default fuel option, consumers are no longer being offered a choice between different petrol blends. The government's decision to remove multiple fuel grades to simplify logistics raises an important question about how ambitious energy transitions should balance efficiency with public confidence.

India's ethanol programme is driven by clear policy goals. Reducing oil imports, strengthening energy security and expanding opportunities for farmers are all worthwhile goals. The government's case for a single fuel standard is equally straightforward. Maintaining multiple fuel grades would require additional investments in storage, distribution and inventory management, increasing costs across the fuel supply chain.

With substantial resources already committed to ethanol production and infrastructure, policymakers argue that standardisation is the most practical way forward. It is a reasonable case. But every policy choice involves trade-offs. While the government has explained the cost of offering consumers more flexibility, it has paid far less attention to the cost of removing that flexibility altogether.

For millions, particularly those driving vehicles originally designed for lower ethanol blends, the issue is not whether ethanol has a role in India's energy future. It is whether consumers should have any say during the transition. The Petroleum Ministry cites consultations with automobile manufacturers and field data from companies such as Maruti Suzuki and Hero MotoCorp, which it says show no widespread evidence of E20-related engine damage.

It also acknowledges that E20 may reduce fuel economy by three to five per cent, while arguing that lower oil imports, higher octane ratings and reduced emissions justify the trade-off. Yet policy acceptance is not determined by technical assessments alone. It is shaped by trust. Even when governments believe they have made the right decision, public confidence is easier to build when consumers retain a degree of flexibility rather than being presented with a single nationwide standard.

That is the real lesson from Brazil's ethanol programme. Often cited as the benchmark for higher ethanol blending, Brazil's success was not simply the result of ambitious targets. It was built on a gradual transition in which vehicle technology, fuel infrastructure and consumer adoption evolved together.

As flex-fuel vehicles became commonplace, users retained the flexibility to choose the fuel that best suited their needs, allowing the market to adapt alongside policy rather than after it. That gradual evolution proved just as important as the blending targets themselves. Rather than asking consumers to adapt first and technology to follow later, Brazil aligned vehicle technology, fuel infrastructure and consumer expectations over time.

Consumer confidence became an outcome of the transition rather than an afterthought. India's circumstances are, of course, different. Its energy security concerns, vehicle fleet and agricultural priorities cannot simply be compared with Brazil's. Nor should India's ethanol ambitions be questioned. Diversifying India's fuel mix remains an important strategic objective.

But lasting policy success depends on more than meeting government targets. It requires a framework that encourages innovation, gives consumers confidence, and allows technological change to be driven by adoption rather than compulsion. India's ethanol ambitions deserve recognition, but ambitious targets alone do not guarantee lasting success.

The real measure of any energy transition is whether it creates the conditions for innovation, consumer confidence and technological adaptation to advance together. That is the lesson India should take from Brazil, not simply how much ethanol to blend, but how to build a transition that consumers are willing to embrace. By SHREY MADAAN The writer is Indian Policy Associate, Consumer Choice Center.
