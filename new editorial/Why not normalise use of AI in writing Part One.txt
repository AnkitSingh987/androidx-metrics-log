Title: Why not normalise use of AI in writing Part One

Why not normalise use of AI in writing?

AI writing has emerged as a genuine de-colonial tool for post-colonial societies, pushing back against the malpractices normalised under literary neocolonialism Why not simply normalise the use of artificial intelligence in academic or any other forms of writing? Some will call this an ethical violation, arguing that human intellect cannot be surrendered to thoughtlessness, and that people will hand the tasks to AI and then claim the outcome as their own.

But have we not already crossed that bar? Taking credit from our mechanised tools. The potatoes a chef puts into an air fryer are sold as the "chef's special". The shooter gets the credit or blame for pulling the trigger, not the metallurgist or the arms manufacturer. The cosmonaut is remembered as the one who landed on the moon, and not the thousands of engineers and taxpayers who indirectly made that expedition possible.

In every one of such cases, we already assign authorship to the person who directs the tool, not to the tool itself, or to every component that contributed to it. Yet, when the tool is generative AI, the same logic is suddenly reversed. The individual who directs it is accused of forfeiting authorship rather than exercising it.

How come it is legitimate for a professor or researcher to claim authorship, despite taking all the help from their research assistants, such as collected and digitised data, field observations, case studies and narratives? Why such dualism? It appears that this is not even a question of why, but whose.

Whose authority is being protected by that resistance and challenged, if AI writing gets the green signal? With a good research idea and access to advanced AI tools, someone who has never set foot on remotest sites such as Antarctica or the Everest can now produce research that rivals the work of those who spent years in the field. Someone who never became bilingual can now write fluently across languages.

Someone who always felt short to sharpen their own prose can now have it handed to them, polished, on a plate. Whose authority does that threaten? Parallels in history History gives a consistent answer as every novelty that redistributes access to power is resisted, and the resistance is rarely about the novelty itself.

People resisted metal tools when stone tools were displaced. They resisted motorised wheels when animal carts were phased out. They resisted paper currency when it replaced gold and silver coins. The high-born and propertied classes resisted the idea of social reformers that everyone deserves a right to education.

Every novelty has been resisted, from Galileo's heliocentrism to the visibility of a non-binary identity in contemporary societies. And each time, the resistance came because the novelty threatened an existing arrangement of power. Now, the question arises: who, then, benefits from labelling AI writing an "ethical violation", and who stands at a disadvantage?

Much of the loudest objection comes from a milieu long accustomed to a Western (Euro-American-centric) and elitist model of knowledge production. It is this claim of legitimate scholarship, a standard by which knowledge produced anywhere in the world is deemed worthy or unworthy. And AI has quietly punctured one of that milieu's deepest advantages  -  the near-monopoly of native English fluency.

A non-native speaker can now go toe-to-toe with a native English writer, with AI's help. Outside the Anglophone world, standard "proper" English has been the single biggest challenge for brilliant minds, and AI has turned that barrier into an opportunity. In other words, a genuine redistribution of linguistic capital.
