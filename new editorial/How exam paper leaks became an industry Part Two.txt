How exam paper leaks became an industry

But according to The Print, some of these syndicates can earn as much as ₹60 crore by selling leaked papers. So when the potential reward is many times larger than the punishment, the deterrent simply isn't strong enough. And even when a paper leak is detected, convictions are rare. These are sprawling, pan-India networks that generate massive amounts of evidence, leading to investigations and court cases that can drag on for years.

By the time a verdict arrives, public attention usually shifts elsewhere and many accused remain out on bail awaiting trial. The second problem is that the law itself has gaps. The Public Examinations Act covers only central examinations such as those conducted by the NTA (National Testing Agency)  -  NEET, JEE, or UPSC and IBPS.

State-level competitive exams like Police Service Commission exams or state CETs, fall outside its ambit. That means each state has to enact and enforce its own laws, creating an uneven system. In some cases, syndicates blacklisted in one state can simply change identities, move elsewhere, and continue running the same racket.

And finally, perhaps the biggest problem with the exams conducted by the NTA is that the organisation relies heavily on outsourced and contractual staff. A government response in the Rajya Sabha shows that while the NTA has 39 sanctioned permanent positions, only 24 are filled. Compare that with nearly 197 contractual or outsourced personnel and that creates problems.

Temporary workers handling sensitive stages like paper-setting, printing and transportation may not always be bound by the same institutional accountability as permanent employees, making it easier for standard operating procedures to be bypassed. And the economic consequences, as you can imagine, are enormous. Every paper leak forces authorities to spend money cancelling and conducting exams all over again.

Unofficial estimates also suggest that Indian families spend nearly ₹1.2 lakh crore every year preparing their children for competitive exams. That's almost as much as the country's annual education budget. So, when an exam is compromised, a part of that investment goes to waste. Also remember that the people who buy leaked papers end up beating candidates who got there on merit.

And some of them eventually become the country's future doctors. Which means a paper leak doesn't just undermine an exam, but can eventually weaken the quality of healthcare for years to come. So how do we fix this, you ask? Well, the first step is making paper leaks a loss-making business by imposing penalties that genuinely outweigh the profits.

The second is building a professionally staffed NTA with far fewer temporary vendors involved in critical processes. And finally, if possible, moving more exams online could help. JEE is already computer-based, eliminating much of the physical chain where leaks occur. And the NTA can afford these reforms.

Over the past six years, it has generated about ₹3,500 crore in revenue and nearly ₹450 crore in surplus -  money that could be reinvested to build a more secure examination system. That said, some of these reforms are already being considered. The government is looking at expanding the law to cover state-level examinations as well, while also increasing the punishment for organised exam-related crimes to as much as 10 years in prison and a minimum fine of ₹10 crore.
