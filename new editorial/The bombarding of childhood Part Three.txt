Title: The bombarding of childhood Part Three

The bombarding of childhood

We know that some apps are better designed than others. We know that interactive features in digital books can help when they serve the story, and distract when they do not. We know that characters and narratives shape children's ideas about identity, gender and social life. Pace cuts across all of these questions as the condition under which content becomes experience.

Screen time can run fast or slow. This is partly why merely clocking 'screen time' is too crude a measure. Two children may each watch 20 minutes of media and have entirely different encounters with time. In one case, the child is moved through a coherent story with pauses, repetitions and recognisable emotional beats.

In another, she is pulled through 10 clips, 30 characters, flashing colours, sudden songs and rewards that arrive before thought has had time to form. The clock records the same duration but the child does not live the same time. And parents are rightly worried: a four-year-old does not come to media with adult reserves of self-regulation.

She is still learning how to stay with a feeling, how to wait for a story to unfold, how to connect one event to another, how to notice that a character's sadness matters before the joke wipes it away. If a programme moves too quickly, it may encourage a child to expect information to arrive in rapid, constantly shifting bursts, making slower, more sustained forms of attention harder to tolerate. Stopping this vicious cycle starts by asking what exactly makes children's media fast or slow.

A child can look intensely engaged while being given very little time to think Don't get me wrong, I am not saying that slow is always good or better than fast media. Some lively children's media can be excellent; some slow media can be dull, inert or badly made. The point is that good children's media give a child time to complete an act of attention.

Bad media keep interrupting that act before it can become a memory or play. When my research group compared children's shows, apps and digital stories, we saw that pace can become a set of design choices. A story can move in a way that helps a child anticipate what comes next, or it can jump so quickly between scenes that cause and effect become blurred.

A screen can guide the eye toward one important object, or fill the frame with competing movement. This is why the familiar adult language of 'engagement' is so poor for children. A child can look intensely engaged while being given very little time to think. In the Children's Slow Media Project, we have therefore tried to make these differences visible.

We evaluate children's media by looking at how these media organise time. We ask whether the narrative is coherent enough to follow; whether the sensory field overwhelms meaning-making at specific age junctures; whether the design builds in stopping points, where the story can be digested before it goes on. Does a piece of media allow learning to develop through meaningful repetition?

And does a story or piece create openings for a child and adult, or two children, to notice something together? When in March 2026 the UK Department for Education advised parents of children aged under five to choose 'slow-paced, age-appropriate' content, without saying what slow-paced actually is, our group sought to make the term concrete. What makes a show slow or fast for a given child?
