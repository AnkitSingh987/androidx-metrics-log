Title: What does the oil shock mean for the economy Part Two

What does the oil shock mean for the economy?

The RBI should definitely prevent chaotic depreciation, but it should not resist depreciation itself, as this could be costly. Third, we must protect public capital expenditure. When fiscal pressure increases due to prolonged oil price shocks, essential investments in infrastructure, logistics, and energy diversification are often the first to be cut.

However, this is the wrong trade-off. Reducing capital expenditure to manage a temporary revenue shock sacrifices long-term growth for short-term gain, especially when private investment is also likely to decline. In such situations, public capital expenditure becomes not just a driver of growth but also a stabiliser.

Maintaining it during a commodity shock is crucial for sustaining medium-term growth. Lastly, India has documented its vulnerability to the Strait of Hormuz in successive energy policy reports without significantly addressing it. The ongoing conflict has simply priced this risk. Every dollar increase in the Indian crude basket above its pre-war level is, in some sense, a tax on the failure to diversify earlier.

Long-term contracts, diversified suppliers, and increased storage capacity must be integral to this adjustment. By Amarendu Nandy The writer is Assistant Professor of Economics at IIM Ranchi. Views are personal
