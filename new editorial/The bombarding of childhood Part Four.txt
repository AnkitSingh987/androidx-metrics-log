Title: The bombarding of childhood Part Four

The bombarding of childhood

It depends. Children differ by age, temperament, language, sensory sensitivity and circumstance; thus, without testing their responses, it is impossible to provide a firm recommendation: a show that suits one four-year-old may overwhelm another. We therefore give ratings only to provide a map for adult judgment.

The ratings are illustrations, inviting parents to check whether their child is being given enough time to complete an act of attention. The shows scoring highest on the slow rubric are those that leave room around the characters' actions and allow the child to recognise the actions. Often there is a face, a puppet, a song, a greeting, a familiar body on screen that treats attention as a relationship.

Indeed, the power of programmes such as the US show Mister Rogers' Neighborhood (1968-2001), the Norwegian series Fantorangen (2007-), and the British shows In the Night Garden… (2007-09) and Teletubbies (1997-2001) lies in their ability to build a rapport with the child and then repeat, almost as in a ritual. Here, small acts and greetings or songs provide a familiar frame, almost like a safety net, within which new concepts can be introduced. In the Australian animated series Bluey (2018-24), play often begins in an ordinary family situation and develops into imaginative games that involve parents, siblings and friends; the pace can be lively, but it tends to return the child to pretend play, negotiation and emotional understanding.

In the animated TV series The Adventures of Paddington (2019-25)  -  a British-French co-production  -  gentle mishaps are usually held inside a world of curiosity, politeness, family and repair, so comedy becomes a route into perseverance or social understanding. Such programmes are slow because they leave openings through which the story can travel back into meaning and play. The child's attention is kept in motion by a manipulative rhythm that continuously restarts attention with the next tune, next bright image, next comic beat Conversations between characters are another way in which higher-ranked shows convey new concepts with appropriate pace.

When introduced with context, a new word or idea might not be immediately clear, but the child can notice and infer. Over time, the child arrives at understanding, and this slow, even arduous process helps the concept stick. For example, in one episode of Daniel Tiger's Neighbourhood (2012-)  -  based on Mister Rogers' Neighborhood -  Daniel is nervous about trying the swing.

The scene allows that feeling to be acknowledged, sung through, and gradually transformed into confidence, with support from Daniel's friend Jodi and her grandmother. Similarly, in the Sesame Street-based game app Elmo Loves ABCs, a letter is traced, sounded, repeated, connected to words, and encountered through touch and play. The lowest-scoring media are those in which the child's attention is kept in motion by a manipulative rhythm that continuously restarts attention with the next tune, next bright image, next comic beat, next loop of familiarity or personalised nudge.

Instead of allowing the child to settle into the story, such children's shows repeatedly pull attention back; the same is true of many preschool game apps built around loops of reward and progression. Evaluating children's popular media taught me that pace is a revealing measure of quality but also a proxy for a deeper question about the child's agency, their own sense of self. The real distinction is whether a medium leaves room for the child's own attention or continually redirects it.
