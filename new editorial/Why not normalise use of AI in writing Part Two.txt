Title: Why not normalise use of AI in writing Part Two

Why not normalise use of AI in writing?

The West has lost a linguistic monopoly it once did not have to defend. The discomfort around AI writing under the banner of "No, AI Please" tracks that redistribution more closely, tracking any coherent theory of authorship. AI writing has emerged as a genuine de-colonial tool for post-colonial societies, pushing back against the malpractices normalised under literary neocolonialism.

I wonder how long this charade can hold, as one can notice the selective normalisation. It is entirely acceptable to let AI generate references, citations, infographics, graphs, slide decks, and let it silently correct the grammar too. But letting it touch the sentences is not acceptable. This very selective normalisation has created an entire economy of tools such as "AI Content Humanizer", "5th- Grade Style Writer", "No-Fluff Humanizer", "Natural Tone Rewriter", "Conversational Rewriter", and dozens more to launder AI prose back into a humanised pattern.

It is strange that instead of letting AI help people cross language barriers and let their ideas flow, we normalise the malpractice of hiding it. In other words, the rule is not "don't use AI", rather, it is "don't get caught". As a professor of mine once put it, if you are going to lie, lie well enough not to get caught.

And if you can't, don't lie at all. Consider J.K. Rowling's Platform 9¾ at London's King's Cross Station, a lie told with such conviction that it convinced the world of a magical place that never existed but people now travel to see. Not plagiarism Now, before moving on to the arguments against AI writing, one must establish that AI use is not plagiarism.

Plagiarism is theft of another's ideas and work. AI-assisted writing is neither and entirely a separate debate. The objections raised against AI writing can be located into four broad strands: that it threatens originality and the meaning of scholarly work, heavy reliance on it dulls critical intellect, it risks leaking respondents' personal data, and that it erodes human creativity.

On reflection, scholarly worth has never been simply about originality. Illogical outcomes, rubbishness, and genocidal ideologies, too, have claimed "originality" within their own internal logic. What matters is contribution with a logical solution to the problem statement without harm. As for intellect, human habits of mind take generations to change, not possible within a span of a technology cycle.

If anything, AI is capable of solving in minutes what once took months of manual labour, freeing attention for much more grave problems rather than dulling it. Third, data leakage is not an inherent property of tools such as AI, but a research-design failure, as pseudonymisation and anonymisation remain the researcher's own ethical responsibility. And lastly, AI is not Alzheimer's for human creativity.

Better tools have never suppressed creativity. They have always redirected creativity. Now, once these arguments are set aside, what remains is not a principled position but a familiar social reflex. The one that promotes "I have suffered through this, so you should too." It reflects in professors' posts on LinkedIn that they won't read a draft touched by AI.

Sounds similar to one of patriarchy's oldest tricks: the mother-in-law who insists her daughter-in-law endure what she endured, or my office clerk who advised me to pile work on a new hire, stating why should they get an easy run when we did not. Schadenfreude dressed as rigour, a bizarre satisfaction taken in someone else's hardship. Moreover, it is not a coincidence that the loudest objections tend to belong to those who have already cleared the language-proficiency bar the rest of the world is still trying to reach.
