Title: What does the oil shock mean for the economy Part One

What does the oil shock mean for the economy?

Higher import bill and a sharper decline in terms-of-trade would weaken the rupee further and push up inflation, too The ongoing US-Israel conflict with Iran, which has disrupted activity around the Strait of Hormuz, has revealed several weaknesses in India's macroeconomic position. According to the Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell (PPAC), India's crude oil basket averaged $69.01 in February.

However, a month later, it has surged by about 112 per cent to $146.09 per barrel. India imports about 90 per cent of its crude oil, much of it from the Middle East, and a large portion of that supply moves through the Strait. For India, this means not only a higher import bill but also the risk of a sharper decline in the terms-of-trade.

Essentially, the country would need to export more goods and services just to pay for the same amount of energy, which would mean a real income transfer to other countries. This shock comes at a time when the external account is already under pressure. In February 2026, merchandise imports totalled $63.71 billion, while exports were only $36.61 billion, resulting in a monthly deficit of $27.1 billion.

That deficit had already widened before crude prices began to rise, partly due to increased imports of gold and silver. Meanwhile, the rupee explores new lows every other day, increasing the domestic cost of imported oil and further straining the import bill. Foreign investors have also started reacting to these vulnerabilities.

Nearly ₹56,883 crore left Indian equities in the first half of March, adding pressure to the current-account strain. Unless oil prices drop or policy counteracts this cycle, we may see a familiar pattern emerge. The rupee could weaken further, the import bill could rise, and the higher import bill would then further weaken the rupee.

For the RBI, the more complex issue is inflation, as price rises from imported energy behave differently. It affects transport, fertilizer, LPG, and industrial intermediates. This type of inflation is broader and stickier, and it doesn't respond quickly to changes in domestic interest rates. Central banks can often overlook short-term supply shocks.

But a geopolitical oil shock that also weakens the exchange rate provides little comfort. Once currency devaluation influences energy prices, and energy costs drive core inflation, the transmission is already underway. March CPI will likely reflect some of that pressure. Thus, the RBI might face the tough choice of tightening policy amid a slowing investment cycle or holding back and risking a resurgence of inflation.

Some defences That said, India has some defences. Services exports from April to January reached $354 billion, remittances hit $135.4 billion in FY25, and foreign exchange reserves, although below their late-February peak after RBI intervention, remain over $715 billion. These buffers give policymakers some room to act calmly.

Right now, the top policy priority must be household access to energy. An LPG price shock is not distributionally neutral and affects lower-income households the most because cooking fuel is non-discretionary. If crude prices stay high, policymakers need to be clear about who bears the burden. We should avoid the usual habit of hiding costs in oil-marketing companies' balance sheets, as this only delays the necessary adjustments and makes future corrections more complicated.

The second priority should be allowing the exchange rate to adjust. When the current account is under stress, some depreciation of the currency is not a policy failure; it is part of the adjustment. Trying to maintain a fixed nominal rate that the balance of payments can no longer support will simply burn reserves and postpone necessary corrections.
