Title: The bombarding of childhood Part Two

The bombarding of childhood

To develop empathy in the early years, a child must notice that another person feels something different. That small act of crossing over takes time. In this sense, pace is not a minor stylistic feature of children's media. It is one of the ways media imagine the child. Is the child a consumer to be retained, a nervous system to be stimulated, a vessel to be filled with educational content  - or a person whose attention is still forming, and who therefore needs time to arrive?

People who worry about children and screens tend to worry about violent content, advertising, or whether a phonics app calling itself 'educational' truly supports phonics. These are reasonable worries and I share them. But spend five minutes watching what many young children watch, and you will see how fast, loud and bright everything on the screen has become.

It does not matter whether the child is watching on a smartphone in the car or on a television at home. The screen has inherited the adult logic of media design. That logic is, for the most part, the logic of the attention economy. It overwhelms and overshouts, making it easier for the screen to steer the viewer.

The attention economy has introduced a host of small design features for shortening time. The Like button is faster than writing an appreciative comment. A progress bar tells us how many minutes remain in an article before we have decided whether to read to the end. Increasingly, AI appears beside long documents with an offer that sounds helpful but carries a philosophy: this text is long; would you like a quick summary?

The child is swept along by urgency rather than invited to dwell on responsibility or care for the other There are, of course, features that work in the opposite direction. The delayed message and the pop-up that asks: 'Are you sure you want to delete this?' are two examples. Such features interrupt automatic action and return a fragment of judgment to the user.

A nature documentary with linearity and the interpretive presence of an adult voice is a great model for children's media; for example, in the TV show Prehistoric Planet (2022-25), David Attenborough's voiceover and the camera's zoom-ins encourage children to look closely and wonder about unfamiliar life. But these are exceptions within a larger system in which speed is treated as the default. When learning is crowded with content that keeps jumping ahead  -  a crisis appears, a mission begins, a spaceship launches, a door opens  -  the child's attention is restarted as each problem is introduced and immediately solved.

Such media may gesture at teamwork or perseverance, but the speed of the format leaves little time for those lessons to sink in. In the animated TV series Paw Patrol (2013-), for instance, a rescue is clear and reassuring: someone is in trouble, pups assemble, each uses a skill, and order is restored. Yet the movement from problem to rescue to resolution is often so efficient that the child is swept along by urgency rather than invited to dwell on responsibility or care for the other.

Similarly, in the app-based game Hot Wheels Unlimited, building tracks and racing cars may suggest creativity and problem-solving, but the dominant rhythm is acceleration: boost, race, crash, win, unlock, repeat. Research has told us many useful things about children's media. We know that some educational television (like Sesame Street) can support learning.
