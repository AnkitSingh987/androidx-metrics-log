Why Chinese President Xi Jinping's visit to India has drawn considerable attention

Jinping's visit to India has drawn considerable attention China-India ties in renewed state of flux In a month marked by high profile bilateral meets and multilateral exchanges, China's President Xi Jinping's visit to India after seven years has drawn considerable attention. India's engagement with China has always been intriguing, tense, multi-faceted, and yet driven by a sense of pragmatism. As a result, bilateral ties go through frequent highs and lows; while the trade and economic engagement remains deep, China gets very aggressive along the border from time to time and unsettles India by collaborating with Pakistan.

A thaw that came into being four years after the Galwan clash in 2020 has receded. Tensions have hardened on China's provocation and on two fronts. While China regards Arunachal Pradesh as 'South Tibet' and has encroached into a part of the State, it has also entered into a dubious border deal with Pakistan in the PoK region, disregarding India's rightful claim on that area.

China's incursion in Arunachal Pradesh marks a setback to the joint patrolling arrangement agreed upon in October 2024 along the Line of Actual Control. India has unequivocally expressed its displeasure. As reported by this newspaper recently, India has said that it will not provide China any trade or investment concessions unless China stops its aggression at the border.

Coming as it does six months after the government relaxed its Press note 3 curbs on Chinese investment into India, the message is clear. While PN 3 was relaxed in March this year to allow minority Chinese investment of up to 10 per cent, China might have entertained thoughts of further concessions at a time when India is faced with fickle capital flows. The Chinese should know that they cannot access the Indian market by undermining national interest.

The government has taken the right position by linking the border issue with trade and investment ties. A tendency to separate the two gives China the leeway to inflict harm on India. China should be reined in, where managing common waters is concerned. As for leverage, India may be dependent on Chinese intermediates and critical minerals for its electronics, solar, automobiles and pharma sectors, but China ,too, eyes India's large market to offset the paucity of domestic demand back home amidst huge overcapacities.

Yet, with the bilateral trade deficit at $131 billion in FY26, up about 16 per cent from FY25, India must urgently develop domestic capabilities across value chains to deal with a hegemonic neighbour  -  who is also a leading trade partner  -  on our terms. A 40 per cent jump in India's exports to China in April-August FY27 could just be the beginning. Bilateral ties will remain challenging, while being influenced by US actions.

President Trump's weaponisation of tariffs has, in fact, opened up space for making common cause on multilateral issues. China, more than ever, sees itself as a countervailing force, and a voice for the Global South in contested areas of trade and technology. India's multi-alignment would serve it well against the two powers.
