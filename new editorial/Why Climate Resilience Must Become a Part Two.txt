Title: Why Climate Resilience Must Become a Part Two

Why Climate Resilience Must Become a Priority for MSMEs

The first step is improving climate risk intelligence. MSMEs need access to localised climate information, practical risk assessment tools, and simple frameworks that help them understand how floods, heat stress, water scarcity, and other climate related risks affect their operations and supply chains. This will help them plan for business continuity as a standard practice rather than an exception.

Second, resilience must be built into industrial infrastructure. Many adaptation measures are neither technologically complex nor expensive. Improved drainage systems, flood-resilient storage, elevated equipment, cool roofs, better ventilation, insulation, and nature-based cooling solutions can significantly reduce climate-related losses.

Equally important are investments in shared infrastructure within industrial clusters, including drainage networks, water management systems and emergency response facilities. Third, workers must be placed at the centre of climate adaptation strategies. Simple interventions such as easy access to drinking water, shaded rest areas, cooling facilities, heat-health awareness programmes, and flexible work schedules can significantly reduce health risks and productivity losses.

Fourth, financial resilience must be strengthened. MSMEs require better access to emergency credit, resilience-focused lending, climate-responsive insurance products, and innovative risk-transfer mechanisms such as parametric insurance. Dedicated resilience finance windows within MSME support schemes can help enterprises invest in adaptation before disasters occur rather than recovering after losses have already accumulated.

Building Climate-Ready MSMEs Climate resilience cannot be achieved by enterprises acting alone. It requires an enabling ecosystem. Governments must integrate climate resilience into MSME support programmes, industrial policies, labour regulations and infrastructure planning. Industry associations can facilitate knowledge sharing and collective action within industrial clusters while financial institutions can develop products that reward resilience investments.

Large corporations on the other hand can support suppliers through resilience-focused procurement and capacity-building programmes. Most importantly, climate resilience must be recognised not merely as a risk management exercise but as an economic development priority. As India seeks to strengthen its manufacturing base and expand its presence in global supply chains, resilience of its MSMEs will increasingly determine the resilience of the economy.

This is about safeguarding productivity, protecting workers, maintaining competitiveness, and ensuring that the backbone of India&#39;s economy can thrive in a climate-constrained future. (Bhawna Ahuja is a manager, Ananya Chakraborty is a senior research specialist at WRI India and Arivudai Nambi Appadurai is a director at the World Resources Institute India.
