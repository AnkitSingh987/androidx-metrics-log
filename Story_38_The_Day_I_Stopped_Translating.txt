Title: The Day I Stopped Translating

Category: Intermediate

Kenji was twenty-three years old when he finally achieved his dream of moving to Sydney, Australia. Back in his hometown in Japan, he had studied English for more than ten years. He always got excellent grades on his written tests. He knew a massive amount of vocabulary and could explain complicated grammar rules better than some native speakers. Because of this, he expected his life in Sydney to be very easy. However, the reality of living in an English-speaking country hit him very hard.

Kenji had a major problem: he couldn't hold a normal conversation. Whenever someone spoke to him, his brain went through a long, exhausting process. First, he listened to the English sentence. Second, he translated the English sentence into Japanese in his head so he could understand it perfectly. Third, he thought of his answer in Japanese. Finally, he translated his Japanese answer back into English, checked the grammar mentally, and then opened his mouth to speak. 

By the time he finished this four-step process, the person he was talking to had usually changed the subject, walked away, or was looking at him with a confused expression. Kenji’s speech was painfully slow, and he constantly hesitated. He felt like a slow computer trying to run a heavy program.

One bright Saturday morning, Kenji was sitting in a busy coffee shop near the famous Bondi Beach. He was nervously waiting for his new language exchange partner. He had found an advertisement online from an Australian university student named Liam, who wanted to practice Japanese in exchange for helping someone with English.

Liam arrived a few minutes later, wearing a simple t-shirt and carrying a skateboard. He had a big, friendly smile. 
"G'day, mate! You must be Kenji," Liam said, sitting down across from him. "Nice to meet you."

Kenji froze. His brain immediately started working. *G'day mate. Good day friend. You must be Kenji. Anata wa Kenji ni chigainai. Nice to meet you. Hajimemashite.* 
After ten seconds of silence, Kenji finally replied carefully, "Hello. I am Kenji. It is very nice to meet you, too."

Liam chuckled. "Relax, mate! You don't need to be so formal. Let's just grab some coffee and chat."

For the first thirty minutes, they practiced Japanese. Kenji noticed that Liam made a lot of mistakes. His pronunciation was far from perfect, and he often used the wrong verb tense. However, Liam didn't seem to care. He spoke quickly, laughed at his own errors, and used his hands to explain things when he didn't know a word. Liam was communicating successfully, even with bad grammar.

When it was time to switch to English, Kenji immediately felt stressed. Liam started asking him questions about his life in Sydney and his favorite Japanese food. Every time Liam asked a question, Kenji stared at the table, his eyes moving back and forth as he translated everything in his head. 

"Kenji, can I ask you something?" Liam interrupted gently after watching Kenji struggle for a few minutes. "What exactly are you doing in your head before you speak? It looks like you are doing difficult math equations."

Kenji sighed, feeling a bit embarrassed. "I am sorry. I am translating. I think in Japanese, and then I build the English sentence. I want to make sure my grammar is one hundred percent correct before I speak. If I make a mistake, it is embarrassing."

Liam shook his head and took a sip of his coffee. "That is your biggest problem, mate. You are a human, not a dictionary. If you keep translating, you will never be able to speak naturally. You have to stop thinking in Japanese."

"But how?" Kenji asked. "My brain is Japanese. I can't just turn it off."

"You don't turn it off, you just create a new English environment in your head," Liam explained. "Look at that dog over there." Liam pointed to a small brown dog walking outside the window. "When you see that animal, what is the first word that jumps into your mind?"

"Inu," Kenji answered immediately. "The Japanese word for dog."

"Exactly," Liam said. "Your brain connects the image of the animal directly to the Japanese word. You need to train your brain to connect the image directly to the English word 'dog', without going through 'inu' first. You have to attach English words to feelings, images, and situations, not to Japanese translations."

Kenji looked thoughtful. "That sounds incredibly difficult."

"It takes practice," Liam promised. "Start small. When you are walking home, name the things you see around you directly in English. Say 'tree', 'car', 'cloud'. Don't use Japanese. Then, try talking to yourself in English. When you are cooking dinner, describe what you are doing out loud. 'I am cutting the onions. The pan is hot.' It sounds crazy, but it forces your brain to operate in English."

Kenji decided to try Liam’s advice. On his walk home from the coffee shop, he looked at the bright blue sky. Instead of thinking 'sora', he forced himself to think 'sky'. He looked at a bus and thought 'bus'. It felt strange and artificial at first, but he kept trying.

The real challenge began at home. Kenji started narrating his daily life. While making his breakfast, he muttered to himself in English. "I am pouring the milk. The coffee smells good. I am tired today." He realized that when he spoke aloud without a partner, he wasn't afraid of making mistakes. The pressure was gone. 

He also changed his digital environment. He changed his phone's language setting to English. He stopped reading Japanese news and started listening to Australian radio stations while he cleaned his apartment. He let the English language surround him completely. 

A few weeks later, Kenji met Liam at the same coffee shop. Liam had just returned from a surfing trip and started telling an exciting story about seeing a large shark in the water. 

"The water was dark, and suddenly I saw this huge shadow moving under my board," Liam said excitedly, using his hands to show the size of the shark. "I was absolutely terrified!"

Without even thinking, Kenji reacted. "Wow, that is so scary! Did you swim away fast?" 

Liam stopped talking and stared at Kenji with a wide smile. "Did you notice what just happened?"

Kenji blinked, slightly confused. "What?"

"You didn't pause. You didn't stare at the table. You just answered me immediately," Liam laughed happily. "You didn't translate, did you?"

Kenji realized Liam was right. For the very first time, he hadn't translated Liam's English into Japanese. He had heard the English words, imagined the scary shark, felt the emotion, and responded directly in English. The middle step was gone. 

A huge wave of relief washed over Kenji. It wasn't perfect, and he knew he had probably made a small grammar mistake in his response, but it didn't matter. He was actually having a real, natural conversation. 

From that day on, speaking English stopped being a painful mathematical equation for Kenji. It became a bridge to connect with people. He learned that fluency wasn't about having perfect grammar or knowing every word in the dictionary; it was about letting go of his native language and trusting himself to think, feel, and live directly in English.
