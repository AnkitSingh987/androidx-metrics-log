Title: Schooling can put an end to child marriage Part Two

Schooling can put an end to child marriage

Girls from poorer households and those with little or no schooling remain significantly more vulnerable to early marriage than their better-educated peers. India's progress over the past two decades deserves recognition. According to NFHS-5 (2019-21), 23 per cent of women aged 20-24 were married before reaching 18 years of age.

While this figure remains concerning, it represents a substantial decline compared with previous generations. UNICEF describes India as one of the countries that has achieved some of the fastest reductions in child marriage globally, although the pace must accelerate if the Sustainable Development Goal of eliminating child marriage by 2030 is to be achieved. The geographical distribution also reveals important lessons.

More than half of India's child brides are concentrated in five states  -  Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Maharashtra, and Madhya Pradesh. This concentration highlights the close relationship between child marriage, poverty, educational attainment, and regional disparities rather than suggesting that the practice is uniformly prevalent across the country. If history explains why child marriage survived, education explains why it is steadily disappearing.

Education delays marriage by expanding opportunities beyond domestic life. A girl who remains in school is more likely to complete secondary education, pursue higher studies, enter the workforce, marry later, and participate actively in family decision-making. UNICEF notes that child marriage and school dropout frequently occur together, with marriage often marking the end of a girl's education.

Conversely, continued schooling lowers the likelihood of early marriage and creates pathways to better employment and greater financial independence. As Nobel Laureate Amartya Sen has consistently argued, expanding education, particularly for women  -  is not merely a social welfare measure but an investment in human capability that strengthens economic development and democratic participation. India has enacted strong legislation to curb child marriage, beginning with the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929 and later strengthening protections through the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006.

Yet experience shows that law alone cannot transform deeply rooted social practices. Communities abandon long-standing customs when viable alternatives become available. Parents are more willing to postpone their daughters' marriages when schools are accessible, public transport is safe, employment opportunities exist, and educated women become visible role models.

Government initiatives such as Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao, scholarship programmes, residential schools, bicycle distribution schemes, and conditional cash transfers have demonstrated that combining education with economic incentives can alter social behaviour more effectively than punitive measures alone. History should help societies understand their past  -  not imprison their future. India's remarkable decline in child marriage over recent decades demonstrates that deeply rooted social customs can evolve when education, economic opportunity, legal reform, and public awareness advance together.

While challenges remain in certain regions, the direction of change is encouraging. Every additional year a girl spends in school is not merely another academic year; it is an investment in healthier families, stronger communities, a more productive workforce, and a more prosperous nation. In that sense, the classroom has become India's most effective instrument for ending a centuries-old social practice and ensuring that every girl can fulfil her potential.

By P S PARAMESWARAN The writer is an entrepreneur and freelance writer
