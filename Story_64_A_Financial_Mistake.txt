Title: A Financial Mistake

Category: Intermediate

Tom had always wanted to be seen as a successful professional. He was twenty-five years old, and he had recently started a new job as a marketing assistant at a large corporation. The office was modern, his colleagues wore nice clothes, and everyone seemed to have the latest technology. Tom, on the other hand, carried an old, slow laptop and drove a rusty car. He felt like he did not fit in.

One afternoon, Tom was checking his mail when he found an envelope from his bank. Inside was a shiny silver credit card. The letter congratulated him and stated that his credit limit was five thousand dollars. Tom had never owned a credit card before. During university, he had only used a debit card, spending exactly what he had in his account. But now, holding the silver card in his hand, he felt a strange sense of power. Five thousand dollars felt like free money.

"I can finally buy some decent things," he thought to himself. "I have a good job now. I can easily pay it back."

The following Saturday, Tom decided to visit the large tech store in the city center. He only intended to look around, but the bright lights and displays were very tempting. He walked over to the computer section and saw it: the newest, most powerful gaming laptop on the market. It had a beautiful screen, an incredible graphics card, and a sleek, black design. It was exactly what he needed to impress his colleagues and play his favorite video games at the highest settings.

He looked at the price tag. Two thousand, four hundred dollars. It was a massive amount of money. It was more than his monthly salary after taxes. He hesitated for a moment. But then, a friendly salesman approached him.
"It is a beautiful machine, isn't it?" the salesman asked. "It is our best model. If you want it, we have only one left in stock."
Tom felt a rush of adrenaline. He took the silver credit card out of his wallet. "I will take it," he said confidently.

When Tom brought the laptop to the office on Monday, his colleagues were very impressed. He felt proud and successful. For the first few weeks, everything was perfect. He loved the speed of the computer, and he spent all his evenings playing games.

At the end of the month, his first credit card statement arrived in the mail. The balance was $2,400. Below the total balance, there was a smaller number: "Minimum Payment Due: $50."
Tom smiled. "Fifty dollars? That is nothing," he thought. "I can easily afford fifty dollars a month."
He logged into his banking app and paid the fifty dollars. He did not read the fine print on the statement. He did not understand how credit card interest worked. He thought he was being responsible by making the minimum payment on time.

Because paying the bill seemed so easy, Tom continued to use his credit card. He used it to buy expensive lunches with his coworkers, new shoes, and video games. Every month, the statement arrived, and every month, he only paid the minimum amount. He did not notice that his total balance was not getting smaller. In fact, it was slowly getting bigger.

Six months later, Tom received a new statement. He opened the envelope and froze. His total balance was now three thousand, two hundred dollars.
He stared at the paper in shock. How was this possible? He had paid the minimum amount every single month. He grabbed his calculator and added up the things he had bought. The laptop was $2,400. The lunches and clothes were maybe $400. That was $2,800. Where did the extra four hundred dollars come from?

The next day at work, Tom looked stressed and tired. His colleague, Lisa, noticed that he was staring blankly at his computer screen. Lisa was older than Tom and worked in the finance department.
"Are you okay, Tom?" she asked, putting a cup of coffee on his desk. "You look like you have seen a ghost."
Tom hesitated, but he was desperate for advice. He trusted Lisa. "I think I made a terrible financial mistake," he whispered. He told her about the credit card, the expensive laptop, the shopping, and the mysterious growing balance.

Lisa pulled up a chair and sat next to him. She looked serious but kind. "Tom, did you look at the interest rate on that credit card?"
"Interest rate?" Tom asked in confusion. "I don't know. I just paid the minimum payment they asked for."
Lisa sighed gently. "That is how the credit card companies make their money, Tom. When you only pay the minimum, you are mostly just paying the interest for that month. You are not actually reducing the original debt. Let's look at your statement."

Tom showed her the paper. Lisa pointed to a small section at the bottom. "Look here. Your annual interest rate is twenty-two percent. That is extremely high. Because you carried a large balance from month to month, they charged you interest on the whole amount. The interest was added to your debt, and then they charged you interest on the interest. It is a trap that many young people fall into."

Tom felt a heavy weight on his chest. He felt incredibly stupid. The laptop was not a symbol of success; it was a symbol of his foolishness. "What do I do, Lisa? I don't have three thousand dollars to pay this off."

"First, you have to stop using the credit card completely," Lisa advised firmly. "Take it out of your wallet and put it in a drawer at home. Second, you need to create a strict budget. You have to cut all unnecessary expenses. No more expensive lunches, no more new clothes. Every extra dollar you earn must go towards paying off this debt."

Tom nodded slowly. "I can do that. But it will take me years to pay it off if the interest keeps growing."
"That brings me to the hard part," Lisa said gently. "Do you really need that expensive gaming laptop for your work?"
Tom looked at the sleek black machine on his desk. "No," he admitted. "I mostly just use it for emails, spreadsheets, and video games."
"Then you should sell it," Lisa suggested. "It is only six months old. You can probably sell it online for one thousand, five hundred dollars, maybe a bit more. If you use that money to make a large payment on your card, it will reduce the principal debt significantly. Then, the interest you are charged every month will be much smaller."

The thought of selling his beautiful laptop hurt. He had bought it to impress people. But looking at the massive debt on his statement, he knew Lisa was right. His pride had caused this mistake, and he had to swallow his pride to fix it.

That weekend, Tom cleaned the laptop, took good photos of it, and listed it for sale online. A week later, he sold it to a university student for $1,600. He immediately transferred the entire amount to his credit card company.

He bought a cheap, used laptop for $200 just to do his basic tasks. He also started bringing simple sandwiches to work instead of going to restaurants. Whenever his colleagues asked why he was not joining them for lunch, he honestly replied, "I am paying off some debt." To his surprise, no one judged him. In fact, a few colleagues admitted that they were also struggling with credit card payments.

It took Tom another eight months of strict budgeting and hard work to finally pay off the remaining balance. When he made the final payment and saw his balance hit zero, he felt a massive wave of relief. He opened the drawer, took out the shiny silver credit card, and cut it into pieces with scissors.

He had learned a difficult and expensive lesson. He realized that true financial success was not about having expensive things to impress others. It was about living within his means, understanding the true cost of borrowing money, and never spending money he did not actually have. From that day on, Tom only paid in cash or with his debit card, and he promised himself he would never fall into the debt trap again.
