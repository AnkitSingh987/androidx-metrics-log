Title: Learning from a Mistake
Category: Beginner

It is Monday morning. The mathematics class is very quiet. The students are working in their notebooks. Mr. Thomas, the math teacher, is walking around the room. He is looking at the students' work. 
Emma is sitting in the second row. She is eleven years old. She is usually very good at math, but today they are learning something new. They are learning about fractions. Fractions are difficult for Emma. She looks at the numbers on the board, and she feels confused. 

Mr. Thomas wrote five problems on the blackboard. Emma is working on problem number three. 
She thinks she knows the answer. She writes the numbers quickly. She draws a line and writes her final answer at the bottom of the page. She closes her notebook. She is the first student to finish. 
She sits back in her chair and smiles. She feels very confident. 

Mr. Thomas walks to Emma's desk. 
"Are you finished, Emma?" he asks softly. 
"Yes, Mr. Thomas," Emma says proudly. "I finished all five problems."
"Let me see your work," the teacher says. 

He picks up her notebook. He looks at the first problem. He nods his head. "Good," he says. 
He looks at the second problem. "Very good," he says. 
Then, he looks at the third problem. He stops nodding. He looks closely at the numbers. Emma's smile slowly disappears. 
"Emma," Mr. Thomas says gently. "Look at problem number three again. I think you made a mistake here."

Emma feels a sudden heat in her cheeks. Her face becomes red. She does not like making mistakes. She wants to be perfect. 
She takes the notebook and looks at the problem. She looks at the numbers, but she still does not see the mistake. 
"I don't understand," she whispers. "I followed the rule."

"You followed the rule for addition," Mr. Thomas explains. "But this problem is subtraction. Look at the sign in the middle. It is a minus sign, not a plus sign."
Emma looks closely at the board. Mr. Thomas is right. It is a minus sign. She read the problem too fast. She added the numbers instead of subtracting them. 
She feels very embarrassed. Some students are looking at her. She wants to hide under her desk. 

"I am sorry, Mr. Thomas," she says. "I was stupid."
Mr. Thomas kneels down next to her desk. He looks at her very seriously. 
"Emma, listen to me," he says. "You are not stupid. You are a very smart girl. Making a mistake does not mean you are stupid."
Emma looks down at her hands. "But I got it wrong," she says. 

"Everyone gets things wrong sometimes," Mr. Thomas says. "I make mistakes too. Do you know why mistakes are important?"
Emma shakes her head. "No. I think mistakes are bad."
"Mistakes are not bad," he explains kindly. "Mistakes are how we learn. When you get everything right, you do not learn anything new. But when you make a mistake, you must stop. You must think about what went wrong. And then, you learn how to do it correctly next time."

Emma thinks about his words. "So, mistakes help us?" she asks. 
"Yes, exactly," Mr. Thomas smiles. "Now you know that you must look carefully at the signs before you start a fraction problem. You will not make this same mistake again, will you?"
"No, I will not," Emma says. "I will read the problems very carefully from now on."

"Good," Mr. Thomas says. He taps her notebook with his finger. "So, do not be sad. Just take your eraser, rub out the wrong answer, and try again. You can do it."
He stands up and walks to the next student. 

Emma feels much better. The red heat in her face goes away. Mr. Thomas is right. She just read the sign wrong because she was rushing. 
She picks up her pink eraser. She rubs out her wrong answer carefully. She blows the eraser dust off the page. 
Then, she takes her pencil. She looks at the minus sign on the blackboard. She thinks about the rule for subtraction. She writes the new numbers. She calculates the answer. 

When she is finished, she looks at her work. This time, she is sure it is correct. 
She raises her hand. 
Mr. Thomas comes back to her desk. He looks at problem number three. He smiles broadly. 
"Perfect," he says. "You fixed it. Well done, Emma."

"Thank you, Mr. Thomas," she says happily. 
She finishes the last two problems without rushing. She checks the signs carefully. 
Emma learned an important lesson today in math class. She learned about fractions, but she also learned about mistakes. A mistake is not a terrible thing. It is just a stepping stone on the path to learning. And trying again is the most important part of the journey.
