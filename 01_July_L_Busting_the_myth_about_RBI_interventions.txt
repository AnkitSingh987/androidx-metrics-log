Title: Busting the myth about RBI interventions

Category: Editorial

The genesis of the myth stems from a fundamental misunderstanding of the RBI's stated policy. Unlike central banks in export-driven economies that explicitly peg their currencies or aggressively undervalue them to maintain a competitive trade advantage, the RBI officially adheres to a policy of a "managed float." Under this regime, the value of the Rupee is primarily determined by the invisible hand of market forces—the intersection of demand and supply dynamics dictated by trade flows, capital movements, and global macroeconomic sentiment. The central bank’s interventions in the spot and forward markets are not designed to subvert these fundamental market forces or to defend a predetermined, sacrosanct exchange rate target. Instead, the RBI's interventions are explicitly calibrated to address a singular, specific objective: the mitigation of excessive volatility.

Volatility, in the context of foreign exchange markets, refers to rapid, erratic, and unpredictable fluctuations in the currency's value. While a gradual depreciation or appreciation, reflecting underlying economic fundamentals such as inflation differentials or current account dynamics, is a natural and necessary adjustment mechanism, sudden and violent swings can be highly destabilizing. Excessive volatility engenders profound uncertainty, hindering the ability of importers and exporters to price their goods competitively and manage their hedging strategies effectively. It can trigger panic among foreign portfolio investors, precipitating abrupt capital flights that further exacerbate currency depreciation. Moreover, a rapidly depreciating Rupee can quickly pass through to domestic inflation, particularly given India's structural reliance on imported hydrocarbons, thereby complicating the RBI's primary mandate of price stability.

Therefore, when the RBI wades into the foreign exchange market to buy or sell US Dollars, its objective is akin to that of a shock absorber, smoothing out the jagged edges of market movements rather than dictating the direction of the vehicle. If the Rupee is depreciating precipitously due to exogenous shocks—such as a sudden tightening of monetary policy by the US Federal Reserve or an unexpected geopolitical crisis—the RBI may deploy its foreign exchange reserves to supply Dollars, thereby absorbing the excess demand and preventing a chaotic freefall. Conversely, during periods of exuberant capital inflows that threaten to overvalue the Rupee and erode export competitiveness, the RBI may intervene to purchase Dollars, sterilizing the inflows and accumulating reserves to build a strategic buffer for future exigencies.

The efficacy and limitations of these interventions must also be understood within the context of the Impossible Trinity, a foundational concept in international economics. The trilemma posits that a nation cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy. India has explicitly chosen to prioritize an independent monetary policy—crucial for domestic inflation targeting—and allows for a relatively open capital account. Consequently, it must logically relinquish absolute control over its exchange rate. The RBI’s immense, yet finite, foreign exchange reserves cannot indefinitely defy sustained, fundamental market pressures. Any attempt to artificially defend an unsustainable exchange rate against the tide of global capital flows would not only result in a rapid depletion of reserves but also invite speculative attacks, ultimately culminating in a more severe and disruptive currency crisis.

Furthermore, the persistent narrative that the RBI’s interventions are aimed at artificially inflating the value of the Rupee ignores the central bank’s historical tolerance for gradual depreciation. Over the long term, the Rupee has steadily depreciated against the US Dollar, a trajectory that accurately reflects the persistent inflation differentials between the two economies. If the RBI were truly obsessed with maintaining an artificially strong currency, this secular trend of depreciation would not have materialized. The central bank’s actions are consistently aligned with the philosophy of allowing the currency to find its fundamental value, albeit in an orderly and non-disruptive manner.

In conclusion, the portrayal of the Reserve Bank of India as a heavy-handed manipulator of the exchange rate is a reductive myth that obfuscates the sophisticated and pragmatic nature of its foreign exchange operations. The RBI's interventions are not an exercise in defying economic gravity, but rather a essential mechanism for managing the risks inherent in an increasingly integrated and volatile global financial system. By focusing on mitigating excessive volatility and ensuring orderly market conditions, the central bank provides a crucial public good: macroeconomic stability. Understanding the nuanced distinction between managing volatility and targeting a specific exchange rate is indispensable for an accurate appraisal of India’s monetary policy and its navigation of the complex currents of the global economy.
