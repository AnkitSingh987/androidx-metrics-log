Title: BRICS and the future of crossborder payments Part One

BRICS, and the future of cross-border payments

The best way forward is for the bloc to provide a broad template, even as the details are worked out bilaterally The idea that BRICS countries should be able to pay each other without routing every transaction through the US dollar is older than most people assume. The Ufa declaration of 2015 asked member governments to examine wider use of national currencies in mutual trade. A Payment Task Force of BRICS central banks was set up in 2020.

Johannesburg in 2023 asked finance ministers to report on local currencies, payment instruments and platforms; Kazan in 2024 named the effort the BRICS Cross-Border Payments Initiative; Rio in 2025 received the task force's first technical report. Over the same period, Russia, cut off from SWIFT after 2022, moved most of its BRICS trade into national currencies, while President Trump threatened tariffs on any move towards a BRICS currency. India has been consistent that it is not pursuing de-dollarisation, and MEA reiterated after the summit that there is no proposal for a BRICS currency.

Gets more attention Why, then, did payments get more attention in New Delhi than at any earlier summit? Three things have changed. First, the volumes. Intra-BRICS exports reached $1.17 trillion in 2024, 13 times the 2003 level (UNCTAD). India's own trade with BRICS members crossed $417 billion in FY26, with a deficit of about $226 billion, half of it with China (GTRI).

Second, the friction is now measured. The Financial Stability Board's 2025 report on the G20 cross-border payments roadmap found fewer than 45 per cent of business-to-business payments credited within a business day globally, and for South Asia, not a single B2B service crediting funds within an hour. These delays come from correspondent banking chains, batch processing across time zones and compliance checks that remain largely manual, not from distance.

Third, every BRICS member now runs a fast payment system at population scale: UPI, Brazil's Pix, Russia's SBP, Indonesia's QRIS and the UAE's Aani. There is finally something to connect, and the Prime Minister said at Global Fintech Fest on September 8 that connecting UPI with other countries' payment systems should be India's next goal. Against this backdrop, the New Delhi Declaration's payments paragraph is careful and deliberately so.

It acknowledges the task force's work on interoperability of payment and messaging channels, encourages more trade settlement in local currencies while respecting national priorities, and notes that "there is no one-size-fits-all approach". It asks the task force to keep working towards solutions that are fast, low-cost, accessible, transparent and safe. There is no BRICS payment system, no common settlement currency and no timeline.

The settlement and depositary infrastructure Russia has proposed at three successive summits is recorded as the subject of a technical workshop rather than a decision. Jaipur Consensus The finance track did move in adjacent areas. Finance ministers and central bank governors met in Jaipur in August and Mumbai this month.

The BRICS Multilateral Guarantees initiative moves to pilot transactions. The Jaipur Consensus asks members to study an invoice-discounting mechanism for MSME exporters. The New Development Bank, which has approved over $40 billion of projects in its first decade, has been asked to expand local currency lending.

On the sidelines, Russia's BRICS Pay signed an agreement with RDIF and an Indian entity to explore connecting UPI with Russian payment rails, a private initiative to which NPCI and the RBI are not party. It is tempting to read this as a summit that promised more than it delivered. I would read it differently.
