In absolute terms, the real GDP rose to ₹48.63 lakh crore, compared with ₹44.94 lakh crore in the corresponding July-September quarter of the previous fiscal year. This acceleration was accompanied by a rebound in domestic consumption, strong industrial output, and continued momentum in services. The robust quarterly performance reinforces India's position as one of the world's fastest-growing major economies.

Several sectors contributed to this outcome. The services sector, especially financial, communication, transport, and real estate services, remained central to the expansion. Manufacturing grew by roughly 9%, supported by higher domestic demand and better industrial capacity utilisation. Construction activity expanded due to major infrastructure projects and a revival in real estate investment.

These sectoral trends suggest progress not only in cyclical recovery but also in policy-driven reorientation. Private consumption showed some signs of recovery, aided by stable inflation. Government expenditure, while increasing more moderately, continued to support aggregate demand. Investment activity, reflected in gross fixed capital formation, expanded steadily, suggesting a gradual strengthening of capital expenditure.

The sector-wise growth rates clarifies the pattern of growth. Agriculture and allied activities expanded at an estimated 3.5% year-on-year, reflecting sluggish performance influenced by monsoon variability and structural constraints. Manufacturing, on the other hand, grew by 9.1%, driven by improved industrial output and domestic demand.

Construction posted a slower growth of 7.2% mainly supported by ongoing infrastructure expansion. Among service activities, trade, hotels, transport, and communication posted a yearly growth rate of 7.4% due to increased logistics activity. Financial, real estate, and professional services continued their strong performance with yearly growth of 10.2 %, supported by credit expansion and vibrant capital-market activity.

Despite the optimistic headline figure, the 8.2% growth rate attracts some caution. First, the growth rate must be interpreted cautiously due to the base effect. The previous year's economic activity was relatively subdued with real growth rates of 6.5% in quarter one and 5.6% in quarter two of 2024-25.

When current output is measured against a weak base, even moderate improvements can create the appearance of stronger-than-substantive growth. Therefore, the sustainability of the observed acceleration requires some time to stabilise. Second, there are a set of concerns relating to methodological issues in national income accounting, particularly the use of GDP deflators.

Critics argue that if the deflator underestimates true inflation  -  especially in the services sector, where price measurement is challenging  -  the real GDP growth may be overstated. Divergences between consumer price trends and wholesale indices raise questions about the accuracy of deflators and, by extension, real output estimates. Third pertains to the composition and drivers of growth.

Much of the recent expansion appears dependent on government capital expenditure and consumption, rather than on private-sector investment as a 31% rise in the government's capital expenditure in July-September helped push up the Gross Fixed Capital Formation by 7.3%. While this was down from a growth of 7.8% in April-June, it was higher than an increase of 6.7% in the second quarter of 2024-25. Heavy reliance on government expenditure raises concerns about fiscal sustainability if revenue growth does not keep pace with rising commitments.

Such a scenario may also crowd out private investment in the medium term, affecting the durability of growth. Slower nominal growth The nominal, or non-inflation-adjusted, growth rate of the GDP is a cause of some concern for macroeconomic policy. It is 8.7% for the quarter ending September 2025 and 8.8% for the first half of the fiscal year.
