Title: On fertiliser Centre and states must align policy

Category: Editorial

The genesis of the current crisis lies in the highly distorted subsidy regime administered by the central government. For decades, the Centre has heavily subsidized urea, ensuring that its retail price is kept artificially low—often significantly below the cost of production and international market prices. Conversely, the subsidies for complex fertilizers containing Phosphorus (P) and Potassium (K) are governed by the Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) scheme, allowing their retail prices to fluctuate in response to market dynamics. This extreme pricing asymmetry has fundamentally corrupted the agronomic behavior of Indian farmers. Driven by economic rationality rather than soil health requirements, farmers disproportionately apply the hyper-subsidized urea while chronically under-utilizing essential P and K fertilizers. This practice has severely skewed the Nitrogen-Phosphorus-Potassium (N-P-K) ratio in the soil, leading to rampant nutrient imbalance, soil degradation, and a paradoxical situation where increased fertilizer application yields diminishing marginal returns.

The ecological consequences of this urea-centric approach are devastating. The excessive application of nitrogenous fertilizers results in severe environmental externalities, primarily the contamination of groundwater resources with toxic nitrates and the emission of nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. Furthermore, the systematic depletion of micronutrients from the soil, a direct consequence of unbalanced fertilization, is manifesting in declining crop productivity and compromised nutritional quality of the agricultural output. This ecological degradation not only threatens immediate food security but also fundamentally undermines the sustainability of the agrarian ecosystem for future generations.

The administrative architecture governing fertilizer distribution exacerbates this structural crisis. While the central government determines the overarching subsidy framework and imports, agriculture remains a state subject under the Indian Constitution. The states are responsible for assessing the localized demand for fertilizers, managing intra-state distribution networks, and executing extension services to educate farmers on optimal agronomic practices. However, there is a profound systemic disconnect between these two tiers of governance. State governments frequently prioritize short-term political dividends, demanding excessive allocations of urea from the Centre to appease their rural constituencies, rather than promoting the use of complex fertilizers or organic alternatives. The lack of robust, localized soil-testing infrastructure and the failure of state extension services to effectively communicate the dangers of unbalanced fertilization further compound the problem.

Aligning the policies of the Centre and the states requires a comprehensive, multi-pronged strategy that dismantles the current perverse incentive structures. The most critical intervention must be the immediate rationalization of urea pricing. The political apprehension surrounding any increase in urea prices must be overcome; the artificial depression of its cost is the root cause of the crisis. Bringing urea under the ambit of the Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) scheme, or gradually phasing out the blanket subsidy in favor of targeted direct benefit transfers (DBT) to small and marginal farmers, is essential to restore pricing parity and incentivize the balanced application of all primary macronutrients.

Concurrently, the central government must incentivize the states to actively promote sustainable agricultural practices. This can be achieved by linking central financial assistance or fertilizer allocations to verifiable improvements in state-level soil health indicators and the adoption of balanced fertilization techniques. The states, in turn, must radically revamp their agricultural extension services, moving away from a prescriptive, top-down approach toward a participatory model that relies on extensive soil-testing networks. The distribution of 'Soil Health Cards' must transcend a mere bureaucratic exercise; the data must be actively utilized to formulate localized, crop-specific fertilizer recommendations, and farmers must be educated on interpreting and applying this data.

Furthermore, aligning policy necessitates a coordinated push toward alternative, sustainable sources of plant nutrition. Both the Centre and the states must aggressively promote the production and adoption of bio-fertilizers, organic manure, and nano-fertilizers. Subsidizing the establishment of localized composting units and integrating organic farming practices into the mainstream agricultural policy framework are crucial steps toward reducing the systemic reliance on imported, energy-intensive chemical fertilizers.

In conclusion, the current trajectory of fertilizer usage in India is ecologically catastrophic and economically unsustainable. The profound misalignment between the central subsidy regime and state-level agricultural priorities has trapped the Indian farmer in a vicious cycle of soil degradation and diminishing returns. Escaping this agrarian trap demands immediate, coordinated action. The Centre and the states must recognize that securing India's food security is not contingent on flooding the fields with cheap urea, but on restoring the fundamental health of the soil. Aligning fertilizer policy to reflect agronomic reality rather than political expediency is not merely a bureaucratic reform; it is an existential imperative for the survival and prosperity of Indian agriculture.
