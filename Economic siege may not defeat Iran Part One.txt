Title: Economic siege may not defeat Iran Part One

Economic siege may not defeat Iran

The United States' new economic offensive against Iran may prove less decisive than Washington hopes. The United States' new economic offensive against Iran may prove less decisive than Washington hopes. On August 24, the U.S. announced "Operation Economic Outcast", imposing sanctions on 60 individuals, entities and ships across five sectors of Iran's economy, from digital assets to shipping.

The move seeks concessions without opening another military front. But can economic pressure achieve what military force and diplomacy have failed to deliver: a negotiated settlement of Iran's nuclear programme? Under U.S. sanctions since 1979, Iran has built mechanisms to circumvent them. China is the crucial example.

Despite American sanctions, it buys about 90 per cent of Iran's crude exports, often at a discount of about $12 a barrel. Iran also relies on shadow banks and tankers. The damage is severe. The rial has weakened sharply since January. War damage to industry and refineries has contributed to fuel shortages, while inflation has reached 88 per cent.

Iranian oil exports have fallen to a new low. Tehran has about 80 million barrels outside Hormuz, but sanctions make them difficult to monetise. Yet greater pressure may produce diminishing returns. Even if Washington imposes secondary sanctions on Chinese banks, Iran could use its own payment systems for oil transactions.

Beijing has warned that the new sanctions threaten global growth and financial stability and has vowed to protect its legitimate interests. Washington will therefore be cautious about confronting China. Beijing's restriction of rare-earth supplies last year demonstrated the potential cost of economic retaliation and was followed by President Donald Trump's effort to improve relations with China.

This exposes a contradiction in the American strategy. Washington wants to choke Iran's economy without turning the confrontation into a wider contest with China. Aggressive pressure on Chinese institutions risks turning the crisis into another arena of U.S.-China rivalry. Iran believes the political clock favours it.

Only 31 per cent of Americans, according to the latest survey, support continued military action against Iran. With the November midterm elections approaching, Trump has strong incentive to end the confrontation without appearing to concede defeat. Tehran understands this constraint. Keeping oil prices above $80 could increase pressure on the White House through higher energy costs.

Hormuz provides Iran another source of leverage. Tehran has been close to an arrangement with Oman under which ships crossing the strait would pay tolls. This could increase pressure on Washington to enforce its embargo. But further U.S. attacks could damage American credibility without producing a decisive result.

The central unresolved issue is nuclear. Iran's nuclear programme and stockpile of fissile material remain substantially intact. When Trump launched "Epic Fury", the expectation was that military pressure would weaken the Iranian regime and compel it to abandon its nuclear ambitions. When that failed, Washington offered sanctions relief and billions of dollars in investment, hoping Tehran would open Hormuz and give up its nuclear programme.

That too failed. The economic offensive is now being used to achieve what force and inducements have not. The June MOU was significant. It offered Iran an end to U.S. opposition, protection for its proxies, sanctions relief, the unfreezing of oil revenues and $300 billion for reconstruction. In return, Iran would have had to eschew its nuclear programme and open Hormuz.

President Masoud Pezeshkian wants Washington to return to the understanding. It demonstrates that a negotiated exit was possible. The difficulty is that Tehran now believes its bargaining position has improved. The Yemen front strengthens that perception. The Houthis have attacked Saudi ports, airports and refineries.
