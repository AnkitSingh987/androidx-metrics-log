{"Title":"The Two-Year Threshold: How a Quiet Amendment Reshapes Who Owns Their Own Birth","Content":"Parliament rarely legislates on the mundane machinery of paperwork, yet the Registration of Births and Deaths (Amendment) Bill 2026, passed in the Lok Sabha, does precisely that, and in doing so it **amends** Section 13(3) of the 1969 Act with consequences far beyond the registrar's office. Under the updated **framework**, registrations delayed for up to two years still require an order from a district, subdivisional, or authorised executive magistrate. But once the delay exceeds two years, the law now demands that a judicial magistrate personally verify the event before passing an order. This may sound like a technical recalibration, yet it arrives atop an earlier and more consequential shift: the 2023 amendment had already **rendered** birth certificates the principal, near-**conclusive** proof of date and place of birth for school admission, voter rolls, and applications for passport, Aadhaar, driving licence, and government jobs. It also obliged the Centre and States to jointly build digital databases, and required them to share that data with the Registrar General. As the certificate's gatekeeping power over civic life grew, so, inevitably, did the incentive to obtain one **fraudulently** — and it is this rising temptation that the 2026 amendment is designed to counter.\n\nThe Bill's passage was itself shadowed by controversy. It cleared the Lok Sabha without meaningful debate, since the Opposition was at the time protesting the July 20 police **crackdown** on the CJP protests, leaving the chamber's usual scrutiny function largely unexercised. This matters because identity documents have grown steadily more politically **sensitive**, and shifting potentially **contentious** verification cases into the judiciary **insulates** the executive from **accusations** that it is **arbitrarily** granting or withholding documentation. Judicial oversight, in other words, places the decision at arm's length from bureaucratic discretion. Yet this same distancing carries a cost: when a judicial magistrate errs, the error may prove considerably harder for a disadvantaged applicant to have corrected, given the comparative formality, expense, and inaccessibility of court proceedings relative to an executive magistrate's office.\n\nWhat the amendment conspicuously does not do is touch the underlying **evidentiary** rules. If the government's actual concern was that executive magistrates were approving **suspicious** applications because the standard of proof required was too **lax**, the more direct remedy would have been to **prescribe** more **rigorous** documentation at the point of application. It has not done so. Nor has it explained, with published data, why the risk of **fraudulent** registrations should be presumed to rise specifically after the two-year mark, or whether it examined less **burdensome** alternatives before settling on judicial **intervention** as the threshold response. For now, the two-year cutoff reads less like an evidence-driven calculation than a **compromise**, possibly reflecting an assumption that shorter delays are common enough to be resolved through ordinary administrative channels, while longer ones warrant judicial weight.\n\nThis assumption, however, glosses over the many ordinary and entirely legitimate reasons registrations fall behind. Births frequently **transpire** in remote areas with no easy access to a registration office; undocumented migrants often only recognise the need for an educational certificate years after a child's birth; families in flux, displacement, or poverty may simply lack the means to register promptly. As identity documents become **indispensable** to accessing state support — rations, schooling, employment, banking — the government's policy **instinct** has consistently been to raise the level of assurance attached to these documents rather than to make the underlying registration process more **accessible**. In that same spirit, this amendment may reflect a broader **bureaucratic** philosophy: one that, in the pursuit of administrative certainty, treats the wrongful **exclusion** of **legitimate** citizens as an acceptable price for minimising the risk of wrongful inclusion of the fraudulent. Whether that trade-off is genuinely backed by sufficient evidence remains unclear — and it is precisely this question that the Rajya Sabha must now
