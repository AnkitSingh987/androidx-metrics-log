Title: Resource nationalism and the green transition

Category: Editorial

The structural vulnerability of the green energy supply chain lies in its extreme, terrifying geographic concentration, far surpassing the historic concentration of the global oil market. While oil reserves are relatively broadly distributed across the Middle East, the Americas, and Russia, the extraction and, more crucially, the complex chemical processing of critical transition minerals are dominated by a remarkably small handful of nations. The Democratic Republic of Congo controls the overwhelming majority of global cobalt extraction; a "lithium triangle" spanning Chile, Argentina, and Bolivia dominates lithium reserves; and Indonesia exercises immense leverage over the global nickel supply. Even more alarmingly for Western policymakers, the highly specialized, capital-intensive refining and processing of virtually all these critical minerals is overwhelmingly, almost monopolistically, dominated by a single geopolitical adversary: China. This profound structural asymmetry creates immense, unprecedented leverage for the nations that control these critical nodes.

Recognizing the immense strategic and economic power conferred by their geological endowments, governments in resource-rich nations are aggressively abandoning the laissez-faire, export-oriented models of the past two decades. They are increasingly deploying the aggressive instruments of resource nationalism to maximize state revenue and force domestic industrialization. This manifests through a spectrum of coercive policies: imposing punitive export taxes on raw, unprocessed ores; mandating that foreign mining conglomerates surrender majority ownership to state-run enterprises; and, most drastically, enacting outright export bans on raw minerals to force multinational corporations to build higher-value processing and battery manufacturing facilities within their borders. Indonesia’s successful, albeit highly disruptive, ban on the export of raw nickel ore serves as a potent template, demonstrating how a developing nation can leverage critical mineral dominance to aggressively move up the global value chain.

For the advanced economies of the Global North, particularly the United States and the European Union, this resurgence of resource nationalism presents a profound, arguably existential, strategic nightmare. Their ambitious, legally mandated climate targets are completely unachievable without massive, uninterrupted access to these specific minerals. The realization that their entire green industrial strategy—and by extension, their future economic competitiveness—is entirely dependent on complex, highly vulnerable supply chains controlled by geopolitical rivals or volatile, resource-nationalist states has triggered immense panic. In response, Western governments are desperately attempting to execute a rapid "de-risking" strategy. This involves massive, state-subsidized efforts to rapidly develop domestic mining capabilities, aggressively seek alternative, less politically volatile supply chains in allied nations ("friend-shoring"), and heavily invest in recycling technologies to reduce reliance on virgin resource extraction.

However, this frantic global scramble is generating immense friction and profound environmental contradictions. The aggressive push to rapidly open new mines in the West, often bypassing stringent environmental regulations in the name of national security, frequently triggers fierce opposition from local communities and environmental groups, highlighting the deeply uncomfortable reality that the green transition necessitates massive, highly destructive industrial extraction. Furthermore, the aggressive subsidies and protectionist policies deployed by major powers to secure their own supply chains threaten to spark a ruinous global trade war, severely fragmenting the global economy and driving up the overall cost of the climate transition precisely when massive, rapid deployment is most critical.

In conclusion, the green transition is not a harmonious march towards a post-scarcity utopia; it is a brutal, intensely competitive struggle for control over the critical physical inputs of the twenty-first-century economy. Resource nationalism is the inevitable, rational response of developing nations seeking to finally leverage their geological wealth for genuine economic development, rather than remaining mere extraction colonies for the Global North. The global community must abandon the naive assumption that the free market will seamlessly manage this transition. Navigating this dangerous era requires the establishment of new, robust multilateral frameworks governing critical minerals—ensuring equitable access, establishing stringent, universally enforced environmental and labor standards for extraction, and providing mechanisms to share the economic benefits of processing more equitably with the Global South. If the world fails to manage the geopolitics of the green transition, the fight to save the climate will inevitably degenerate into a chaotic, zero-sum resource war.
