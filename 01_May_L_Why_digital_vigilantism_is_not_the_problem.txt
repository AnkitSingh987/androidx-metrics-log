Title: Why digital vigilantism is not the problem

Category: Editorial

The term "vigilantism" historically connotes the extrajudicial execution of justice, where individuals or mobs usurp the punitive functions of the state, often through the application of physical violence. Digital exposure, however, rarely involves the application of violence or the direct imposition of penalties. Instead, it operates on the mechanism of public visibility and reputational consequence. When a citizen uploads a video of a municipal contractor utilizing substandard materials, or a powerful politician flouting pandemic regulations, they are not executing justice; they are demanding accountability. They are leveraging the only power available to the marginalized—the power of the collective gaze—to force unresponsive institutions to acknowledge and address malfeasance. Equating the recording of a crime with the commission of violence is a deliberate rhetorical strategy employed by the state to delegitimize public scrutiny and maintain its monopoly on narrative control.

The necessity for this digital mobilization is rooted in the profound power asymmetry that defines the interaction between the Indian citizen and the state apparatus. The formal mechanisms for grievance redressal are notoriously labyrinthine, intimidating, and structurally biased against the common citizen. Filing a complaint against a corrupt official or a brutal police officer through official channels often results in endless bureaucratic obfuscation, targeted harassment, or the systemic protection of the accused by their institutional peers. The formal justice system, crippled by astronomical pendency rates and prohibitive costs, offers little hope for timely intervention. In this environment of pervasive institutional failure, the viral video serves as the ultimate, and often the only, effective catalyst for action. It forces the hand of the administration by generating irresistible public pressure, compelling institutions to act not out of a commitment to justice, but out of a desperate need for damage control.

Furthermore, the condemnation of digital vigilantism often relies on a deeply hypocritical invocation of privacy rights. State actors, who routinely advocate for the ubiquitous deployment of facial recognition technology and massive CCTV surveillance networks with minimal oversight, suddenly become fervent champions of privacy when a citizen's smartphone is pointed in their direction. The principle of privacy cannot be selectively weaponized to shield public servants from accountability while simultaneously subjugating the citizenry to pervasive state surveillance. When an individual engages in official duties or operates within the public sphere, particularly when exercising state power, their expectation of privacy is necessarily and legitimately diminished in the interest of transparency.

This is not to suggest that the phenomenon of digital exposure is without significant risks. The architecture of social media is inherently prone to algorithmic amplification of outrage, lacking the nuance, due process, and evidentiary rigor required for fair adjudication. Instances of misidentification, the deliberate manipulation of video footage through editing or deepfake technology, and the potential for devastating, disproportionate reputational ruin are real and alarming challenges. The transition from public accountability to mob harassment is a perilous boundary that is easily crossed in the digital realm.

However, the solution to these risks is not the heavy-handed suppression of citizen documentation or the criminalization of digital exposure. The remedy lies in addressing the root cause: the systemic accountability deficit of state institutions. If the police functioned transparently, if municipal corporations were responsive to formal complaints, and if the judicial system delivered timely justice, the necessity for citizens to resort to viral shaming would evaporate. The prevalence of digital exposure is a direct indictment of institutional efficacy.

In conclusion, framing citizen documentation as "digital vigilantism" is a deliberate misdiagnosis designed to protect entrenched power structures from public scrutiny. It is an act of democratic participation born out of institutional despair. Rather than silencing the citizen with the smartphone, the state must focus on fixing the broken systems that compel the citizen to hit the 'record' button in the first place. Until the formal mechanisms of justice become accessible, transparent, and responsive, the digital public square will remain the court of last resort, and the viral video will continue to serve as the most potent, albeit imperfect, instrument for enforcing accountability in a resistant system.
