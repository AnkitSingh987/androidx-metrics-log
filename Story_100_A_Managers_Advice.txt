Title: A Managers Advice

Category: Intermediate

David had been the regional manager of ‘Global Freight Solutions’, a large logistics and delivery company, for over ten years. He was known for his calm personality, his excellent problem-solving skills, and his ability to develop young talent. He believed that a good manager didn't just give orders, but rather guided their employees to reach their full potential. 

Recently, David had promoted a young woman named Elena to the position of team leader. Elena was intelligent, incredibly hardworking, and highly ambitious. When she was a regular employee, she was always the first to arrive and the last to leave, and her work was always flawless. Because of this, David thought she would make an excellent leader for the customer service department. 

However, over the past month, David had noticed some worrying signs. Elena’s team was missing their performance targets, several staff members had complained about low morale, and Elena herself looked completely exhausted. She had dark circles under her eyes, she was drinking far too much coffee, and she seemed to be constantly rushing around the office looking stressed. 

On Wednesday afternoon, David decided it was time to intervene. He walked out of his private office and approached Elena’s desk. She was frantically typing an email while simultaneously holding a phone to her ear, trying to explain a shipping delay to a frustrated client. 

"Elena, when you have a moment, could you please come into my office?" David asked gently, not wanting to startle her.

Elena quickly finished her call and looked up, looking worried. "Of course, David. Give me two minutes to finish this report, and I'll be right there."

Five minutes later, Elena knocked on David’s door and stepped inside, clutching her laptop tightly to her chest. She sat down opposite him, her posture stiff and anxious. 

"Is everything okay, David? Are you unhappy with the quarterly numbers?" Elena asked quickly, her words rushing out. "I know they are slightly lower than last month, but I am personally auditing all the delayed shipments this evening, and I will have a full recovery plan on your desk by tomorrow morning."

David smiled warmly and held up a hand to stop her. "Elena, relax. You are not in trouble. I asked you here because I am worried about you, not the numbers. You look completely burnt out. Tell me honestly, how many hours did you work this week?"

Elena looked down at her lap. "About sixty, maybe sixty-five. But it's necessary! The team is struggling with the new software update, and there are so many mistakes happening. I have to check everything they do to make sure our clients don't suffer."

David nodded slowly, recognizing a classic mistake that many first-time managers made. 

"Elena, you are a brilliant worker. When you were a customer service agent, your strategy for success was to work harder and faster than everyone else," David explained calmly. "But you are a manager now. Your job is no longer to do the work; your job is to make sure the work gets done by your team."

Elena frowned, looking confused. "But if I don't check their work, they make mistakes. Just yesterday, Tom almost sent a large shipment to the wrong address because he didn't double-check the client's file. If I hadn't reviewed his documents, it would have been a disaster."

"And what did you do after you found the mistake?" David asked.

"I fixed the address myself and sent the shipment out, of course," Elena replied proudly, expecting praise. "I saved the company a lot of money and protected our reputation."

David leaned forward, his expression serious but kind. "And what did Tom learn from that experience?"

Elena paused, her mouth slightly open. She thought for a moment before answering softly, "He didn't learn anything. He probably doesn't even know he made a mistake because I fixed it before he saw it."

"Exactly," David said, leaning back in his chair. "Elena, you are micromanaging your team. You are trying to do everyone’s job because you are afraid they won't do it as well as you can. This is causing two major problems. First, you are working yourself into a state of exhaustion. You cannot physically do the work of five people, and if you continue like this, you will get sick."

He paused, letting the words sink in, before continuing. "Second, and more importantly, you are destroying your team’s confidence. If you check every single thing they do, you are sending a clear message: 'I do not trust you.' When employees feel untrusted, they stop trying to improve. They will just wait for you to fix their mistakes. You are not leading them; you are babysitting them."

Elena felt a lump form in her throat. She had been working so hard, sacrificing her evenings and weekends, believing she was being the perfect manager. Hearing that her efforts were actually hurting her team was difficult to accept, but deep down, she knew David was right. 

"So, what should I do?" she asked quietly, her voice trembling slightly. "If I step back and they make a massive mistake, I will be the one responsible."

"Yes, you will be responsible," David agreed. "That is the heavy burden of leadership. You have to allow people to make small mistakes so they can learn. When Tom makes an error, you don't fix it in secret. You call him into your office, you show him the mistake, and you ask him how he plans to fix it and ensure it doesn't happen again. You train him, you coach him, and then you trust him."

David stood up and walked over to the whiteboard on the wall. He wrote three words in large letters: DELEGATE, TRAIN, TRUST. 

"Your goal for the next two weeks is simple," David instructed. "You are not allowed to handle any basic customer service issues or review standard shipping documents. You will delegate those tasks to your team. If they don't know how to do something, you train them. And then, you trust them to execute it."

Elena looked at the board and took a deep breath. It felt terrifying to give up control, but she also felt a sudden sense of relief. She didn't have to carry the entire department on her shoulders anymore.

"It will feel uncomfortable at first," David warned her, walking back to his desk. "There will be growing pains, and there will be mistakes. But if you focus on developing your people instead of doing their work, I promise you, they will eventually surprise you with how capable they are."

"Thank you, David," Elena said sincerely, standing up. "I think I really needed to hear this. I will start delegating this afternoon. In fact, I am going to ask Tom to run the afternoon team meeting today."

David smiled, pleased with her quick understanding. "That is a great first step, Elena. Now, go home at 5:00 PM today. That is an order from your manager. The company will survive without you for one evening."

As Elena left the office, her step felt lighter than it had in weeks. She realized that true leadership wasn't about being the smartest or the hardest working person in the room; it was about empowering the people around you to succeed. She had a lot to learn, but with David's advice, she felt ready to finally become the leader her team needed.
