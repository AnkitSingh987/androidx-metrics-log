Retrofitting: the missing link in India's vehicle scrappage policy

Third, Regional Transport Offices (RTOs) and local authorities must establish clear frameworks for registering retrofitted vehicles, updating ownership documentation, and inspecting retrofit quality, so that the process is both transparent and trustworthy for vehicle owners. If these measures were implemented, retrofitting could emerge as a mainstream, legally sanctioned alternative to scrapping, one that is cheaper for consumers, cleaner for the environment, and considerably more scalable than the current formal scrapping system, which processes only a fraction of India's ELVs each year. The macroeconomic implications extend well beyond individual vehicle owners.

Collectively, retrofitting could save over ₹1.2 lakh crore in combined manufacturing capital and consumer acquisition costs compared with a scenario in which all ELV owners purchase new EVs. It would also redistribute economic value away from a small number of centralised manufacturing hubs toward thousands of local certified mechanics and engineering garages across the country, generating skilled employment in EV kit installation, battery servicing, and vehicle inspection at the grassroots level. On the environmental ledger, retrofitting at scale could prevent over 21 tonnes of industrial carbon dioxide from entering the atmosphere by eliminating the need to manufacture new vehicle bodies, while the reuse of metal frames would keep hazardous waste out of local soil and water systems.

Proven technology Several countries have already demonstrated that regulated conversion of existing vehicles can complement, rather than compete with, new EV manufacturing. In countries such as the U.K., the government permits approved retrofit conversions through recognised engineering standards, while clean-air initiatives have encouraged electric conversion of taxis and heritage vehicles instead of outright replacement. Similarly, in the United States, several states allow certified EV conversions for passenger vehicles, school buses, delivery vans, and municipal fleets under federal and state vehicle safety regulations, creating a growing aftermarket industry.

These international practices reveal that India need not choose between an ageing fleet of polluting vehicles and the considerable expense of replacing it wholesale with new EVs. Retrofitting offers a credible middle path, one that is more affordable for consumers, more sustainable for the environment, and more equitable in its distribution of economic opportunity. What stands between India and this outcome is not a lack of technology or demand, but the absence of a clear legal and regulatory framework to let the industry develop safely and at scale.
