When a part of us dies silently

Language is far more than a collection of words and grammatical rules. It is the living memory of a people, carrying their history, beliefs, customs, songs, stories, and unique understanding of the world.

Every language reflects a distinct way of thinking and interpreting reality. Yet, in our rapidly globalizing world, thousands of these linguistic treasures are disappearing, often silently. The last speakers of many endangered languages are living witnesses to cultures that may vanish forever when they are gone.

Linguists estimate that nearly half of the world's approximately 7,000 languages are endangered. Some are spoken by only a handful of elderly people, while others have fewer than a hundred fluent speakers. Every few weeks, somewhere in the world, a language falls silent forever. Unlike the extinction of an animal species, which often attracts global attention, the death of a language usually goes unnoticed.

Yet its loss is equally irreversible. Languages disappear for many reasons. Economic opportunities often require people to adopt dominant languages such as English, Mandarin, Spanish, Arabic, or Hindi. Parents may encourage their children to speak these languages, believing they offer a better future.

Migration to cities, globalization, intermarriage, and the influence of television, cinema, and the internet further weaken the use of local tongues. In some cases, governments have historically discouraged or even prohibited indigenous languages in schools and public institutions, leaving generations disconnected from their linguistic heritage. The disappearance of a language is not merely the loss of vocabulary.

It is the loss of an entire worldview. Indigenous languages often contain detailed knowledge of medicinal plants, farming techniques, weather patterns, and local ecosystems accumulated over centuries. Many possess words and expressions that have no equivalent in other languages because they describe experiences unique to a particular culture.

Oral traditions, myths, folk songs, and rituals are deeply rooted in language. Once a language dies, much of this cultural wealth becomes inaccessible. The story of the last speakers is often deeply moving. Imagine an elderly woman who is the only person left capable of speaking the language she learned from her parents.

She remembers lullabies, proverbs, and stories that no one else understands. She carries within her a library that has never been written down. When she passes away, an irreplaceable chapter of human civilization disappears with her. Such stories are unfolding across continents  -  from the indigenous communities of the Americas and Australia to remote villages in Asia and Africa.

India, celebrated for its linguistic diversity, also faces this challenge. The country is home to hundreds of languages and dialects, many of which are spoken by small tribal communities. Several are now endangered because younger generations increasingly shift to regional or national languages for education and employment.

The loss of these languages would diminish India's remarkable cultural mosaic. Despite these challenges, there is hope. Modern technology has become a powerful ally in language preservation. Audio and video recordings, digital dictionaries, online archives, and mobile applications allow endangered languages to be documented and taught.

Universities and linguistic organizations work alongside native communities to record grammar, vocabulary, folklore, and oral history. Some communities have established language nests, where children learn directly from elder speakers, helping to revive traditions that seemed destined to disappear. However, preservation cannot rely solely on scholars or governments.
