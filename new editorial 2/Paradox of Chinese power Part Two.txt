By embedding itself as the primary supplier of critical industrial inputs and green technology, Beijing creates structural dependencies across both the Global South and Western economies.

Weaponizing these supply chains, whether through export restrictions on critical minerals or targeted trade sanctions, gives Beijing potent immediate leverage in international disputes. However, this strategy carries severe long-term costs. As Wright points out, flooding global markets with subsidized manufacturing triggers an inevitable political backlash.

Nations across Europe, North America, and Latin America are increasingly erecting tariffs, anti-dumping duties, and supply-chain restrictions, eroding China's diplomatic standing and accelerating economic de-risking. What are the implications for global stability and U.S. foreign policy? Recognizing China's dual nature  -  a state possessing formidable coercive manufacturing power alongside deep domestic financial vulnerability  -  demands a calibrated strategy.

We must avoid both strategic panic and complacency. Beijing is neither a ten-foot-tall economic giant immune to market forces nor a collapsing state on the brink of implosion. As Wright suggests, America's "strategic opening" lies in understanding that Beijing is constrained by its own internal balance-sheet arithmetic.

United States strategy need not rely on blanket containment or alarmism. Instead, Washington should target strategic chokepoints in advanced technology, strengthen regional alliances such as the Quad, of which India is a member, and AUKUS, and offer viable infrastructure and investment alternatives to developing nations. Is China geopolitically strong but economically weak?

The answer is nuanced. China is a p o tent , state-directed industrial superpower built upon a fragile, debt-laden domestic economy. Its real-economy dominance in critical industries ensures that it will remain a formidable rival for decades to come, even as its internal credit model faces severe headwinds.

For policymakers in the USA and India navigating this complex landscape, seeing China clearly means measuring both its undeniable industrial strength and the quiet, persistent decay of its financial foundations. By NARAIN BATRA (The writer is a contributor for The Institute of Arts and Ideas, a philosophical think-tank based in London.)
