Title: India must own its energy security Part One

India must own its energy security

For this, raising oil storage capacity, boosting the energy produced at home, and completing projects on time are vital Indian equities markets have recorded their eighth straight weekly loss. Over the last weekend, President Trump rejected Iran's proposal to end the conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude rose above $100, the Sensex and the Nifty explored near six-month lows, and the rupee declined to below 96 to the dollar.

Peace remains elusive, and financial upheaval is underway. Investors seek safety, and economies like ours are hit first. In March 2026, the West Asia conflict led to the closure of the Hormuz Strait, which affected 14 per cent of the world's oil and gas. Since July, the Houthis have resumed attacks in the Red Sea, controlling Mokha and Perim Island at Bab al-Mandab.

On September 10, drones attacked Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, which bypasses Hormuz. These assaults have cut Qatar's gas exports by 17 per cent for an estimated three to five years. About two-thirds of global energy trade depends on maritime chokepoints. As regional rivalries grow, disruptions are becoming commonplace.

What a long shock does Oil fundamentally influences all prices in India. It affects the rupee, bus fares, fertilizer costs, and vegetables transported by diesel trucks. Since January 1, Brent oil has increased by about 63 per cent. Every ten-dollar increase in oil price results in approximately $18 billion leaving the country.

The damage is evident in the data. Wholesale inflation reached 9.92 per cent in August, with fuel and power costs up 22.93 per cent. Retail inflation stands at 4.82 per cent, and the RBI forecasts it will be 5.9 per cent between October and December. Foreign portfolio investors withdrew ₹2.45 lakh crore from Indian shares by September 18, surpassing the total for all of 2025.

Additionally, the rupee has depreciated by about 6 per cent since January, despite a bumper $135 billion inflow, thanks to the RBI's recent efforts. On the street, restaurants struggled when cooking gas was rationed at the peak of the war, and small factories saw their margins shrink. A prolonged shock is a slow tax on every household.

The Viksit Bharat question To become a developed nation by 2047, India must grow around 8 per cent a year for 20 years. This growth depends on energy, and 88.7 per cent of our crude oil, an all-time high, is imported. Half of our gas arrives by ship. Our target was to cut oil import dependence to 67 per cent, but we are 22 percentage points short.

Each external shock takes away a slice of the growth that Viksit Bharat relies on. India has never lacked plans. The Turkmenistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan-India pipeline agreement of 2010 remains on paper. At home, too, projects slip. The ONGC's deepwater KG-DWN-98/2 project was to produce oil in March 2020.

But it did so only in January 2024. The second phase of expansion of our strategic oil reserves, approved in July 2021, remains incomplete. Power trends mirror this pattern. By August 2026, renewable capacity reached about 296 gigawatts, yet grid growth hasn't kept pace. Over five years, India achieved only about 80 per cent of its transmission targets.

In the first quarter of 2026, 300 gigawatt-hours of clean energy were wasted because of grid limitations. Additionally, transmission lines in Rajasthan and Gujarat are embroiled in legal disputes concerning the habitat of the Great Indian Bustard. Every crisis prompts strategies. When prices ease, they are shelved.
