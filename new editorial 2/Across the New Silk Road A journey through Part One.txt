Title: Across the New Silk Road A journey through Part One

Across the New Silk Road: A journey through Uzbekistan

Uzbekistan itself becomes a metaphor for a certain kind of quiet diplomacy which is pragmatic, understated and conscious of its room for manoeuvre. Flying from Delhi to Tashkent, the geography of the region unfolds with remarkable clarity. The Uzbekistan airlines aircraft passes over Peshawar, Pakistan, before the landscape rises sharply towards the formidable ranges of the Hindu Kush and the Pamirs.

Then, almost abruptly, the mountains give way to an immense expanse of flat land stretching towards Central Asia. The flight ends in a hot, windswept Tashkent. It is striking how close Central Asia is to northern India. Delhi and Tashkent are barely two and a half hours apart by air and this is roughly the same flying time between Delhi and Mumbai.

Yet the mountains that once appeared to divide these regions have never truly kept them apart. For centuries, people, armies, merchants, ideas and religions have crossed these formidable barriers, creating connections between South and Central Asia that remain visible even today. I was travelling through Uzbekistan for ten days, visiting Tashkent, Khiva, Bukhara and Samarkand, entirely by train.

But the journey was also, in a sense, a return to a region I had encountered professionally more than a decade earlier. With a population of nearly 38 million, Uzbekistan's geography gives it particular importance. It borders every other Central Asian state as well as Afghanistan, making it a natural crossroads within the region.

In 2015, as project manager of a United Nations project focused on Central Asia, I worked closely with diplomats and officials from countries including the United States, Russia and China. Part of my responsibility involved liaising with diplomatic missions in New York and Central Asian governments as we organised a regional conference on border management. One such conference in Ashgabat, Turkmenistan, came at a time of heightened international concern over foreign fighters from Central Asia travelling to the Middle East to join extremist groups like ISIS.

That experience had introduced me to Central Asia through diplomatic meetings, policy discussions and conference rooms. The trip also offered an early lesson in travelling through Central Asia. My baggage arrived late, and the airline staff were taciturn and hardly apologetic. One of my colleagues had warned me beforehand that communication could be difficult: Russian was the principal language of communication, and responses in English could be limited.

With no formal clothes for the conference, I had little choice but to borrow my colleague's oversized shirt. It was an amusingly inauspicious beginning to my engagement with the region. Travelling through Uzbekistan years later offered an entirely different perspective. The borders, cities and infrastructure that had once been subjects of professional discussions were now visible from the ground.

Communication could still be challenging, but it was far easier than on my earlier visit to Central Asia. Google Translate provided a useful workaround, while a growing number of young English speakers, particularly in the cities and tourist areas, made navigating the country considerably easier. Long forgotten and often viewed in the 19th and 20th century primarily through the prism of the Great Game between the British Empire and Russia, Central Asia was far more than a peripheral theatre of imperial rivalry.
