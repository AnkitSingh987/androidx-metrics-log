Title: When the screen becomes a mirror Part Two

When the screen becomes a mirror

Now look at India's Time Use Survey 2024. Indian women spend between 289 and 350 minutes a day on unpaid domestic work and caregiving, about five hours. Indian men spend roughly 88 minutes. The more revealing figure is the change over time: between 2019 and 2024, women's unpaid domestic load fell from 315 minutes to 305.

Ten minutes, in five years, during which their participation in paid work rose steeply. The same survey shows Indian men putting in about eight hours a day of paid work and women about 5.7. It is tempting to read that gap as women choosing to work less. It cannot be read at all without the five hours of unpaid work sitting underneath it.

This is what the sociologist Arlie Hochschild called the second shift: the day's real work beginning when the office day ends. It is also why the care economy, meaning the cooking, cleaning, raising of children and looking after the old, remains invisible in GDP despite holding up everything that is counted. The Economic Survey has itself acknowledged that this burden falls disproportionately on women and constrains their entry into paid work.

Article 39 of the Constitution directs the State towards equal means of livelihood and equal pay for equal work, but the largest body of work done in this country happens outside the employment framework altogether. The consequences are no longer abstract. School enrolment in 2025 fell to its lowest level in seven years, about 25 crore students, with roughly 11 lakh fewer children in a single year, a decline that officials themselves attribute in part to falling births.

A smaller cohort of children becomes a smaller workforce, a narrower tax base and a heavier pension load, arriving at the same moment as a larger elderly population. None of this is an argument against anyone's life. Fewer children can mean more invested in each one, and the freedom that produced this shift is worth defending.

But if half the population is expected to hold a job while still carrying the household alone, the decision to marry later and have fewer children stops looking like liberation and starts looking like a rational response to an institutional failure. The remedies are unglamorous and well known: paternity leave normalised to the point where men actually take it, affordable creches and childcare at scale, working hours that bend for care, and statistical systems that count unpaid work instead of assuming it. India is not Korea.

Our 1.9 is a long way from their 0.8, and that distance is time we still have. But the Korean pattern, in which education rose, employment rose, and the domestic burden did not move, is visible in our own government's data. The serials we watch are a mirror held up at an angle. Behind the modern lives on screen is a society discovering what happens when one half of an arrangement changes and the other half does not.

The question worth asking is not why Indian women are postponing marriage and children. It is whether our homes and our workplaces changed when their lives did. By PRAVIN KAUSHAL (The writer is an entrepreneur and columnist. He is a director at Verda.ai and MriKal.)
