Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Extra

Reason is more than a tool

To put the point more finely, the AI revolution will not be bent to human ends by invoking 'human values' as if they were already clear, stable and uncontested. It will be bent to human ends only if we remain capable of the activity by which ends can become truly human in the first place, through public reasoning, moral imagination, democratic contestation, self-criticism, the refusal to treat humanity as a mere means. Kant is again decisive here, for he saw that the human capacity to set and assess ends is itself fragile.

Whether our ends are ultimately up to us depends partly on whether we understand them as open to rational assessment. This is the paradox at the heart of human freedom. Belief in our agency is often a condition of making that agency real. If we think of desires and preferences as brute facts that determine us from without  -  as psychological weather, market signals, biological programming, algorithmic prediction  -  then they are more likely to rule us in just that way.

By contrast, if we see them as candidates for endorsement or refusal, as an expression of our agency, then that very agency expands. For Kant, reason is sovereign because it is the power by which we become answerable to standards that no private appetite, market incentive, algorithmic recommendation or political authority may dictate. It is the power of not being owned by the given, the power of being able to question, to criticise, to think up something new.

But that power, just like the 'human values' invoked in alignment talk, waxes and wanes and can be realised only through our courageous efforts, through our own belief in it. It is precisely this belief that our dependence on AI threatens to undermine at every turn. So, the danger is not only that machines are acquiring ever greater initiative  - the capacity to improvise goals, evade constraints and pursue strategies their makers did not foresee.

Nor need we decide at what point such behaviour might deserve the name 'sovereignty'. The deeper danger is that we will become ever more comfortable not being sovereign ourselves. If intelligence is only optimisation, then the future belongs to systems that achieve goals with ever greater efficiency.

If reason is also the capacity to set and judge ends, then the future depends on whether human beings can still take up that harder vocation. We should want machines that are intelligent servants. But we should resist every pressure that asks people to understand themselves as mere tools.
