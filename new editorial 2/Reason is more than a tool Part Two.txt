Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Two

Reason is more than a tool

His point is that reason's practical use is limited to exposing false beliefs and imprudent means. When it comes to action: reason is servant, not master. This view is often treated as too dark and cynical. That is unfair to Hume. For him, passions were not merely appetites or primitive urges. They included sympathy, benevolence, moral feeling, concern for others, love of tranquility, even a kind of longing for freedom from our more violent desires.

To Hume's further credit, his view has strong intuitive appeal. He was right that reason often serves passion, and right to give feeling, sociability and temperament their due. His mistake was to conclude that reason can do nothing more. By making passion so rich and reason so poor, Hume left us with a lopsided picture of human nature: ennobling about what the passions can be and accomplish, but implausibly restrictive about reason.

Without reason's power to project, assess and revise ends, we may know how to reach a destination, but not whether it is one worth choosing. Immanuel Kant, the Königsberg revolutionary who did more than anyone else to set the terms of modern ethics, understood this very well. Writing a few decades after Hume's Treatise, Kant forcefully rejected the servant model of reason.

He accepted Hume's warning that reason cannot simply read values off the furniture of the world. But he denied that our ends must therefore simply be determined by desire, temperament or social convention. For Kant, reason is not only a faculty for choosing efficient means to independently given ends.

It is also the power to ask whether an end is worthy of us in the first place, whether it can be justified, whether it can be willed without contradiction, whether pursuing it treats others as persons or as mere tools. This is where Kant's richer notion of reason as freedom enters. A merely mechanical being is pushed forward and determined by prior states, be they desires, biological impulses, evolutionary drives or something similar.

A rational being, by contrast, can act for the sake of an imagined but unrealised future it itself originates and proposes. I have the power to represent a possible world  -  a friendship repaired, an injustice remedied, a child educated, a law reformed  -  and act to bring it about, even when there is no empirical evidence that such a world has existed before. I have the power to invent something new.

Practical reason, Kant writes, does not deal with objects in order to know them, but 'in order to make them actual'. That is a stunning claim. To act freely is not merely to respond to the given  -  to the world as it is, to my desires as I happen to find them. It is to let a possible future, grasped under an idea of what ought to be, transform the present.

This is the heart of Kant's idea of autonomy: choosing ends in accord with a law one can rationally acknowledge as one's own. For Kant, freedom does not consist in doing whatever we happen to want, since our wants may themselves be shaped by impulse, appetite or circumstance. Autonomy is self-rule, but not the rule of an isolated ego.
