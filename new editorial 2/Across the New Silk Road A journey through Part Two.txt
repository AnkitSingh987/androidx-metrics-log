Title: Across the New Silk Road A journey through Part Two

Across the New Silk Road: A journey through Uzbekistan

Geographically, it stood at the crossroads of South Asia, Europe, Xinjiang, Iran and the wider Eurasian world, making it a vital corridor for the movement of people, ideas, cultures and commerce. In Uzbekistan, one frequently encounters maps tracing the various arteries of the Silk Road. One such map shows a route linking South Asia with Central Asia through Ladakh and Xinjiang in present-day China, offering a reminder of how closely these regions were connected long before the emergence of modern borders.

Tashkent, the port of entry, is a city of contrasts. Soviet-era architecture and monumental avenues exist alongside modern hotels, restaurants, shopping areas and new construction. Wide roads and spacious public areas reflect the planning traditions of the Soviet period. At the Uzbekistan Hotel, one gets a glimpse of the importance Tashkent once enjoyed as a diplomatic venue during the Soviet era.

There are longstanding stories that foreign guests were monitored or that parts of the hotel were bugged during the Soviet period. Whether every such story is true is difficult to establish, but they capture something of the atmosphere associated with that era. The city hosted the famous Tashkent Conference in January 1966, where Indian Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri and Pakistani President Ayub Khan met under Soviet mediation following the 1965 India-Pakistan war.

The talks resulted in the Tashkent Declaration, signed on January 10, 1966, with Soviet Premier Alexei Kosygin playing a central mediating role. From Tashkent, I travelled by train. The newer Uzbek trains were remarkably comfortable, modern and clean. In my experience, their cleanliness compared favourably with some of the trains I have travelled on in Europe.

The Silk Road has not disappeared. Its infrastructure has changed. Travelling from Tashkent to Khiva, one can see the landscape gradually changing as the desert becomes more prominent. A distance of above 700 km from Tashkent to Khiva is completed in seven and a half hours in a comfortable train journey.

Situated on the western side, Khiva had a distinct character of its own, shaped by its place in the historic region of Khorezm, one of the ancient centres of civilisation in Central Asia. Inside the walls of Itchan Kala, the old inner city, mud-brick buildings, turquoise domes, minarets, mosques and madrasas create an extraordinary sense of continuity. Walking through its narrow streets, it is possible to imagine the city as a major stop on the old routes linking Central Asia with Persia, the West Asia and South Asia.

But the Silk Road was never simply the romantic highway of popular imagination. Commerce existed alongside conquest, political rivalry and slavery. Cities prospered and declined as rulers and empires changed. Khiva's history was not only about trade. It was shaped by the power struggles of Khorezm, by conquest and rivalry, and by the harsh geography that made control of an oasis city strategically important.

The borders visible on today's map can therefore be misleading when looking at Central Asian history. Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan are modern states. For much of history, however, people, armies, languages and merchants moved across spaces that now belong to different countries.

On the journey back towards Tashkent, Bukhara felt more intimate. I had known the name long before visiting it. In Punjabi, there is an old saying: "Jehri mauj Chajju de chubare, na oh Balkh na Bukhare. (There is no pleasure like that of Chajju's rooftop, not even in Balkh or Bukhara.) The proverb is revealing.
