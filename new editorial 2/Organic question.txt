Title: Organic question

Organic question

It suggests health, safety and responsible farming, and consumers in India's cities are increasingly prepared to pay more for products carrying that promise. Yet the very success of the label raises an uncomfortable question: does the buyer actually know what is being bought? This is not a trivial matter of consumer awareness.

It goes to the credibility of an entire market. Organic agriculture is not simply a synonym for "good food". Certification is intended to establish compliance with specified production practices. That is important because a market cannot function on claims that cannot be independently verified. The certification mark is supposed to reduce the information gap between producer and consumer.

But certification can solve only part of the problem. The consumer must also understand what the certification signifies. A shopper may reasonably associate organic food with fewer synthetic inputs, greater safety, better nutrition or environmentally responsible farming. Some of those associations may be justified in particular circumstances; others may go well beyond what certification establishes.

The result is a peculiar form of information asymmetry: the producer and certifying authority may know precisely what a label guarantees, while the buyer may attach a much broader meaning to it. That creates risks on both sides. For the consumer, the danger is paying a substantial premium on the basis of assumptions that may not be warranted.

For the farmer, the danger is equally serious. Certification involves costs and compliance requirements. If consumers cannot recognise the specific value that certified production adds, there is little reason for them to pay more. The price premium that is supposed to reward the farmer for meeting higher standards then becomes difficult to sustain.

India should therefore think of organic certification not merely as a regulatory exercise but as an information system. Its effectiveness ultimately depends on whether information travels accurately from the farm, through the certification process, to the supermarket shelf and finally to the household. This does not mean abandoning the word "organic".

Nor does it justify treating certification as meaningless. It means recognising that a technical standard and a consumer proposition are not necessarily the same thing. Food labels are, after all, promises. Their value depends on precision. If consumers need to know whether a product was produced without particular synthetic inputs, that information should be communicated plainly rather than hidden behind a single reassuring word.

If organic standards encompass much more, those distinctions should be made equally clear. The larger lesson extends beyond agriculture. In any market built on certification  - from food and forestry to ethical sourcing and environmental claims  -  trust depends not merely on having standards, but on making their meaning intelligible.

A label that consumers misunderstand may still sell products. But a certification system earns lasting credibility only when buyers know what they are paying for.
