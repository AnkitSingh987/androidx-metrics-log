Title: Skills gap mismanaged Part One

Skills gap mismanaged

Corporate training, industry-academia tie-ups vital Every technological shift revives a familiar rhetoric: India has a skill gap. Today, as artificial intelligence, cloud computing, data engineering, and cybersecurity reshape the economy, this concern has returned to the centre of policy debate. Industry leaders argue that graduates are not job-ready; governments respond with skilling missions; universities promise reform.

Yet missing from much of this discussion is an uncomfortable truth: skill gaps are not new - and India once addressed them far more deliberately than it does today. In the 1990s and early 2000s, India's IT industry expanded rapidly despite an obvious shortage of "ready-made" talent. Companies such as TCS, Infosys, and Wipro recruited graduates from diverse academic backgrounds through aptitude-based screening, not because they were fully skilled, but because they demonstrated learning potential.

This was followed by structured training programs lasting three to six months, closely aligned with business needs. Industry estimates suggest that large IT firms trained tens of thousands of fresh graduates annually, creating a scalable and resilient talent pipeline. While comprehensive longitudinal data is limited, anecdotal evidence indicates that many such internally trained recruits built long careers within their organizations and progressed through the corporate hierarchy, underscoring the long-term returns of early-career skilling investments.

Private institutions like NIIT and Aptech complemented this effort, forming an ecosystem in which industry demand and workforce supply were actively bridged. This was not merely an employment strategy - it was national capability building. Two decades later, the picture looks very different. Despite louder complaints about talent shortages, most large organizations show limited appetite for trainee hiring or long-term skilling investments.

Instead, expectations of "day-one productivity" dominate hiring practices. This shift has happened at a time when India is undertaking its most ambitious education reform in decades. NEP push The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 explicitly encourages industry-institute partnerships, flexible curricula, credit-based internships, practitioner-led courses, and applied learning.

Across the country, educational institutions are responding. Universities are revising syllabi, setting up innovation cells, inviting industry experts as adjunct faculty, and actively seeking collaboration. Yet the response from industry has been far more muted. While policy intent is clear and institutional readiness is growing, industry enthusiasm to meaningfully engage remains uneven.

Many partnerships remain transactional - limited to guest lectures or short internships - rather than deep, co-designed programs that shape employability outcomes. This asymmetry is now emerging as a structural bottleneck in the skilling ecosystem. To be fair, the context has changed. Complex scenario First, the nature of work is more complex.

Earlier IT services involved standardized tasks that could be trained at scale. Today's roles  -  AI engineering, cloud security, advanced analytics - require deeper specialization. According to NASSCOM, some emerging digital roles can take 12 - 24 months to reach full productivity. This increases corporate risk in training fresh graduates.

However, complexity should drive training innovation, not disengagement. Modular learning, apprenticeships, and stackable credentials  -  co-designed with institutions under NEP frameworks  -  can reduce risk while preserving entry-level access. Second, attrition concerns loom large. With IT-sector attrition often ranging between 15 - 20%, firms worry that training investments may benefit competitors.

Yet evidence from corporate HR studies consistently shows that early-career hires with structured growth pathways demonstrate higher long-term retention than lateral recruits. Policy can support shared-cost models that lower individual firm risk while sustaining the talent pipeline. Third, higher education must shoulder responsibility.
