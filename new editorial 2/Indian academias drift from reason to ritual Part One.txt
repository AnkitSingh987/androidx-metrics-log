Title: Indian academias drift from reason to ritual Part One

Indian academia's drift from reason to ritual

Academic institutions must remain spaces for scepticism, dissent and fearless inquiry At their core, educational institutions are intended to be 'temples of empiricism'. Their entire legitimacy rests on a single, unforgiving premise: that truth is discovered through observation, experimentation, and peer-reviewed verification, not through revelation, tradition, or charismatic authority. The scientific method thrives on questioning, falsifiability, and rigorous doubt - virtues that stand in direct opposition to belief, spiritualism, and uncritical reverence.

This is not a mere aesthetic choice or a harmless nod to cultural heritage; it is a profound philosophical contradiction. This raises a disquieting question: when an academic institute prioritises spiritual performance and political deference over intellectual rigour, does it erode the very foundation of its own mission? Reason versus reverence Knowledge advances not by revering the past, but by challenging it.

Newton's famous "shoulders of giants" line was half-jest - not a call to worship, but to build critically on past work. History shows this: Galileo, Darwin, and Einstein each overturned once-sacred truths. This relentless, iconoclastic spirit is the engine of human knowledge. When a university or research body such as the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) introduces feudalistic, socially hierarchical etiquette  -  requiring students to bow before dignitaries and formal functions such as convocations suffused with religious incantations  -  it transmits a deeply corrosive message.

Consider the 2026 convocation ceremony, in August, at IIT Delhi, where Prime Minister Narendra Modi was the chief guest. Reports surrounding the ceremony highlighted two practices that drew criticism: students were reportedly instructed on how far to bow while receiving medals from the Prime Minister, and were reportedly asked to stand during Vedic chants. In a secular, democratic republic, these are ideological statements with far-reaching consequences.

By doing so, the institution subtly endorses the idea that the state and its functionaries operate under a supernatural mandate, a concept antithetical to the rational, evidence-based framework of a modern republic. The bowing protocol resurrects a pre-modern, courtly hierarchy that has no place in an institution ostensibly committed to meritocracy and intellectual emancipation. It infantilises the graduating students, reducing them from autonomous, critical thinkers to supplicants seeking the favour of a political figure.

Yet, it is precisely here that the ceremony reveals its deepest irony: the Prime Minister  -  a self-avowed votary of his religious practices  -  used his address to urge students to question conventional wisdom and challenge accepted traditions. The dissonance between the ritual performance and the rhetorical exhortation could hardly be more pronounced. This performative contradiction reveals the ceremony for what it is: a carefully orchestrated spectacle of power, where the substance of the message is sacrificed for the optics of reverence.

The spirit of the Constitution requires us to value democratic equality over feudal submission. Implications for institutional integrity By reintroducing ritualised submission into academic life, we are resurrecting an intellectual straitjacket. When a student is compelled to bow before a political figure on a dais, the message is that authority is to be obeyed, not interrogated.

When a scientific convocation opens with a religious prayer, it is implied that the supernatural has a seat at the table of empirical inquiry. Defenders will call these practices mere "cultural flourishes"  -  innocent tributes to India's ancient past, meant to ground students and teach respect. But that argument falters the moment we examine it.
