Title: Indian academias drift from reason to ritual Part Two

Indian academia's drift from reason to ritual

The old guru-shishya paradigm, for all its reverence, demanded unquestioning obedience  -  the student absorbing the master's word without debate. Modern pedagogy has moved on. It is built on Socratic dialectic  -  on the clash of ideas; on rigorous, respectful dissent. The performative traditionalism often serves a political function.

By wrapping themselves in the cloak of ancient culture, institutions deflect scrutiny and discourage dissent. Critics can be branded as "westernised", "anti-national" or a recently added epithet, "Dimagi Naxal (intellectual extremist") for objecting to what is, in essence, a breach of academic ethics. This rhetorical strategy is intellectually dishonest; it conflates so-called cultural pride with uncritical obedience, and tradition with truth.

The result is a climate of fear  -  where academics and students self-censor, wary of being branded traitors for defending institutional integrity. Most Indian researchers today are too afraid to question government overreach, including their academies, for fear of retaliation. The "Free to Think 2026" report published by Scholars at Risk (SAR)  -  an international network that monitors education communities worldwide  - presents a comprehensive assessment of the challenges confronting India's academic landscape.

Based on data from July 1, 2025, to June 30, 2026, the report assesses academic freedom in India as "severely restricted." The report says that India's Academic Freedom Index score fell from 0.38 in 2022 to 0.14 in 2025, leaving it in the "severely restricted" category. This is attributed to deterioration to systematic government interference in university governance, suppression of dissent among faculty and students, and a pervasive climate of fear and self-censorship across Indian campuses. The report says that the central government, led by the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party, has expanded its authority over higher education through the University Grants Commission (UGC).

Measures include relaxing academic qualification criteria for Vice-Chancellors and accelerating the casualisation of the academic workforce by removing the 10% cap on non-permanent or contract-based faculty appointments. These policy shifts eliminated the previous limit on contractual faculty positions under the 2018 regulations. The report highlights that government interference in university operations  -  such as exerting influence over administration, curricula, and management  -  undermines institutional autonomy and standards of teaching; it also cites police violence against student protests as instances of direct repression of critical voices on campuses and outside.

A call for vigilance and the Tagorean ideal The presence of spiritual rituals and hierarchical protocols in research institutions is no trivial matter. It is a canary in the coal mine of academic freedom. If a scientist can be compelled to bow to a politician, or to sit through religious chants before a lecture, we must ask: what other compromises await?

Will research agendas be steered by political piety rather than public need? Will funding be allocated based on ideological alignment rather than scientific merit, thereby rewarding sycophants? If we are to preserve the integrity of scientific inquiry, we must insist that our universities remain sanctuaries of scepticism, not stages for spiritual theatre or political sycophancy.

The future of knowledge, and indeed the future of a free and democratic society, depends on it. If we truly wish to honour India's legacy of cultural renaissance and critical thought, we need only turn to Rabindranath Tagore. His poem "Where the Mind is Without Fear"  -  written under British rule  -  is not a relic of nostalgic pride, but an invocation for a future world: one where people think with courage, seek truth fearlessly, and live without narrow walls of division.
