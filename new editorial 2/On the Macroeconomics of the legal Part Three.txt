Title: On the Macroeconomics of the legal Part Three

On the Macroeconomics of the legal redefinition of labour processes

India's share of global exports of apparel, leather, textiles and footwear rose from 0.9 per cent in 2002 to a peak of 4.5 per cent in 2013 before dropping since then, while Vietnam's and Bangladesh's shares increased to above 5 per cent over the decade. It, therefore, shows that wage suppression does not help if labour productivity does not rise. The Supreme Court's approach causes the ratio of real wages to labour productivity to improve over time, as it reduces the incentive for employers to invest in training, task-specific knowledge and organisational improvements.

In effect, there is probably going to be a reallocation of income that fails to boost external demand while at the same time decreasing domestic demand; this is taking place at a time when trade protectionism in the Global North is shrinking the scope for labour arbitrage. In an economy of India that is based on macroeconomic demand, this legal action will have the effect of causing stagnation and will thus exacerbate the existing difficulties from the point of view of the political economy. By Trishna Sarkar & C Saratchand Sarkar is Assistant Professor in the Department of Economics, Dr Bhim Rao Ambedkar College, University of Delh; Saratchand is Professor in the Dept of Economics, Satyawati College, University of Delhi
