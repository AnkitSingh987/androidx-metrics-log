Title: Across the New Silk Road A journey through Part Three

Across the New Silk Road: A journey through Uzbekistan

Bukhara and Balkh (now in present-day Afghanistan) were not merely distant names in the Punjabi imagination. They were part of a wider geographical and cultural world that had long been connected with northern India through trade, scholarship, religion and political movements. Bukhara is not simply an archaeological site or a collection of restored monuments.

Its mosques, madrasas, bazaars and courtyards remain embedded in everyday life. Central Asia was also an important centre in the history of Islam. Islam began taking root early in the region in the seventh and eighth centuries and became deeply established over subsequent centuries. Yet older cultural layers survived.

Iranian traditions and influences associated with the pre-Islamic Zoroastrian world can still be seen in aspects of art, archaeology and cultural memory. The result is a civilisation built in layers rather than replacing one era completely with another. Then came Samarkand. If Tashkent represents modern Uzbekistan and Khiva and Bukhara preserve different layers of their mediaeval past, Samarkand represents the grandeur of the empires that once ruled this part of Central Asia.

The Registan is the most spectacular expression of that legacy. The city's historic monuments have undergone extensive restoration, giving them an extraordinary visual splendour. Timur dominates much of Samarkand's historical imagination. Outside Central Asia, history books and popular culture have often portrayed Amir Timur primarily through his conquests and the violence associated with them.

In Uzbekistan, the Timurid period is also remembered for the political power, architecture, scholarship and cultural achievements that developed under Timur and his successors. I visited the Amir Timur mausoleum and several monuments associated with the Timurids. Timur has been elevated as an important national figure in independent Uzbekistan and has become part of the country's post-Soviet effort to reclaim and reinterpret its pre-Soviet history.

The political geography of Timur's world was also very different from today's. Timur emerged from the Barlas, a Turco-Mongol group rooted in Central Asia. The empires of his time did not conform to today's national boundaries. Their territories extended across areas that are now part of several different countries, including parts of Persia, Afghanistan and South Asia.

Listening to oral histories, one finds it difficult to place ruling dynasties neatly into fixed categories, because the history of Central Asia was shaped by a complex exchange of languages, traditions and cultural influences. Timur and other ruling groups of Mongol origin, for instance, became Turkic-speaking over time while also absorbing a strong Persian cultural and administrative imprint. The result was not a simple transition from one cultural identity to another, but a layered process in which Mongol, Turkic and Persian traditions interacted and were continually reshaped.

Babur illustrates this fluidity particularly well. A great-great-grandson of Timur, he was born in the Fergana Valley, moved through Kabul and eventually established the Mughal dynasty in India. The Mughals actually did retain Turkic, specifically Chagatai Turkic, for a time, but they gradually shifted toward Persian as their principal court and administrative language in India.

There is an inscription at a palace built during Timur's reign that captures the monumental ambition of his rule: "If you doubt our power, look at our buildings." Food provides another connection between the past and present. Plov, rice cooked with meat, carrots and spices, is almost a national institution. And then there are the melons.
