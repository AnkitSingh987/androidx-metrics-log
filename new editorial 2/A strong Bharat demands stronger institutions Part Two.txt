Title: A strong Bharat demands stronger institutions Part Two

A strong Bharat demands stronger institutions

No discussion on institutional reform can ignore agriculture. Nearly half of India's population continues to depend directly or indirectly on agriculture, yet millions of farmers remain economically vulnerable. Fragmented landholdings, climate uncertainty, rising input costs, inadequate irrigation, price volatility, post-harvest losses, and limited value addition continue to constrain farm incomes.

The objective of agricultural policy should move beyond ensuring production to ensuring prosperity. Farmers require modern technology, market access, storage facilities, affordable credit, insurance, research support, and integrated supply chains that allow them to participate meaningfully in India's economic growth. The rise of social media has fundamentally transformed the relationship between governments and citizens.

Public opinion now forms and spreads within hours, turning local grievances into national or even global debates. The Arab Spring demonstrated the disruptive power of digital platforms, while countries such as Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Pakistan have witnessed major political upheavals influenced by rapid information flows and mass mobilization. Several European democracies have also experienced swift political shifts shaped by online discourse.

In this digital age, governments must embrace greater transparency, responsiveness, and accountability, as citizens increasingly expect not just policy announcements but tangible, measurable, and timely governance outcomes. India's young population further amplifies the urgency of reform. Aspirations have risen dramatically.

Young Indians seek quality education, meaningful employment, efficient governance, and equal opportunity. When expectations repeatedly encounter institutional inefficiency, frustration naturally increases. Governments must recognize that economic growth alone cannot sustain public confidence unless institutions function fairly, efficiently, and transparently.

Mahatma Gandhi reminded us that "The true measure of any society can be found in how it treats its most vulnerable members." Institutional reform is therefore not an administrative exercise; it is a moral obligation. Artificial intelligence, digital governance, blockchain, predictive analytics, and data-driven administration present unprecedented opportunities to modernize governance. Governments should deploy these technologies to improve judicial efficiency, detect corruption, streamline public services, strengthen healthcare delivery, enhance agricultural productivity, and improve policy implementation.

Technology should become an instrument of accountability rather than merely a symbol of modernization. The choice before India is clear. Political debates will continue, elections will come and go, and governments will change. But unless India undertakes bold institutional reforms in policing, the judiciary, healthcare, education, agriculture, municipal governance, and public administration, public dissatisfaction will continue to grow irrespective of which party occupies office.

Strong institutions, not strong personalities, ultimately determine the success of a democracy. India needs reforms more than swanky airports, ports, railway stations, road network, administrative buildings. Time, however, is running out fast for governments and citizens alike, for political parties and voters, for the ruling establishment and the opposition.

Across India, public patience is wearing thin, and the electorate is increasingly willing to embrace credible alternatives when they emerge. Recent electoral outcomes-from Tamil Nadu, where established political equations have evolved over time, to the Bankipur Assembly constituency in Bihar, where a new political force upset a long-standing BJP stronghold-demonstrate that no party can take public support for granted. In a vibrant democracy, performance, governance, and institutional credibility-not historical legacy or political dominance will ultimately determine who earns the people's trust.

Reform, therefore, is no longer a matter of choice; it is an imperative for India's democratic and developmental future. By Prashant Tewari The writer is a public policy expert and columnist; Views presented are personal.
