Price Signals

Its surprising longevity says something about the limits of financial markets and the enduring relevance of the real economy.

Exchange rates are often treated as objective reflections of national economic strength. In reality, they are shaped by a complex interplay of interest rates, capital flows, investor sentiment, geopolitical tensions and central-bank intervention. These forces can keep currencies away from levels that reflect what people can actually buy in their own countries for years, sometimes decades.

When that disconnect becomes persistent, it is more than a statistical curiosity. It becomes an economic distortion. Currencies that remain significantly undervalued make exports artificially competitive and encourage trade surpluses. Overvalued currencies, meanwhile, erode industrial competitiveness and widen trade deficits.

The consequences extend beyond commerce. They influence investment decisions, manufacturing employment, inflation, debt burdens and even political stability. Many of the trade disputes that have defined relations among the United States, China, Japan and Europe over the past two decades have ultimately been arguments about currencies, whether explicit or implied.

The danger is that financial markets increasingly reward short-term capital movements while paying insufficient attention to underlying purchasing power. Reserve-currency status, expectations of monetary policy and speculative flows can dominate exchangerate movements long after they cease to reflect economic fundamentals. The result is a world in which prices and everyday economic realities drift further apart.

History suggests such imbalances rarely endure indefinitely. If exchange rates fail to adjust, economies are forced to do the job through inflation, slower growth, wage pressures or painful corrections in trade and investment. The adjustment eventually arrives; only its form remains uncertain. This debate also carries relevance for India, even though the country's unique consumer market prevents one of the world's best-known purchasing power benchmarks from using the same product sold elsewhere.

Policymakers nonetheless rely extensively on purchasing power-parity measures to compare India's economy with those of other nations, a reminder that market exchange rates alone seldom capture what money is truly worth within national borders. No single indicator can fully explain the world's monetary system. Consumer habits differ, labour costs vary and governments frequently influence markets in ways that no simple comparison can capture.

Yet simple benchmarks sometimes expose truths that elaborate models obscure. When a standard product consistently costs far less or far more across countries than exchange rates would suggest, it raises uncomfortable questions about whether currencies are serving economies or merely reflecting the moods of financial markets. The world economy has become more interconnected than ever, but its monetary architecture remains riddled with persistent distortions.

Behind the daily fluctuations of exchange rates lies a simpler question that policymakers should never lose sight of: what does money actually buy? Until financial markets answer that question more faithfully, debates over currency misalignment will remain as consequential as ever.
