Title: The Broken Bicycle
Category: Beginner

That afternoon, David and his father drove to the other side of town. They parked their car in front of a small, neat house. A man was waiting for them in the driveway. Next to the man was the dark red mountain bike from the internet picture. 

David jumped out of the car. He walked quickly towards the bicycle. Up close, it looked much older than it did on the computer screen. A thick layer of grey dust covered the red paint. The black rubber tires were completely flat and squeezed tightly against the ground. The metal chain hung loosely, covered in thick, black grease and dirt. The seat was torn in one corner. 

"It has been in my shed for a long time," the man explained apologetically. "My son went to university five years ago, and nobody has used it since. It definitely needs some love and attention."
David did not say anything. He walked slowly around the bicycle. He touched the handlebars. They felt sticky. He tried to squeeze the brakes, but the metal wires were very stiff and hard to pull. It was a very broken bicycle. 

"What do you think, David?" his father asked gently. "It is a lot of work. We can say no and look for a different one if you want."
David looked at the flat tires and the dirty chain. He felt a moment of doubt. Maybe it was too much work for him. He had never fixed a bicycle before. What if he could not do it? 
But then he looked at the strong metal frame. Beneath the dust, the red paint was a beautiful, deep color. It looked like a fast, sturdy machine that just needed some help. 

"No," David decided, his voice firm. "I want it. I can fix it."
He reached into his pocket and pulled out the thirty dollars. He handed the money to the man. 
"Thank you, young man," the seller smiled. "I hope you enjoy fixing it up. It was a great bicycle when it was new."

David and his father lifted the heavy bicycle and carefully put it into the trunk of their car. They had to leave the trunk slightly open because the bicycle was so big. They tied it securely with a rope and drove slowly back home. 

When they arrived at their house, they carried the bicycle into the garage. The garage was cool and smelled of motor oil and cut grass. His father turned on the bright overhead lights. Under the harsh lights, the bicycle looked even worse. There were deep scratches on the metal pedals, and the spokes of the wheels were covered in brown rust. 

David grabbed an old rag and started to wipe the thick dust off the top tube of the frame. The deep red paint shone brightly underneath. 
"See?" David said, pointing to the clean spot. "It is going to look great."
"I agree," his father said, rolling up his sleeves. "But we cannot just wash it. We have to repair the moving parts first. A clean bicycle is useless if the wheels do not turn."

David knelt on the concrete floor. He looked closely at the loose, dirty chain. 
"Where do we start?" he asked. He suddenly felt a little overwhelmed. There were so many broken parts. The tires, the brakes, the chain, the gears. 
"We start with the most important thing," his father said, walking over to his large red toolbox. "We start with a plan. We make a list of everything that is broken, and we fix them one by one."

David nodded. A plan sounded manageable. 
"First, we need new inner tubes for the tires, because these ones are completely ruined," his father said, writing on a piece of paper. "Second, we need a special brush and strong cleaner for this terrible chain. Third, we need new brake cables. And fourth, we need a lot of patience."

David smiled. The project had officially begun. The broken bicycle was currently useless, but in David's mind, it was already a perfect, fast racing machine. He was ready to learn how to make it work again.
