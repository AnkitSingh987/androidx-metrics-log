Title: Drill deeper now

Category: Editorial

The arithmetic of India’s energy vulnerability is stark and unforgiving. The nation currently imports approximately 85% of its crude oil requirements and nearly 50% of its natural gas consumption. This massive import dependence acts as a colossal structural vulnerability, rendering the Indian economy highly susceptible to exogenous geopolitical shocks. Any disruption in global supply—whether precipitated by the protracted conflict in Eastern Europe, coordinated production cuts by the OPEC+ cartel, or escalating tensions in the critical chokepoints of the Middle East—immediately translates into a massive import bill, precipitating a severe current account deficit and exerting intense downward pressure on the Rupee. Furthermore, the imported inflation associated with exorbitant crude prices rapidly permeates the domestic economy, elevating logistics costs, compressing corporate margins, and triggering a broader inflationary spiral that forces the central bank into aggressive monetary tightening, thereby throttling economic growth.

The argument that accelerating domestic hydrocarbon exploration contradicts India's climate commitments is a manifestation of profound cognitive dissonance regarding the mechanics of the energy transition. The transition to a green economy is not a binary switch, but a protracted, multi-decadal process. While massive investments in solar, wind, and green hydrogen are critical, fossil fuels will remain the indispensable bedrock of India's energy mix for the foreseeable future, particularly for powering energy-intensive sectors such as steel, cement, and heavy transportation. Securing a reliable, domestic supply of oil and natural gas is not an alternative to the green transition; it is the essential bridge that provides the macroeconomic stability and the fiscal space required to finance the colossal investments needed for decarbonization.

However, realizing this objective requires a radical overhaul of India’s deeply flawed Exploration and Production (E&P) ecosystem. Despite possessing substantial sedimentary basins with significant estimated hydrocarbon potential, India remains vastly under-explored. The primary culprit is a historically convoluted, bureaucratic, and highly restrictive regulatory regime that has systematically deterred private and foreign investment. The exploration sector has been characterized by interminable delays in environmental clearances, archaic fiscal regimes, and an overarching framework that prioritizes bureaucratic control over operational efficiency. Consequently, global energy majors, possessing the requisite deep-water technology and risk capital, have largely bypassed the Indian E&P sector in favor of more lucrative and investor-friendly jurisdictions.

The recent implementation of the Open Acreage Licensing Policy (OALP) and the shift towards a revenue-sharing model represent positive, albeit delayed, steps toward liberalization. However, to trigger a genuine resurgence in exploration activity, the government must adopt a far more aggressive and transformative approach. The overarching priority must be to drastically reduce the "time to first oil"—the excruciatingly long gestation period between the award of a block and the commencement of commercial production. This necessitates the establishment of a robust, single-window clearance mechanism, bypassing the labyrinthine inter-ministerial approvals that currently paralyze operations. Furthermore, the fiscal regime must be highly incentivized and globally competitive, acknowledging the inherent geological risks associated with exploration in relatively unproven basins.

Crucially, the "drill deeper" mandate must also extend to the aggressive deployment of Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) and Improved Oil Recovery (IOR) technologies in India’s mature, declining oilfields. A significant proportion of India’s current domestic production is derived from aging assets, particularly those operated by the state-owned ONGC. The injection of advanced technologies, potentially facilitated through strategic partnerships with specialized global service providers, can substantially increase the recovery factor from these existing reservoirs, providing a rapid and cost-effective boost to domestic output without the extended lead times associated with greenfield exploration.

Furthermore, a specific focus must be directed toward the accelerated monetization of India’s natural gas reserves. Natural gas, with its significantly lower carbon footprint compared to coal and oil, is the designated transition fuel in India's energy strategy. Expanding the domestic production of natural gas, coupled with massive investments in the national gas grid infrastructure, is vital for transitioning industrial clusters and urban transportation networks toward a cleaner, more efficient energy source, while simultaneously reducing the reliance on expensive Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) imports.

In conclusion, while the ultimate objective of achieving a sustainable, zero-carbon energy architecture remains sacrosanct, the immediate realities of global geopolitics and macroeconomic vulnerability demand a pragmatic, uncompromising focus on energy security. India cannot afford to remain hostage to the volatility of global oil markets while it constructs its green energy future. The aggressive revitalization of the domestic exploration and production sector is not a regression, but an indispensable strategic imperative. By dismantling regulatory bottlenecks, incentivizing technological deployment, and aggressively unlocking its domestic hydrocarbon potential, India can build the robust, self-reliant energy bridge necessary to navigate the current crisis and successfully complete its long-term transition.
