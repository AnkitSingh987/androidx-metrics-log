Title: Understanding a Bank Form

Category: Intermediate

Mateo sat at a small, round table in the university library, staring at a piece of paper that seemed to be written in a secret code. Mateo was an international student from Colombia, currently studying engineering in his second year. He spoke English quite well and usually had no trouble understanding his professors or chatting with his classmates. However, the document in front of him was not an engineering textbook; it was a credit card application form from a local bank.

Mateo needed a credit card to help build his credit history in his new country, and to have a safe way to pay for emergency expenses. He had walked into a bank branch earlier that morning, asked for an application, and confidently brought it to the library to fill out. But as he read through the boxes and the small text, his confidence disappeared. 

He took out his phone to translate a few words, but the direct translations didn't make much sense in the context of the sentence. Feeling frustrated, he sent a quick text message to his friend Julia. Julia was a local student who studied finance, and she often helped Mateo practice his conversational English.

"Are you busy?" Mateo texted. "I am at the library fighting with a bank form. I think the bank is winning. Help please!"

Ten minutes later, Julia appeared, carrying two large cups of coffee. She smiled and placed one cup in front of Mateo. "I brought reinforcements," she said, sitting down. "Show me this terrible monster."

Mateo pushed the application form across the table. "Look at this," he complained, pointing at the second section. "It asks for my 'Residential Status.' What does that mean? Am I a resident if I only have a student visa?"

Julia pulled a pen from her bag and looked at the form. "Ah, bank vocabulary. Don't worry, it confuses native speakers too. Residential status just means your current living situation. The options here are 'Homeowner,' 'Renting,' or 'Living with parents.' Since you rent an apartment near the university, you should check the box for 'Renting.'"

"Okay, that makes sense," Mateo said, making a small mark on the paper. "But look at the next line. It asks for my 'Permanent Address' and then my 'Mailing Address.' Aren't those the same thing?"

"Usually, yes," Julia explained. "But for international students, they can be different. Your permanent address could be your family home in Colombia, because that is where you are officially from. But your mailing address is where you want the bank to send your physical mail, like the actual credit card and your monthly statements. So, put your current apartment address here for the mailing address."

Mateo carefully printed his apartment number and street name in the small white boxes. "Alright. Now we reach the worst part. The financial section." He pointed to a box labeled 'Annual Gross Income.' "I work part-time at the university bookstore. I get paid by the hour. How do I calculate my annual gross income?"

"Gross income is an important term," Julia said, taking a sip of her coffee. "Gross means the total amount of money you earn before any taxes or deductions are taken out. Net income is what you actually receive in your bank account after taxes. The bank wants to know your total earning potential. How many hours do you usually work in a week?"

"About twenty hours," Mateo replied. "And I make fifteen dollars an hour."

Julia did some quick math on her phone. "So, that is three hundred dollars a week. Multiply that by fifty-two weeks in a year, and your annual gross income is roughly fifteen thousand, six hundred dollars. You should write that number there."

Mateo wrote the number down, feeling a little embarrassed that it wasn't a very large amount. "Will they give me a credit card with such a small income?"

"For a student credit card, yes," Julia reassured him. "They don't expect students to be rich. They just want to see that you have some regular money coming in to pay your bills."

They moved down the page. Mateo stopped at a question that asked, 'Number of Dependents.' He looked at Julia, confused. "What is a dependent? Does it mean people I depend on, like my parents?"

Julia laughed softly. "No, it is the opposite. A dependent is someone who relies on you for financial support. For example, children are dependents. Since you are single and don't have any kids, your number of dependents is zero."

Mateo smiled and wrote a big zero in the box. The form was finally starting to make sense. With Julia's help, they quickly finished the section about his employment details and his monthly rent payment. 

Finally, they reached the very bottom of the page. There was a large block of tiny, unreadable text, followed by a signature line. Above the line, in bold letters, it said: 'Declaration of Consent.'

"Do I really need to read all of this?" Mateo asked, groaning at the small print. 

"Honestly, most people don't read every single word," Julia admitted. "But you should understand what you are signing. This declaration means you are promising that all the information you provided is true. It also gives the bank permission to check your credit history and contact your employer to verify your job. If you lie on this form, it is considered fraud."

"I definitely did not lie," Mateo said. "Except maybe I write slightly better than I speak."

"Your English is great, Mateo," Julia smiled. "And your bank form is officially complete."

Mateo signed his name neatly on the line and dated it. He let out a long breath of relief. "Thank you so much, Julia. I really appreciate your help. If I did this alone, I probably would have told the bank that I owned a house and had five children."

Julia laughed. "Well, that certainly would have made your application interesting! Come on, let's take this to the bank branch before they close. And then, you owe me a real lunch to pay for my expert financial consulting services."

Mateo happily agreed. The scary bank form was defeated, and he felt one step closer to establishing his independent life in a new country.
