Title: The Street That Needed Repair

Category: Intermediate


Pine Avenue was supposed to be a quiet, pleasant residential street. It was lined with old, beautiful trees and comfortable family homes with small gardens. However, for the past two years, nobody in the neighborhood described Pine Avenue as pleasant. In fact, most people completely avoided driving on it if they could. The problem was the road itself. The gray asphalt was completely broken, ruined by years of harsh winters, heavy delivery trucks, and total neglect from the local city government. The street was covered in massive, deep potholes that looked like small craters on the moon. 

Every time a car drove down Pine Avenue, you could hear loud bangs and crashes as the tires hit the deep holes. During the rainy season, the huge potholes filled with muddy water, hiding their true depth and making the road even more dangerous for drivers and cyclists. 

Robert lived halfway down Pine Avenue. He was a careful driver, and he always drove incredibly slowly when he was coming home from his office job. He knew exactly where the worst holes were located, and he tried to steer his small blue car around them like he was playing a complex video game. However, one rainy Tuesday evening in November, his luck finally ran out. 

It was dark, and the rain was falling heavily, making the street lights reflect blindingly on the wet road. Robert was driving home, tired after a very long day at work. Suddenly, he heard a terrible, loud 'CRACK!' from the front of his car. The steering wheel jerked violently in his hands, and the car immediately started pulling strongly to the right. 

Robert safely stopped the car on the side of the road, turned on his hazard lights, and stepped out into the cold rain. He looked down at his front right wheel with a sinking feeling in his stomach. The tire was completely flat, and the metal wheel itself was bent and broken. He had driven straight into a new, incredibly deep pothole that had been completely hidden by the dark rainwater. 

"This is ridiculous!" Robert shouted angrily into the empty street, kicking his broken tire. "I cannot afford to buy a new wheel right now!"

The next morning, Robert was forced to take the crowded public bus to work, leaving his broken car parked sadly on the street. While he was waiting at the bus stop, his neighbor, Emily, walked over to him. Emily was a retired librarian who always knew everything that was happening in the neighborhood.

"Good morning, Robert," Emily said, holding a colorful umbrella. "I saw your poor car this morning. Did the street finally claim another victim?"

"Yes, it did," Robert sighed, looking annoyed. "I hit that massive hole near the old oak tree. My wheel is completely ruined. It's going to cost me at least three hundred dollars to fix it. I'm furious. We pay high local taxes every year, but the city refuses to fix our road."

Emily nodded sympathetically. "I know. It's completely unacceptable. Last week, the mail delivery truck almost lost its bumper in the exact same spot. I've called the city's maintenance department three times this month, but they always give me the same boring excuse. They say there is no money left in the road repair budget for this year."

"Well, complaining on the phone clearly isn't working," Robert said, frustrated. "We can't just wait until someone gets seriously hurt or has a terrible accident. We need to do something more."

Emily’s eyes lit up with sudden determination. "You are absolutely right, Robert. A single phone call is very easy for them to ignore. But they cannot ignore an entire neighborhood. We need to organize ourselves and demand action. Are you willing to help me start a formal petition?"

Robert agreed immediately. He was tired of feeling helpless. Over the next few days, Robert and Emily worked together after he finished work. They typed up a very formal, clear letter addressed directly to the city council and the local mayor. The letter detailed the terrible condition of Pine Avenue, the damage to several vehicles, and the serious safety risks for children and cyclists. 

On Saturday morning, they printed fifty copies of the petition and started walking down Pine Avenue together, knocking on every single door. 

At first, some neighbors were skeptical. Mr. Jenkins, an older man who rarely drove, told them it was a waste of time. "The politicians don't care about us," he grumbled, leaning on his doorframe. "They only fix the roads in the wealthy neighborhoods downtown."

"That might be true right now, Mr. Jenkins," Emily argued politely but firmly. "But if we all stay quiet, they definitely won't fix it. If we all sign this petition, we force them to listen to our complaints on official record."

Eventually, even Mr. Jenkins signed the paper. Everyone on the street was angry about the potholes, and they were happy that someone was finally taking real leadership. They collected over eighty signatures in just two days. They also asked neighbors to send them photographs of their damaged cars and pictures of the deepest potholes filled with water. 

Robert compiled all the evidence into a thick, professional-looking folder. He included the long list of signatures, printed photographs, and estimates from local mechanics showing the high cost of car repairs caused by the terrible road. 

On Tuesday afternoon, Robert and Emily took the morning off work and drove to the impressive City Hall building downtown. They requested a meeting with the head of the public works department, but the secretary told them he was busy.

"That is fine," Emily said politely but with a sharp voice. "We will wait here in the lobby until he has five minutes to speak with us. We represent the eighty residents of Pine Avenue, and we have a formal complaint."

They waited in the uncomfortable plastic chairs for two long hours. Finally, a tired-looking official named Mr. Barnes came out of his office to see them. He looked annoyed, expecting them to just complain loudly. 

However, Robert did not shout. He simply handed Mr. Barnes the heavy folder. 

"Mr. Barnes, we are here representing our entire neighborhood," Robert said calmly and professionally. "Pine Avenue is no longer safe for public use. We have documented severe damage to multiple vehicles, which you can see in these photographs. We have eighty signatures from angry taxpayers demanding immediate repairs. If the city does not address this dangerous situation by the end of the month, we will take this entire folder directly to the local television news station."

Mr. Barnes opened the folder and looked at the photographs. His expression changed from annoyed to concerned. The pictures clearly showed holes that were deep enough to cause a serious accident. He looked at the long list of signatures. He knew that bad publicity on the local news was the last thing the city mayor wanted before the upcoming elections. 

"I... I see," Mr. Barnes said quietly, closing the folder. "I did not realize the situation on Pine Avenue had become this severe. You have prepared a very thorough report. Let me speak with the budget committee this afternoon. I will see if we can find emergency funds for temporary repairs immediately, with a full repaving scheduled for early spring."

Robert and Emily thanked him and left the building. They felt cautiously optimistic, but they knew they couldn't celebrate yet. 

Two days later, Robert was drinking his morning coffee when he heard a loud, unfamiliar noise outside. He looked out his front window and smiled broadly. A large, yellow city maintenance truck was parked directly in front of his house. Several workers in bright orange vests were pouring hot, steaming asphalt into the deepest potholes on the street, flattening it with heavy machines. 

It was not a perfect, brand-new road yet, but it was safe to drive on again. 

Emily walked out of her house, waving excitedly at Robert. "We did it!" she called out over the noise of the machines. 

Robert walked out and joined her on the sidewalk, watching the workers fix the street. "Yes, we did," he agreed happily. "It just goes to show that complaining quietly gets you nowhere, but working together and demanding action actually gets the job done."

The broken wheel on Robert's car had been expensive and incredibly frustrating, but it had finally forced the neighborhood to stand up for themselves. From that day on, whenever there was a problem on Pine Avenue, the residents didn't just complain to themselves. They knew exactly how to organize, how to speak up, and how to make the city listen.
