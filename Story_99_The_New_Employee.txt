Title: The New Employee

Category: Intermediate

Chloe walked into the bright, modern office of ‘Creative Solutions’, a successful marketing agency in London. As a team leader, she was responsible for managing a group of six designers and copywriters. She enjoyed her role because she liked helping creative people develop their skills and produce great work. Recently, the agency had won a major contract with a large sportswear brand, and Chloe’s team was under a lot of pressure to deliver a new advertising campaign by the end of the month.

To help with the extra workload, the company had hired a new graphic designer named Julian. Julian had an impressive portfolio and had graduated at the top of his university class. He was quiet, polite, and seemed very eager to prove himself. Chloe had assigned him to work on the visual concepts for the sportswear campaign, giving him a list of specific requirements and a deadline of Friday afternoon.

It was now Thursday morning, and Chloe had scheduled a brief meeting to check on Julian’s progress. She found him sitting at his desk, staring intensely at his computer screen. He looked tired, and there were three empty coffee cups next to his keyboard.

"Good morning, Julian," Chloe said cheerfully, pulling up a chair beside him. "How are the concepts coming along? I’d love to see what you have done so far."

Julian jumped slightly, startled by her voice. He quickly minimized a window on his screen. "Oh, hi Chloe. Um, they are coming along fine. I'm just making some final adjustments to the colors."

"Great," Chloe replied, ignoring his nervous behavior. "Can we take a quick look? Even if it's just a rough draft, it’s good to get some feedback before you spend too much time on the details."

Julian hesitated, his hands hovering over the mouse. Slowly, he opened the file he had been working on. 

When Chloe looked at the screen, she felt a sudden drop in her stomach. The designs were beautiful, highly artistic, and very modern. However, they were completely wrong for the client. The sportswear brand wanted a bright, energetic, and aggressive style that appealed to young athletes. Julian’s designs were dark, moody, and very minimalist, looking more suitable for an expensive perfume brand. 

"These are very interesting, Julian," Chloe started carefully, trying to find the right words. "You clearly have a lot of talent. But, I think we have a slight misunderstanding about the client's brief. Did you read the brand guidelines I sent you on Monday?"

Julian looked down at his desk, his face turning slightly pink. "Well, I started reading them, but they were very long, and I thought my new concept would be more unique. I wanted to impress the client with something different, rather than just copying what they usually do."

Chloe sighed internally. It was a common mistake for young designers to prioritize their own artistic vision over the client's actual needs. 

"I appreciate your ambition, Julian, but in commercial marketing, we have to follow the client's guidelines strictly," Chloe explained gently. "They have spent millions building a specific brand identity. If we ignore that, they will reject the work, no matter how beautiful it is. We have a presentation on Monday morning, which means these designs need to be completely redone by tomorrow evening."

Julian’s eyes widened in panic. "Redone? Completely? But that took me four days! I don't think I can finish a whole new set of concepts by tomorrow. Why didn't anyone tell me I was going in the wrong direction?"

"Julian, I scheduled a feedback session with you on Tuesday afternoon, but you sent me a message saying you were too busy and didn't need any help," Chloe reminded him softly. "When you start a new job, nobody expects you to know everything immediately. But we do expect you to ask questions when you are unsure."

Julian rubbed his eyes, looking defeated. "I'm sorry, Chloe. At my university, if you asked the professors for help, they thought you were weak or not creative enough. I just wanted to show you that I could handle this project independently. I didn't want you to think you made a mistake by hiring me."

Chloe immediately understood the problem. Julian was suffering from 'imposter syndrome'—the fear that he wasn't good enough and that he had to prove his worth by working in complete isolation. He lacked the understanding of how a collaborative corporate environment actually functioned. 

"Julian, look around the office," Chloe said, pointing to the other desks. Two senior designers were standing together, arguing passionately about a font choice, while a copywriter was laughing and sharing ideas with a project manager. "We are a team. We don't work in secret, and we don't expect perfection on the first try. Creativity in an agency is a collaborative process."

She leaned closer, lowering her voice. "Asking for help isn't a sign of weakness; it’s a sign of professionalism. It shows that you care more about getting the project right than protecting your own ego. If you hide your work until the deadline, you waste time and risk failing the client."

Julian took a deep breath and nodded slowly. "I understand. I really messed this up, didn't I? Are you going to take me off the project?"

"No, I'm not taking you off the project," Chloe said firmly. "But we are going to change how you work. You have exactly thirty-six hours to fix this. You cannot do it alone."

Chloe stood up and walked over to Marcus, one of her most experienced senior designers. She quickly explained the situation. Marcus, who was known for his speed and positive attitude, immediately agreed to help. 

For the rest of Thursday and all of Friday, Julian did not work in isolation. He sat beside Marcus, and they worked together. Marcus showed Julian how to interpret the brand guidelines, how to use energetic color palettes, and how to design quickly without getting stuck on tiny details. Every two hours, Chloe came over to review their progress, offering quick, constructive feedback. 

Julian was amazed at how different it felt. Whenever he felt unsure, he simply asked a question. Instead of being judged, he received immediate, helpful answers. The heavy burden of trying to be perfect all by himself had disappeared. 

By Friday evening at 6:00 PM, the new concepts were finished. They were bright, energetic, and perfectly matched the client's brief, while still showing off Julian's unique talent for clean layouts. 

Chloe looked at the final designs on the large presentation screen and smiled. "These are fantastic, Julian. Excellent work. The client is going to love these on Monday."

Julian slumped in his chair, exhausted but smiling. "Thanks, Chloe. And thank you, Marcus. I couldn't have done it without your help."

"That's the whole point, Julian," Chloe said, picking up her bag to leave for the weekend. "You are a great designer. But remember, the most valuable tool you have in this office isn't your computer or your software. It’s your voice. Never be afraid to use it to ask for directions."

Julian nodded, finally understanding the true culture of the company. He realized that being a good employee didn't mean never making mistakes; it meant learning how to communicate and collaborate to solve them.
