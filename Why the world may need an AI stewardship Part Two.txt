Title: Why the world may need an AI stewardship Part Two

Why the world may need an AI stewardship treaty

Strategic autonomy in the twenty-first century may depend as much on access to intelligence as it once depended on access to energy, telecommunications or defence capabilities. None of this implies that frontier AI should simply become freely available to everyone. That would be irresponsible. Legitimate security concerns must be addressed.

The world does need robust safeguards against catastrophic misuse. But preventing misuse is fundamentally different from preventing capability. The emerging international conversation risks conflating the two. If AI is becoming global public infrastructure rather than merely another strategic technology, then its governance cannot be modelled solely on arms-control regimes.

It must also address questions of equitable access, institutional legitimacy and shared stewardship. Hybrid treaty framework The appropriate precedent may therefore not be a single treaty but a hybrid framework. One element could draw from the logic of the Chemical Weapons Convention: establishing internationally agreed prohibitions against clearly harmful applications of artificial intelligence, together with verification mechanisms and independent oversight.

Another could borrow from international regimes governing domains of shared global importance such as the Law of the Sea and the Outer Space Treaty, which recognise that resources of profound significance require cooperative governance rather than exclusive control by a small number of states. The objective would not be to declare AI itself the common property of humanity. Rather, it would recognise that a technology capable of shaping the future of civilisation cannot be governed solely through unilateral export controls or corporate discretion.

Such a framework would distinguish between restricting malicious uses of AI and denying responsible nations access to frontier capabilities. Safety, equity, accountability and international legitimacy would become complementary objectives rather than competing ones. The world has entered an era in which intelligence itself is becoming infrastructure.

Infrastructure of such consequence cannot indefinitely remain under the stewardship of a technological oligopoly  -  whether governmental or corporate. A genuinely multipolar AI ecosystem, involving the United States, Europe, India, China and other capable innovation centres, may ultimately prove more stable than one in which technological supremacy is concentrated within a narrow group of nations. Multipolarity does not eliminate competition.

But it reduces the ability of any single bloc to define the future of intelligence for everyone else. The debate before us is therefore much larger than whether AI models should be open or closed. It is about the architecture of global governance for humanity's most consequential technology. Perhaps what the world actually needs is an AI Stewardship Treaty: one that prohibits harmful uses of artificial intelligence while ensuring that its transformative benefits remain safely, equitably and legitimately accessible to all responsible nations.

The objective should not be to monopolise intelligence. It should be to govern it wisely. By GBS Bindra & Rahul Patwardhan The writers are Distinguished Fellows at the Avinyum Foundation
