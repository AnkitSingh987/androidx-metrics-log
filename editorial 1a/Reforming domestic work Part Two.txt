Title: Reforming domestic work Part Two

Reforming domestic work

A market-compatible reform path This approach does not require abandoning labour protections. Instead, it reframes them. If policy were to encourage professional domestic service organisations - through easier registration, skill certification, access to social security schemes, and light-touch regulation - market competition could gradually lift wages and standards.

Such a model addresses the Court's fear of litigation while responding to the moral imperative of reducing exploitation. It also acknowledges a social reality often overlooked: for many workers, status and dignity matter as much as wages. Reform beyond the courtroom India's domestic work sector is too large, too intimate, and too informal to be transformed solely through judicial mandates.

Courts are right to be cautious. The future of domestic work reform may lie in building institutions rather than filing petitions - in creating professional pathways where dignity, protection, and economic viability coexist. Sometimes, the most sustainable justice is delivered not through enforcement, but through better design.

Not platform work, not informal agencies This model should not be confused with platform-based gig work or the mushrooming nursing and care service centres seen in many cities today. In most such arrangements, workers are treated as independent contractors rather than employees - without job security, assured wages, or meaningful social protection. The proposed approach is fundamentally different.

It rests on the principle of direct employment, where domestic workers are recognised as employees of the service organisation, with defined salaries, predictable working hours, and access to basic benefits. The objective is not to create another layer of precarious intermediaries, but to build accountable institutions that assume employer responsibility. Without this distinction, formalisation risks replicating informality under a new name  -  an outcome neither workers nor households can afford.

By Prabir Kumar Bandyopadhyay The writer is a retired management professor
