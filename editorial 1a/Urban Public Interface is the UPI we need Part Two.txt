Title: Urban Public Interface is the UPI we need Part Two

Urban Public Interface is the UPI we need

If a civic platform reaches most residents of a city, verified and simple, the city acquires a stage for something close to a rolling referendum. Should a market street go pedestrian-only on weekends? Ask the people who walk it. Where should the new bus route run? Draw it from the commuting signals of those who will ride it.

Which ward's services are slipping? Publish the ratings monthly, like a scoreboard. Let there be healthy competition. Cooperative federalism cannot be limited between the centre and the states, it needs to percolate in the daily lives of citizens. Participatory budgeting, which today hardly generates any kind of serious attention, will start to be taken seriously.

Resident Welfare Associations can play a crucial role in advocating for this change in participation patterns of residents of gated societies. Smartphones are good for Instagram reels but if smartly utilised could incrementally increase the quality of life in urban centres in a short period of time. If most citizens are on board, it is the entire game.

A platform that samples only the smar tphone -rich and the chronically online generation will faithfully magnify their priorities and call it the city's voice. So, the design requirements must be known in advance, with identities verified, so a campaign cannot impersonate a crowd. There must be assisted access for residents without smartphones.

Every major language in the city must be used and, above all, there must be visible response to grievances. Sensors will go quiet if nobody acts on them, and the surest way to lose a citizen's signal is to ignore it once. India has spent the last decade proving it can build rails for money and identity at population scale.

The civic rail is the one we need the most. A city that can feel itself continuously, through its own residents, is the precondition for every other urban fix we debate. And a resident whose report visibly moves the machinery acquires something no government campaign or slogan can manufacture: ownership of the city by its citizens.

The country's DPI has taken the whole-of-nation approach. As a city, we need to take a whole-of-city approach which cannot be successfully undertaken without the most important organ of the city, its citizens, which populate it. By SAMRAT BANERJEE (The writer is a Digital Advisor at Indo-German Development Cooperation, working on triangular cooperation, digital public infrastructure, and institutional readiness for technology deployment.

He also volunteers with iSPIRT)
