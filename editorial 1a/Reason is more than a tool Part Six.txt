Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Six

Reason is more than a tool

A view of reason that is used to silence or instrumentalise others contradicts itself, because reason is not a private weapon but a common tribunal  -  the space in which claims and ends must become answerable to all, as equal bearers of dignity. That is precisely what the Humean servant model cannot say. If reason is only an instrument for satisfying antecedent ends, then it cannot condemn domination as irrational or at odds with human dignity.

After all, if it is not contrary to reason 'to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger', then reason has already surrendered the authority Kant most wanted to defend, which is our ability to judge not only how we pursue our ends, but whether those ends are worthy of us in the first place. This, then, was the nightmare hidden inside the dream of mastery. Human beings develop reason, Kant thought, in order to free themselves from arbitrary power.

But when reason is reduced to instrumental calculation, it turns back on them. Nature becomes raw material. Animals become units of production. Workers become human resources. Citizens become data points, or worse. The Frankfurt School found that the reduction of human beings to manipulable objects follows naturally once society allows that reason itself is nothing more than a tool for getting what we want.

This is one of the reasons why Pope Leo XIV's encyclical Magnifica Humanitas (2025) is interesting even for readers far outside Catholic social teaching. Its idiom is theological, but its central anxiety overlaps with Kantian and Frankfurt School worries about the technocratic paradigm. When efficiency, control and profit become 'the standard by which everything is judged', they begin 'to dictate what matters and what can be discarded.' The encyclical warns that making efficiency the ultimate measure of value tempts human beings to see themselves, and to treat others, as mere means rather than as persons in the Kantian sense  -  that is, as bearers of dignity.

Hence the pope's striking counterclaim: 'The quality of a civilisation is measured not by the power of its means, but by the care it is able to offer.' The engineers of our AI-soaked future are not blind to the dangers of instrumental reason. As Anca Dragan, head of AI Safety and Alignment at Google DeepMind, observed in an interview in 2017: 'things can go wrong, precisely because we are bad at specifying objectives and constraints for AI agents.' Yet the field's most serious attempts to confront that danger often lapse, unhelpfully, back into Humeanism. The so-called AI 'alignment problem' is a case in point.

It is usually framed as a technical challenge: how do we ensure that machines pursue human values rather than destroying them or imposing their own? This is a genuine problem and recent rogue AI incidents should have us extremely worried. But there is a deeper issue. What happens if human values themselves are formed inside systems that increasingly understand intelligence as mere optimisation?

What if we become so accustomed to being predicted, nudged, ranked and served that we lose confidence in the older, harder activity of asking what ends deserve our allegiance in the first place? The danger is only partly that the machines may fail to align with us. A deeper and largely ignored concern is how, long before that happens, we may have become too well aligned with them.
