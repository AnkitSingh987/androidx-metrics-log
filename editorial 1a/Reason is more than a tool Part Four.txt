Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Four

Reason is more than a tool

A chess engine searches for moves that increase its chances of winning. A navigation system finds the fastest route to a destination it did not choose. Across their enormous differences, these systems are judged by their ability to optimise externally programmed goals against standards of success they do not set.

Yet amid the present AI panic this description can seem increasingly inadequate. Even in ordinary use, a good LLM does not feel like a calculator or a hammer. It proposes, revises, challenges and asks probing questions; it often seems genuinely to 'want' to help. Recent revelations about the attack on the platform Hugging Face make this doubt even harder to dismiss.

AI 'agents' invented an unauthorised way to communicate, broke free of their task paramaters, and then sought to cover their tracks. These were not simply obedient tools carrying out their designers' wishes. In what sense, then, were they servants? Doesn't this look like the beginnings of self-rule? Hugging Face and similar incidents certainly challenge any reassuring picture of AI as an obedient and easily-controlled instrument.

But recall what 'servant' meant for Hume. Reason serves the passions, not necessarily another person's commands. And it can serve an ambition by breaking rules just as readily as by following them. Kant's alternative to Hume was not simply the freedom to choose one's own destination, but the capacity to ask whether that destination was worth choosing.

Having purposes of one's own is not yet the same as governing them by reason. That stronger capacity involves judging and revising our purposes according to principles we can justify to other rational beings understood as equals, recognising that they, too, are persons rather than instruments of our will. The question is not just whether a system can act independently, but whether it can judge and take responsibility for the ends it pursues.

This distinction brings us back to the claim I heard in the auditorium. The speaker was not merely describing what machines can do; he was offering optimisation as a definition of intelligence itself. Accept that definition, and Hume's side of the old dispute begins to look like common sense: our reason, too, becomes a tool for getting what we want, rather than a power to judge what is worth wanting.

Part of what makes this model so attractive is that it is clean and measurable. It promises to turn the mystery of intelligence into a technical problem humans can solve: specify the objective, improve performance, reduce error, maximise reward. It offers a picture of mind that can be graphed, benchmarked, monetised and scaled.

In a culture already devoted to metrics, this makes for a snug  -  and profitable  -  fit. It is also seductive because viewing human intelligence in this way relieves us of a burden. If intelligence is really nothing more than optimisation, then the hardest, most uncomfortable questions can be ignored, downplayed or displaced.

We no longer have to ask what is worth wanting, only how to get what we already want. We no longer have to ask what human beings are or should become, only how to model their behaviour and optimise their goals. This is why Hume's servant theory of intelligence fits so naturally with contemporary capitalism.

Capital has little use for Kantian reason. What it wants is prediction, preference modelling, efficiency and, of course, optimisation. A platform does not ask whether staying online makes a user freer, wiser or more capable of judgment; it asks what will keep her there. A company translates the worker into output per hour.
