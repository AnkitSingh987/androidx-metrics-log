Title: Skills gap mismanaged Part Two

Skills gap mismanaged

India produces over 4 million graduates annually (AISHE), yet employability studies indicate that only about half are immediately employable for formal-sector roles. Outdated curricula and limited industry exposure are real challenges. But this is precisely why industry participation is indispensable.

Without sustained engagement from employers, universities lack real-time feedback loops. NEP has opened the door; industry must now walk through it. Finally, the issue of corporate responsibility must be addressed candidly. While firms exist to create shareholder value, they also benefit from publicly funded education, demographic scale, and policy support.

India's earlier IT success was not achieved by outsourcing skilling entirely to individuals - it was built on shared investment in human capital. From a policy perspective, the way forward is clear: · Incentivize corporate trainee and apprenticeship programs · Reward deep industry - institute partnerships, not symbolic ones · Enable consortia-based training infrastructure · Align public funding with employment outcomes, not just enrolment India today faces a paradox: a young population with rising educational aspirations, alongside persistent graduate underemployment and industry complaints of skill shortages. This cannot be resolved by expecting individuals to endlessly self-skill, nor by universities reforming in isolation.

History offers clarity. India did not become an IT powerhouse by waiting for perfect graduates. It succeeded because industry once believed in potential over perfection, supported by policy alignment. As India enters its next technological transition, the real question is not whether skill gaps exist  -  they always will.

The question is whether industry is willing to match policy intent with participation, and share the cost of transition rather than externalizing it entirely. Rebuilding structured, scalable, and modern entry-level skilling is not nostalgia. It is policy realism  -  and economic foresight. By Prabir Kumar Bandyopadhyay The writer is a retired management professor
