Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Three

Reason is more than a tool

To give oneself the law is not to invent a private rule to suit oneself, but to act on rules whose authority one can recognise for oneself and that any other rational being could, in principle, recognise, too. A law can therefore be one's own without being one's alone. It is mine because I can recognise its authority for myself, and common because any other rational being could, in principle, do the same.

Its universality is precisely what makes it freeing: I am ruled neither by my passing desires nor by the arbitrary will of another, but by a principle I can understand and endorse as an equal among equals. To act autonomously, then, is not to enthrone my private goals and preferences, but to test my actions and possible ends against standards that any rational being could share. It is the opposite of both arbitrary self-assertion and slavery to passion.

This is why Kant's philosophy of reason culminates in the equal human dignity of all persons. For Kant, human beings, as rational, self-determining agents, have an absolute worth that cannot be priced, a dignity that does not depend on any other value. We may use things to get what we want. We may exchange services.

We may build tools for all kinds of purposes. But persons are never mere tools, never mere means, never mere resources or obstacles to others' ends. Why not? Because, through reason, they are the very source of purposiveness itself. They set and select and critique their own ends. Even further, persons are not merely bearers of purposes, but beings capable of taking responsibility for them, of asking what they ought to pursue and how they can justify it to others.

Their dignity lies not simply in originating goals of their own, but in their capacity for rational self-government. It follows that to treat someone as a mere means is not only to degrade them. It is to misrecognise what they are. Here, finally, is the full force of the contrast between reason as servant and reason as sovereign.

For Hume, human beings are clever instruments of whatever desires happen to move them  -  more calculating than animals, certainly, but not free in any deeper sense. They can optimise, strategise and pursue an end, but they cannot judge whether that end is worthy. Kant's reply is that this picture leaves out the very power that makes us moral beings, the very power that endows us with dignity: the capacity to step back from desire, ask whether our ends can be justified, and bind ourselves to laws we give ourselves.

Hume gives us intelligence as instrument. Kant gives us reason as self-rule. The difference is nothing less than the difference between a tool and a person. The AI revolution has made this old dispute newly urgent by bringing the Humean view of reason back with a vengeance, this time in code. The route back to Hume was practical rather than philosophical.

In the absence of any settled account of intelligence, much AI research has proceeded operationally: a system counts as intelligent insofar as it succeeds at a set task. But success is always success relative to some end and, in AI, that end is typically supplied by human engineers. A reinforcement-learning system learns to maximise cumulative reward.
