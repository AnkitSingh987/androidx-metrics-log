Title: The United Nations long road to restoring Part Two

The United Nations' long road to restoring trust

Fourth, though the bulk of the UN's programme delivery is in the Global South and trust in the UN is higher in the Global South (UN Foundation poll), only a fraction of the decision-making power rests with the Global South. Developing nations, which account for 84% of the world's population, hold only 44% of UN civil service leadership positions. Only a third of the UN leadership in Africa is African.

Only two-fifths of the cities in which various UN entities have headquarters are cities in the Global South. When, as part of a cost-cutting measure, thousands of posts were moved out of New York and Geneva in 2025, cities in the Global South (except those mentioned) got a miss despite their cost advantages. Reverse the shrinking space The UN commendably delivered global humanitarian assistance and protection in 2025-26 to 98 million people, or roughly 1.1% of the world population.

But aid effectiveness and the proportion of funds transferred to local actors remains an issue. In wars such as Ukraine, Gaza and Iran, the UN made tactical interventions such as keeping grain exports flowing through the Black Sea, or preventing an oil spill in the Red Sea, but could not build on the goodwill thus earned to create mechanisms for the belligerents to sit and talk. Taking the initiative to facilitate peacemaking has become untenably centralised at the highest rungs of the organisation's bureaucratic ladder.

It is time that the UN moved to enhance its transparency and remake its ties with the people of the world, and not just the governments. The United Nations is more than an inter-governmental organisation. It reflects the best of humanity's hopes for itself. The "transformation" that the 81st General Assembly aims for, would indeed have to start, by restoring trust.

Raja Karthikeya is Adjunct Fellow, Takshashila Institution
