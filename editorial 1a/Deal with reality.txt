Title: Deal with reality

Deal with reality

Five years after the Taliban returned to power, Afghanistan presents the world with an uncomfortable fact: a regime can be deeply repressive and still prove remarkably durable. The question for democracies is no longer how to restore the order that collapsed in 2021, but how to deal with the order that replaced it.

The Taliban have consolidated their control. The fighting that once devastated much of the country has largely stopped, and the government in Kabul faces no credible military challenge. But stability has come at a terrible price. Women have been pushed out of workplaces and public life, girls are denied education beyond primary school, and restrictions on movement and personal freedom have intensified.

Afghanistan remains desperately poor, with millions dependent on humanitarian assistance. This creates a dilemma that cannot be resolved by either moral outrage or diplomatic convenience. Punishing the Taliban through isolation has not persuaded them to change. Nor is there evidence that withholding recognition has weakened their grip on power.

Meanwhile, declining foreign aid risks making ordinary Afghans bear the cost of a policy intended to pressure their rulers. Yet it does not follow that formal recognition should simply be handed to the Taliban. Recognition is not merely an acknowledgement of who controls a capital. It is also a source of diplomatic legitimacy and, potentially, leverage.

If the outside world gives that leverage away without securing anything in return, it may discover that it has little left with which to influence the regime. The sensible course lies between ostracism and acceptance. Afghanistan needs functioning diplomatic channels, not diplomatic pretence. Western governments should be able to speak directly to Taliban officials about terrorism, refugees, border security, trade and humanitarian access.

Embassies and diplomats could provide valuable intelligence and consular assistance while giving the outside world a clearer understanding of the forces shaping the regime. Humanitarian aid should also be protected from political disputes as far as possible. A starving or impoverished population is not an effective instrument for changing the behaviour of its rulers.

Indeed, Afghanistan's economic weakness may itself become a source of wider instability, encouraging migration, extremism and dependence on less scrupulous powers. But engagement should remain conditional. The Taliban should be told plainly that deeper diplomatic and economic normalisation depends on measurable changes: restoring girls' education, allowing women to work, permitting humanitarian organisations to operate effectively and creating at least some space for political participation.

There is little reason to expect the Taliban to become liberal democrats. But there is equally little wisdom in pretending they do not govern Afghanistan. The objective should therefore not be to reward them or to isolate them indefinitely. It should be to maximise the world's ability to influence what happens inside Afghanistan while minimising the suffering of its people.

The Taliban have made themselves unavoidable. That does not make them legitimate. It makes diplomacy necessary.
