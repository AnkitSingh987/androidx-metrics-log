Title: EPR in Asia Pacific Japan sorts its plastic Part Two

EPR in Asia Pacific: Japan sorts its plastic better than any country. It still recycles very little of it

The packaging is still sold there. The waste is still created there. But the obligation does not exist. So, in Japan, producer responsibility depends on how ambitious the local government is. If a city decides separate collection is too expensive, the cost does not move to producers. It simply disappears.

This is the most important warning for any country setting up EPR now. If you link what companies owe to what the government manages to collect, the total will always be smaller than the problem and it will be smallest exactly where the system is weakest. The fee is based on weight, so packaging got lighter, not better Companies pay JCPRA by weight and by material type.

In 2024 the rate was about ¥62 per kilogram for plastic packaging. That is roughly Rs 35. The fee does not change with design. There is no extra charge for using multi-layer laminates. Multi-layer pouches are a recycling nightmare, but they weigh practically nothing. By taxing weight instead of recyclability, Japan's fee structure actively subsidised unrecyclable trash.

Japan added the Plastic Resource Circulation Act in 2021, in force from April 2022. It brought in design guidelines and a certification scheme for good products. But guidelines are only advice. The fee is the only part with real force, and the fee does not care about design. The law also does not reach very far.

It applies only to household packaging. Packaging used between businesses shrink wrap, transport crates, pallets, bags around bulk ingredients sits completely outside the system. The expensive part of managing packaging waste is not recycling. It is collection, sorting and cleaning. Delhi-based Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) measured this in five Indian cities and found that 80 to 90 per cent of what a city spends on plastic goes on those first two steps.

Two more costs never show up anywhere. The first is household work. Washing, drying and sorting to Japan's standard takes hours per household per month, and nobody is paid for it. The second is export. Japan still sends more than a million tonnes of plastic waste a year to other Asian countries. Some becomes another country's landfill, while Japan records it as recovered.

The 2035 target has a loophole inside it In 2019, Japan set six plastic targets. Cut single-use plastics by 25 per cent by 2030. Make all packaging reusable or recyclable by 2025. Reuse or recycle 60 per cent of packaging by 2030. Produce two million tonnes of bio-based plastic by 2030. Double the use of recycled material.

And the one that matters: effectively utilise 100 per cent of used plastic by 2035, through reuse, recycling, or thermal recovery. That last phrase is doing the work. It means Japan can meet its main 2035 plastic target by burning everything it fails to recycle, and the target will still count as achieved.

A country that burns about 80 per cent of its household waste has written its incinerators into its own circular economy goal. What India should take from this The danger for India is copying Japan's metrics instead of fixing its incentives. Blaming civic discipline is a convenient excuse for weaker policy.

India's market is already suffocating under multi-layer sachets. If India adopts flat weight-based fees or lets waste-to-energy burning count as 'recycling', it will recreate Japan's illusion, which is pristine statistics on paper and mountains of burning plastic in reality.
