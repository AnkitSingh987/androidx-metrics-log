Category: Editorial
Title: Reforming domestic work Part One

Reforming domestic work

Towards a model that lends dignity and security The Supreme Court's recent refusal to entertain a PIL seeking minimum wages for domestic workers has triggered strong reactions. The Chief Justice's oral observations  -  warning that enforcing minimum wages in private households could lead to widespread litigation and reduced hiring  -  may sound harsh at first glance. Yet this concern reflects a real problem.

Domestic work is informal, happens in private homes, and supports millions of livelihoods. This makes strict legal enforcement hard and sometimes counterproductive. At the same time, the Court acknowledged what cannot be denied - domestic workers are frequently exploited. Long hours, low pay, lack of job security, and social invisibility remain the norm for millions.

The challenge, therefore, is not whether reform is needed, but how to design it without destabilising employment or overwhelming the legal system. One possible answer lies beyond litigation, in institutional innovation. An illustrative experience from Goa During my time working in Goa, I encountered an arrangement that offers a useful lens for thinking about reform.

In the residential quarters where I lived, domestic services were outsourced by the employer to a registered service provider. Individual households did not hire workers directly; instead, they paid a fixed monthly fee to the employer who in turn pay to the organisation that supplied domestic staff. What made this experience notable was not merely the convenience for residents, but the perspective of the workers themselves.

Through informal interactions, many expressed comfort with this model. Being employed through an organisation appeared to reduce hesitation about the nature of domestic work. They spoke of their jobs with greater confidence, often identifying themselves by the name of the organisation rather than the task itself.

The work had not changed - but the social framing of the work had. Several workers also indicated that they were paid better than typical informal arrangements and had clearer expectations regarding hours and responsibilities. While this is only a personal observation and not evidence of a broader trend, it nonetheless illustrates how organised intermediation can alter power dynamics without recourse to courts.

Such arrangements are common in case of corporate sweeping and cleaning work but extending it to the domestic level was unique. One practical factor that enabled this arrangement to function smoothly was spatial concentration. The residences served by the organisation were located within the same housing complex, reducing travel time, coordination costs, and scheduling uncertainty for workers.

This geographical compactness made regular employment viable and predictable. Such conditions may not exist everywhere at the outset, but they point to a realistic pathway for replication. Organised domestic services are most likely to succeed initially in gated communities, large housing complexes, and institutional residential clusters.

As these models stabilise and learn from experience, broader neighbourhood-level services can gradually be designed. Why professionalisation matters The core difficulty with enforcing minimum wages in domestic work lies in the employment relationship itself. When millions of households become de facto employers, compliance monitoring becomes impractical and adversarial.

Litigation, as the Court noted, could overwhelm both families and the justice system. Organised domestic service providers offer a different architecture. By acting as employers, these entities can: · Standardise wages and working conditions · Provide workers with identity, predictability, and grievance mechanisms · Shield households from legal uncertainty · Enable the state to regulate a manageable number of institutions rather than individual homes In effect, this shifts domestic work from a personal dependency to a contractual relationship, where dignity is institutional rather than negotiated.
