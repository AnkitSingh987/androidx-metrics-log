Title: Reason is more than a tool Part Five

Reason is more than a tool

A political campaign replaces the question of what citizens might rationally will together with a more tractable calculation. Which message will move which demographic, and by how much? But once intelligence is defined as optimisation, the question of ends and their value does not go away. It simply passes unnoticed into the hands of whoever  - or whatever  -  controls the objective function.

That is, what drops from view is whether the purposes built into, learned by or generated within these systems deserve to organise our lives. When a society loses the language, practices and institutions for evaluating ends, those ends become dangerously invisible and therefore less amenable to rational assessment. They harden into defaults: profit, speed, efficiency, growth, engagement.

What cannot be justified openly is smuggled in as architecture. This brings about a state Kant would call heteronomy, in which rational beings, capable of self-rule, forfeit their freedom by submitting to the rule of others. Autonomy means making the ends that govern us answerable to reasons we can recognise as our own; heteronomy begins when those ends are simply imposed from without.

If we do not take up the task of setting and judging our own ends, Kant warned, we'll soon find that someone  -  or something  -  has gladly done so on our behalf. That sounds bad. Who would want to forfeit their freedom and have purposes imposed on them from outside? Well, it turns out that self-rule is harder than it sounds.

Kant's influential essay 'What Is Enlightenment?' (1784) begins not with knowledge, but with courage. Enlightenment is humanity's emergence from 'self-incurred immaturity', Kant writes, and its motto is Sapere Aude  -  dare to know. Have courage to use your own understanding. Kant's examples now sound quaint: the book that thinks for me, the pastor who serves as my conscience, the doctor who decides my diet.

But the danger he is naming is deeply familiar. Immaturity is comfortable. It is pleasant to have others do the hard part. Why think, when someone or something can think for me? Why deliberate, when someone or something can decide? AI gives this old temptation a powerful and ever-expanding new interface.

The Frankfurt School saw a version of this danger, too, and long before machine learning came along. Writing in exile during the Second World War, as fascism, antisemitism, Stalinism and monopoly capitalism shattered 19th-century hopes for steady moral progress, Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno and others asked how the Enlightenment's promise of liberation could collapse into new forms of domination. How could societies committed to the promise of science and reason give rise to such destruction and terror?

The problem, they found, was not reason itself, but reason narrowed into mere instrumentality, the capacity to classify, predict, administer, dominate and control, decoupled from any notion of human dignity. Reason so conceived  -  as an intelligent servant  - readily becomes, when enacted at scale, an instrument of barbarity. Yet the fact that instrumental reason can serve domination does not mean that Kant's richer conception of reason offers any automatic safeguard against evil.

Nothing does. The language of Kantian universalism has itself been used to discipline, exclude and humiliate, as if some people were more fully rational, more capable of autonomy, more entitled to speak in the name of humanity than others. But that is not an objection to Kant's conception of reason. It is an objection to its betrayal.
