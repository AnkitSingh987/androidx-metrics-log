Title: Inflations Trap

Inflation's Trap

The world had begun to believe that the inflation crisis was receding. Central banks had reduced interest rates, supply chains had recovered and price pressures had eased. The world had begun to believe that the inflation crisis was receding. Central banks had reduced interest rates, supply chains had recovered and price pressures had eased.

But the latest developments in the United States and Britain suggest that the struggle to restore price stability is far from over. The danger is no longer simply that inflation will remain high, but that efforts to contain it could undermine economic growth. The Federal Reserve's decision to raise its benchmark rate by a quarter percentage point, to 3.75-4.5 per cent, marks its first increase in more than three years.

The move came despite President Donald Trump's repeated demands for lower borrowing costs. Fed chairman Kevin Warsh has argued that inflation remains too high, while the central bank's projections indicate the possibility of further increases. The decision underlines the tension between the political attraction of cheaper credit and the institutional responsibility to contain inflation.

Britain presents a related but more complicated picture. The Bank of England has held its rate at 3.75 per cent, but three of its nine monetary policymakers favoured an increase. Governor Andrew Bailey has warned that prolonged energy-price volatility could require tighter policy. The Bank also expects inflation to rise more sharply than previously anticipated, even as it has upgraded its near-term growth forecast.

These decisions reflect a common predicament. The conflict in West Asia has disrupted energy supplies, pushed up fuel costs and threatens to spread inflation. Central banks cannot end wars or increase oil supplies. They can, however, restrain demand in an attempt to prevent higher costs from becoming embedded in wages and prices.

That remedy carries its own risks. Higher rates increase mortgage repayments and the cost of business finance, potentially discouraging investment and consumption. Governments face higher borrowing costs, while already struggling households have less room to absorb further increases. The Bank of England's decision to slow its government-bond sales also illustrates the difficulty of managing inflation alongside financial-market pressures.

The implications extend beyond advanced economies. Countries dependent on imported energy face additional pressure on currencies, public finances and household purchasing power. India, where retail inflation has recently increased, must weigh these risks as the Reserve Bank of India assesses whether price pressures are temporary or becoming more persistent.

Yet the choices facing central banks are not identical. A rate increase may be justified where inflation is broadening, but could be counterproductive if higher prices are confined to a temporary supply shock. The crucial distinction is whether rising energy costs are beginning to influence the wider economy.

The deeper lesson is that inflation cannot be managed through interest rates alone. Energy security, resilient supply chains and credible fiscal policies matter too. Without them, monetary authorities may repeatedly be forced to suppress demand in response to disruptions they cannot control. The world's inflation problem is therefore also a growth problem ~ and an institutional one.

Central banks must defend price stability without inflicting avoidable economic damage.
