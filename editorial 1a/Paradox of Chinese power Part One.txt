Title: Paradox of Chinese power Part One

Paradox of Chinese power

For more than a decade, foreign policy circles in Washington and West European capitals have wrestled with a singular, defining question of 21st century statecraft. Is China an unstoppable economic colossus destined to remake the international order, or is it a fragile state burdened by mounting structural contradictions?

The prevailing narrative often attempts to split the difference, casting Beijing as "ge op olitically strong but economically weak." Yet this common dichotomy simplifies a far more complex, interdependent reality. China's international leverage and its domestic vulnerabilities are deeply intertwined. Beijing's global power is not merely exerted despite its domestic financial troubles; in many respects, it is driven by the very mechanisms that create those internal strains.

To understand the true picture of contemporary China, one must comprehend two competing dynamics: the formidable reach of its state-directed industrial engine and the severe balance-sheet decay eroding its domestic foundation. Describing China's $18 trillion economy as entirely "weak" misses the extraordinary power of its manufacturing and export apparatus. China is an unrivaled industrial powerhouse.

As documented by research from the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), China commands over 80 per cent of the world's solar manufacturing capacity, leads global production in electric vehicles (EVs) and lithium-ion batteries, and holds a near-monopoly on critical rare earth processing. In high-tech green energy and advanced manufacturing, what Beijing terms "new quality productive forces", China's competitive edge is formidable and growing. However, running parallel to this high-octane export machine is a domestic economy in deep distress.

The structural collapse of China's property market, a sector that historically accounted for nearly 30 per cent of gross domestic product, has wiped out trillions in household wealth. Youth unemployment, persistent consumer deflation, and rapid demographic decline have collectively crushed domestic consumer confidence. Beijing's reluctance to provide direct stimulus to households, choosing instead to channel state capital into state-owned enterprises and advance d manufacturing, has left domestic demand permanently suppressed.

This structural divergence lies at the heart of Logan Wright's incisive analysis in Foreign Affairs, "China's Moment of Weakness: Economic Decay and America's Strategic Opening." Wright, an economist and director of China market research at the Rhodium Group, argues that Western observers routinely miscalculate Chinese power by evaluating state policy objectives rather than underlying balance sheets. According to Wright, China's economic malaise is not a temporary, cyclical slump, but a systemic debt crisis decades in the making. For years, local government financing vehicles poured trillions in borrowed state credit into unproductive infrastructure and overbuilt real estate.

Today, local authorities lack the revenue to service these debts, forcing state banks to perpetually roll over non-performing loans to avert widespread defaults. This practice locks capital into "zombie" assets, starving private entrepreneurs and service sectors of vital funding. Wright argues that this internal financial decay directly constrains Beijing's outward geopolitical leverage.

As domestic capital efficiency degrades and local government balance sheets deteriorate, Beijing's capacity to deploy vast state funds abroad, such as new tranches of infrastructure financing under the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) or emergency bailouts for indebted nations, is severely curtailed. Yet, where Wright sees domestic fragility, a paradox emerges: China's refusal to empower its domestic consumers forces it to export its industrial overcapacity to global markets. Because Chinese households cannot absorb the massive output of state-subsidized factories, Chinese solar panels, EVs, legacy semiconductors, and industrial goods flood international markets at prices undercut by state support.
