Title: How ready are we for an earthquake Part One

How ready are we for an earthquake?

The Earth appears not to be in a great mood lately. The past six months have offered a harsh reminder of how unpredictable the planet can be. The Earth appears not to be in a great mood lately. The past six months have offered a harsh reminder of how unpredictable the planet can be. Since April, a deadly chain of earthquakes has struck worldwide.

It started with 12 people dying in Afghanistan in April, followed by a quake in the Philippines in June that killed at least 81 people. Then a massive double-quake struck Venezuela, leaving more than 6,000 dead. In July, Japan lost 39 lives. In August, earthquakes killed about 290 in Colombia and at least 51 in Indonesia, with the tolls still rising.

India is not immune to this threat. The 2001 Bhuj earthquake in Kutch killed over 13,800 people, and the 2005 Kashmir earthquake claimed over 1,350 lives. Large swathes of the country, particularly the Himalayan region, the North-East, and Kutch, remain at very high risk for major seismic events. During earthquakes, weak buildings can become death traps.

The government must immediately order strict, mandatory safety audits for all existing public buildings, hospitals, schools, and crowded apartment blocks, while ensuring that new construction complies with crucial seismic codes like IS 1893. These results must be shared transparently, and vulnerable structures must be retrofitted. Japan's 1981 New Seismic Design Code introduced stricter earthquake safety standards.

Buildings had to withstand moderate earthquakes with limited damage and avoid collapse during stronger ones. The 1995 Kobe earthquake demonstrated the difference: 97 per cent of collapsed buildings had been built before 1981. Japan has also retrofitted old buildings by strengthening foundations and walls or installing seismic damping systems.

Turkey has run large-scale retrofitting programmes in Istanbul, including schools, hospitals and other public buildings. Under its Istanbul Seismic Risk Mitigation and Emergency Preparedness Project, more than 1,200 public buildings have been retrofitted or reconstructed. For India, the lesson is simple: new buildings must meet strict seismic standards, while older buildings must be audited and retrofitted.

Safe infrastructure is only half the battle. True safety depends on ready, aware citizens, as first responders are usually ordinary people nearby. Regular drills should make the following steps as familiar as a fire drill: Drop: Get onto hands and knees to avoid being thrown down and crawl if necessary.

Cover: Protect the head and neck; get under a sturdy table or desk, or move to an interior wall away from windows, glass and falling objects. Hold on: Stay until shaking stops, holding furniture if it shifts while continuing to shield the head and neck. These rules adapt to different environments: in bed, stay there and cover with a pillow; outdoors, move to open ground away from buildings, trees and power lines; while driving, pull over away from bridges, overpasses and buildings and remain inside.

Expect aftershocks and repeat the drill. Irrespective of the prospect of an earthquake strike, Indian households should cultivate the habit of firmly fixing heavy cupboards, shelves and appliances to walls so they do not topple during shaking. Heavy objects should be kept on lower shelves, and deep cracks should be repaired promptly.

Families should also maintain an emergency kit and a clear evacuation plan. During shaking, stay calm: indoors, stay indoors; outdoors, stay outdoors. Do not run or use lifts while the ground is shaking. Avoid windows, mirrors and falling objects. Move away from buildings, walls, poles and wires. In vehicles, stop safely and remain inside.
