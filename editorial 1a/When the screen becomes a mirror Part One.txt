Title: When the screen becomes a mirror Part One

When the screen becomes a mirror

On any given night in Delhi, Bengaluru or Kochi, a few million people are watching a Korean serial with the subtitles on. More than five crore Indians now follow Korean content, a number growing at around 35 per cent a year, and one recent Korean season was watched by close to two crore people in this country alone, a figure no other international show has matched here.

Korean clothes, Korean music, Korean ideas about work and love and family have made the journey from Seoul to our bedrooms with remarkably little friction. One number did not make the journey. South Korea's total fertility rate, the average number of children a woman has in her lifetime, is now below 0.8, the lowest recorded anywhere.

In Seoul, where much of the country lives, it is closer to 0.63. Put plainly, ten women are producing fewer than eight children between them. Statistics Korea expects the population to fall from about 5.2 crore today to 3.6 crore by 2072. The society whose fantasies we have adopted is struggling to reproduce itself.

The obvious question is whether India is walking the same road. The answer, from our own statistics, is that we have already stepped onto it. The Sample Registration System report for 2024, released this May, puts India's TFR at 1.9, below the replacement level of 2.1, and further below the 2.15 that India actually requires, given how skewed our sex ratio at birth remains.

Rural India stands at 2.1 and urban India at 1.5. Delhi is at 1.2, lower than Finland. Kerala is at 1.3. Bihar is at 2.9. We are not one country, demographically speaking, but the national average has crossed the line and there was no announcement. Two explanations are usually offered for this, and both contain something true.

The first is that globalisation changed the shape of a life. In 1961, the average Indian woman married at about 16.8 years. By 2010 that had risen to 22.2. Analysis of three decades of survey data points to education, urbanisation and mass media as the drivers, with each additional year of a girl's schooling delaying marriage by roughly 0.36 years.

Marriage and childbearing moved from being a schedule imposed on women to being a decision they make. The second is that women are choosing differently. Female labour force participation has risen from around 24.5 per cent in 2018 to 41.7 per cent in 2024. Studies of delayed motherhood among urban, educated women find the reasons to be career and education, not biology.

Women are finishing degrees, establishing themselves, and only then turning to the question of children. Both explanations flatter us, which may be why we stop there. The third one does not. Korean research found that mothers of small children who hold university degrees were spending about 230 minutes a day on childcare.

Their husbands were spending 36. Korean women are among the most educated in the world, and in several fields, they outnumber men in higher education. They entered the workforce in large numbers. What they did not get was a corresponding exit from the household. The new work was added to the old work rather than substituting for it, and the arithmetic of a day that holds only 24 hours did the rest.
