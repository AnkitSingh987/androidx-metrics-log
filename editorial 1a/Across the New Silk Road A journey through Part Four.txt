Across the New Silk Road: A journey through Uzbekistan

Their prominence in Uzbek markets reminds one of Babur's affection for the melons, recorded in the Baburnama. Uzbekistan lies at the heart of Central Asia, owing to its strategic location, large population and extraordinary historical legacy. With a population of around 38 million and per capita income approaching $4,000, it is the region's most populous country and one of its most consequential economies.

While Kazakhstan has emerged as an upper-middle-income country with $ 14,000 per capita income, Uzbekistan's demographic weight, its population is nearly twice that of Kazakhstan, gives it considerable geopolitical and economic significance in Central Asia. My own visit coincided with an international chess competition in Samarkand. The UAE had sent a strong delegation to observe the organisation and preparations for the event, as it is scheduled to host the next championship in 2028.

It was another reminder of the increasingly international character of the city as it not only as a historical crossroads of the Silk Road, but also as a contemporary venue for diplomacy, sport and international engagement. That international engagement is increasingly visible in Uzbekistan's economic and strategic relationships as well. China has become the largest individual source of foreign investment and loans in Uzbekistan.

In 2024, Chinese investment accounted for 28.7 per cent of the country's $27.7 billion in foreign investments and loans, followed by Russia at 12.7% and Turkey at 7%. Germany accounted for 5.1 per cent, Saudi Arabia 4.8% and the United Kingdom 3.8%. The U.S. Secretary of State Rubio is also expected to travel to Samarkand for a meeting with the foreign ministers of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan, accompanied by representatives of more than 100 U.S.

companies. In 2025, Indian investment in Uzbekistan reportedly rose 135% to $332 million, taking cumulative Indian capital to more than $717 million, with 375 enterprises involving Indian capital operating in the country by early 2026. For Uzbekistan, this diversification creates opportunities but also requires careful balancing.

Rather than relying on a single external power, Tashkent appears to be cultivating multiple economic and diplomatic relationships. My ten-day journey ended in Tashkent where it had begun. There is a great deal of thought that has gone into the way Uzbekistan presents its history. Ancient Khorezm, the Timurid era, the Islamic scholarly tradition and the Silk Road are presented as parts of a much longer human story.

It is using its history to understand itself while trying to shape its future. Uzbekistan appears to be quietly seizing the opportunities created by its geography and by the competition for influence in Central Asia, cultivating relationships with different powers without making any one relationship the centrepiece of its foreign policy. In that sense, Uzbekistan itself becomes a metaphor for a certain kind of quiet diplomacy which is pragmatic, understated and conscious of its room for manoeuvre.

Travelling through the country left me thinking about how closely the present remains connected to the past. The journey also brought home a simple historical continuity: geography may endure, but the meaning of a crossroads is constantly being rewritten. By Luv Puri Luv Puri is a Fulbright scholar at New York University.
