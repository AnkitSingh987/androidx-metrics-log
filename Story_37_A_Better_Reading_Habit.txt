Title: A Better Reading Habit

Category: Intermediate

Maria sat at her small desk, staring at a thick biology textbook. It was almost midnight, and the small lamp cast a yellow light over the complicated pages. She rubbed her tired eyes and sighed heavily. Maria was a twenty-year-old exchange student from Colombia, studying at a university in Toronto, Canada. She was a bright and hardworking student, but lately, she felt completely exhausted. 

Her biggest problem was reading in English. To get into the university program, she had studied English grammar for years and passed all her proficiency exams. However, real academic reading was a completely different nightmare. Every time she had to read a chapter for homework, she approached it like a difficult puzzle. She read every single sentence slowly and carefully. If she saw a word she didn't know, she immediately stopped reading, picked up her heavy bilingual dictionary, and searched for the exact translation. 

Because of this slow process, it often took her over an hour to read just two or three pages. By the time she finished a chapter, she couldn't even remember the main idea because she had focused so much on individual words. Reading had become a painful chore, and she was starting to believe that she simply hated reading in English.

One Friday evening, Maria was sitting on the living room sofa, looking miserable while surrounded by textbooks. Her roommate, Emma, walked in holding a cup of hot chocolate and a small paperback book. Emma was a local Canadian student who always seemed to be reading something for fun.

"You look like you are going to cry, Maria," Emma said, sitting down next to her. "Are you studying biology again?"

"Yes," Maria groaned, closing her textbook. "I have to read forty pages for Monday. It is impossible. I have been reading for two hours, and I am only on page six. My brain hurts, and I hate English."

Emma looked at the heavy textbook and shook her head. "No wonder you hate reading. You only read academic textbooks. Textbooks are boring even for native speakers! When was the last time you read something just for pleasure?"

Maria frowned, confused by the question. "Pleasure? I don't have time for pleasure reading. I have to study. Besides, reading in English is too difficult for me. It is not fun."

"It is difficult because you are treating every text like an exam," Emma explained gently. "You are trying to understand one hundred percent of the words. You need to build a better reading habit. You need to learn how to read for the story, not for the vocabulary."

Emma stood up and went to her bedroom. A minute later, she returned with a thin book with a dark blue cover. It was a famous mystery novel. She handed it to Maria.

"Try this," Emma suggested. "It is a thriller. The language is modern and very natural. The sentences are not too complicated. I want you to try reading the first chapter tonight before you go to sleep."

Maria looked at the book suspiciously. "But what if I don't know all the words? Should I use my dictionary?"

"No!" Emma said strictly, pointing a finger at her. "That is the new rule. You cannot use your dictionary while you read this book. Put it in another room if you have to. If you don't know a word, just guess what it means from the context of the sentence and keep going. Only stop if you completely lose the plot of the story."

Maria was not convinced, but she was too tired to argue. She closed her biology textbook and took the novel to her bedroom. She put her dictionary inside her closet, far away from her bed. She got under her warm blankets and opened the mystery novel.

The first page introduced a detective who was walking through a dark, rainy city. In the second paragraph, Maria found a word she didn't know: 'puddle'. Her immediate reaction was to jump out of bed and check her dictionary. But she remembered Emma’s rule. She forced herself to read the rest of the sentence: *He stepped in a deep puddle and his shoes got completely wet.* 

Maria thought for a second. If it was raining, and he stepped in it, and it made his shoes wet... it had to be a small pool of water on the ground. She had guessed the meaning without translating it! A small spark of excitement lit up inside her.

She continued reading. She found other unknown words, but instead of stopping, she focused on the action. The story was exciting. There was a stolen diamond, a mysterious letter, and a chase through a crowded train station. Because she wasn't stopping every minute to look up words, the story flowed like a movie in her mind. 

Before she knew it, she had read the entire first chapter. Then, she read the second chapter. When she finally looked at her clock, it was 2:00 AM. She had read twenty pages of an English book without stopping, and she had actually enjoyed it. 

The next morning, Emma was eating cereal in the kitchen when Maria walked in. Maria looked tired, but she had a huge smile on her face. She placed the mystery novel on the kitchen table.

"I read five chapters," Maria announced proudly. 

Emma's eyes widened in surprise. "Five chapters? Wow! Did you use your dictionary?"

"Not even once," Maria laughed. "It felt very strange at first. I felt guilty for not knowing every single word. But you were right. I didn't need to know every word to understand the mystery. The story was so good that I just wanted to find out what happened next."

Over the next few weeks, Maria's routine completely changed. She started spending thirty minutes every night reading a novel before going to sleep. She finished the mystery book and immediately borrowed a romance novel from Emma, and then a science fiction story. 

She noticed something magical happening. Because she was reading faster and seeing more words in context, her vocabulary started improving naturally. She started recognizing words she had seen in her novels when she read her biology textbook. She didn't have to translate them because her brain already understood their feeling and meaning. 

Her academic reading became much easier. It didn't take her an hour to read two pages anymore. She learned to read sentences as whole ideas instead of separate words. 

One afternoon, Maria was sitting in the university library. She was reading a complex article about human cells. She realized she was reading smoothly, sliding down the page without any anxiety. She smiled and closed the article when she finished. She reached into her bag, pulled out her new favorite fantasy novel, and began to read just for the joy of it. She finally understood that the secret to becoming a better reader wasn't working harder; it was finding the right story.
